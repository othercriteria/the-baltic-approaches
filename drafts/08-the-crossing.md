# 8 — The Crossing

On the seventh day Hvidt went forward, which was a small
scandal.

Corps commanders visited divisions; that was the liturgy. They
did not stand for an hour and forty minutes on an embankment
above a bridge in fine drizzle, watching traffic. The escort
made its unhappiness known through channels, the channels
being Witzel, who noted the unhappiness, logged the departure
time, and said nothing else, and Holt rode in the second car
and spent the drive rereading the movement returns he already
knew, because he had understood — the summons had been four
words, "Come and see something" — that he was being taken to
be shown a fact he had been reporting for a week without
seeing it.

The fact, from the embankment at Friedrichstadt, looked like
this. Southbound: the tail of the Danish division — the
second battalion of a brigade, self-propelled guns under
netting, bridging sections at walking pace, fuel trucks —
each vehicle paying its toll of eleven to thirteen seconds of
bridge and running north into the gray. Northbound, on the
verge, waiting: a recovered tank on a transporter, two
ambulances, empty civil buses going back for tomorrow's
columns. And between the serials, on the bridge itself,
nothing. Gaps. Eleven seconds of plank, then four hundred
meters of wet air, then eleven more seconds of plank.

"Tell me what I am looking at," Hvidt said.

"A route element operating at capacity, Herr General." It was
the annex's sentence, and it died in the drizzle as he said
it. The lieutenant down at the control point — the signal
lamp, the clipboard, the log that was already famous in one
headquarters — called a serial forward; the serial came up
the approach at the interval its march table prescribed,
crossed, and was gone, and the bridge stood empty again,
holding its four hundred meters of air.

"Look at the gaps and try again."

Holt looked at the gaps. "A valve," he said, finally. "It is
not a route element. It is the valve on everything north.
The ninety-hour clock, the resupply, the evacuation — every
promise the corps has made reduces to seconds of plank on
three crossings, and we are — " he heard it arrive, late,
with the same heat as the Esbjerg question — "we are running
the valve on the spacing tables. The gaps are the war, Herr
General. We are looking at the corps' whole future going
across in the gaps."

"Now tell me who serves the crossing," Hvidt said. "The
lieutenant spaces serials to a staff-college interval,
because that is his order. The colonel back at Husum feeds
the approach march on a readiness schedule, because that is
his. The engineer improves the road behind us, because that
is his. Every man in the chain performs his own stage to his
own green board, correctly, and the bridge stands empty
eleven seconds in thirteen. If the crossing is the corps'
clock — who serves the crossing?"

"Nobody, Herr General."

"Nobody. That is the obscenity of it, and it took me a week
to see, and I commanded a brigade over this river in an
exercise in 1974." He turned up his collar. "Come. We are
going to go and be unreasonable."

---

The three orders were drafted that afternoon and evening at
Rendsburg, between 1400 and 2200, and they were argued, which
was Holt's doing. He had watched the guidance-line decision
arrive from three capitals as sealed weather, and the
rationing of Yellow-3 arrive from his own pen the same way,
and he had begun keeping a private discipline: any order that
would spend other men's hours got built in a room with the
men who owned the objections.

The first order subordinated the chain to the link. The
crossings at Friedrichstadt, the rail bridge at Sorgbrück,
and the Nordfeld ferry became the corps' clock; a single
movement authority at the Eider, answering to the G-3, would
call serials forward at the rate the crossings could pass
them, and every readiness schedule, march table, and staging
plan behind them was subordinated to that call. The Danish
movement colonel who would hold the authority listened to the
draft read aloud and asked for one amendment: his own
translation, for the men on the bridges. "If the bridge is
drinking, the bottle pours. If the bridge is full, the bottle
waits. No pouring because the bottle is proud of being full."
It went into the order's covering instruction verbatim,
because it was better than the order.

The second order attacked the empty seconds, and the second
order drew blood. Staging moved forward, under camouflage
the staff college would have wept over; the call-forward
interval came down from minutes to tens of seconds; and the
safety spacing on the bridges themselves was cut by half,
which brought the engineer captain who owned the crossing
complex to his feet.

"On the record, Herr Oberstløjtnant. At half spacing, one
shell on a bunched column takes the bridge and the column.
The interval exists so that a hit costs us a vehicle. You
are proposing that a hit may cost us the crossing."

"Correct," Holt said. "Now the other side of it. At present
spacing the bridge passes — Roloff?"

"Two hundred and ninety vehicles per working hour. At half
spacing, estimated four hundred ten. The difference across
one day is the closure of the division's tail a full day
early."

"A day of the division against the risk of the crossing.
That is the trade. I am not going to pretend it away,
Captain; I am going to sign it. Your objection goes in the
order — written, named, yours. If the shell comes, the
corps will know exactly what it bought with your interval
and what it paid, and no one will pretend the risk arrived
by weather."

The captain looked at him for a moment. "At half spacing,"
he said, "I want the air-defense umbrella thickened over
the complex and the dummy bridging at Süderstapel made
convincing. If we are to be a target worth a regiment's
shells, let him spend them four kilometers south."

"That," Holt said, "is the third order," and gave the
captain the drafting of half of it.

The third order created nothing. It stopped things. Engineer
effort came off two route improvements with their own green
boards and went entire onto the crossing complex — approach
repair, redundant ferry sites, the umbrella, the decoy. The
verb that governed the order was *nachgeordnet* —
subordinated — and Witzel, countersigning the battle-rhythm
changes it forced, read the draft twice and made the single
comment the staff would quote for weeks: "You are aware,
Herr Oberstløjtnant, that you have just ordered two
lieutenant-colonels to fail their own inspections in order
that a bridge may stand idle less. State it plainly at the
morning conference, in those words. If it is worth doing it
is worth saying without cosmetics, and if it cannot survive
being said plainly it is not worth doing."

It survived being said plainly.

The number responded on the fifteenth. Passage through the
complex rose forty percent day over day; the staging queues
at Schwabstedt halved; the Danish division's closing state,
which had crawled for six days, moved — for the first time
since the clock started, faster than the revised projection.
Roloff plotted scheduled against actual on the wall in two
inks, and for one day the actual crossed to the good side of
the scheduled, and half the night shift found an excuse to
walk past it.

---

Hvidt let the staff have the curve for exactly one evening
conference before he took it away from them.

"Enjoyed?" he asked, when the movement brief ended. "Good.
Now. What does the corps run out of next, and when?"

Silence, of the productive kind. Then Lammers opened the
folder he had, of course, brought.

"Artillery ammunition, Herr General. The corps' batteries
have fired, in seven days, one hundred and eighty percent of
the planning rate the stocks were sited against. The forward
sites are drawing down; the theater reserve is a promise
with a sea voyage in it. At current expenditure the curves
cross in nine days. I can move the date by convoy — and the
convoys use the same roads and the same crossings as
everything else this staff has just learned to worship."

"So the constraint has moved," Hvidt said. "A week ago it
was seconds of plank. We served the crossing, and the
crossing repaid us, and now it is rounds. And when we have
served the rounds, it will move again — to fuel, or to
replacement barrels, or to sleep. Say it, somebody, so the
room owns it."

"Constraints are not defeated," Aakjær said from the
intelligence desk. "They move. You relieve one and the next
steps forward to be recognized." He came to the wall, to
Roloff's two-ink curve, and put his finger not on the good
day but on the space to the right of it. "And a question,
Herr General, since the room is owning things tonight.
Somewhere across the water a thin man with my job is
watching his own curves cross. His bridging is his plank
seconds; his fuel is his rounds. If our curves have a date
nine days out, his have a date too — and I observe that
this staff, which has learned in one week to see its own
war as arithmetic, still reports his war as weather. It is
not weather. It is a problem set, worked by tired men, on
worse roads than ours."

"Then get me his arithmetic," Hvidt said, and stood, and
the conference was over, and Lammers' nine-day date went up
on the wall in the space Aakjær's finger had indicated,
where it began, quietly, to govern everything.
