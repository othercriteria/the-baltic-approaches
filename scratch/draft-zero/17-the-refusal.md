# 17. The Refusal

The last reconciliation at the Coastal Front's command post was held on the night of the twenty-fifth of November, and this time nobody read anybody's annex aloud, because the numbers had stopped requiring presentation. They stood on the map like weather.

The Front's dumps, three-fifths filled against the plan for the great effort, filling more slowly each night as the air war ground the coastal rail approaches; the repair directorate's estimates lengthening in the passive voice that staffs adopt when the news has become structural. The lift — what remained of the lift — dispersed into fishing harbors, its embarkation formations marching inland to fight as infantry, the word *desant* quietly retired from the planning vocabulary a week since. And on the meteorological annex, circled by some staff officer whose pencil had pressed hard enough to tear the paper: the sea's own reconciliation, the first winter gales assessed for the second week of December, the window through which the whole operation had been aimed in June now visibly, measurably closing, like a door with arithmetic for hinges.

Rylski briefed the sum in four sentences, without his columns for once, an auditor at the end of audit. "The Front can mount one full-scale effort, on the twenty-seventh, with what stands in the dumps that morning. It cannot mount a second. If the first succeeds, we winter on the canal and the Kattegat, and the operation's purpose is achieved late. If it does not succeed, the operation has no third week, Comrade General — not because of the enemy. Because of December."

Into the pause that followed, from the visitors' corner, General-mayor Kuragin spoke for the first time in eleven days.

He spoke pleasantly, as always, and he had brought a folder, as always, and what was in the folder, laid open on the map table with the deference of a man presenting an option rather than an order, was the other timetable — the annex with the different classification, refreshed to the current situation by staffs far from the beech wood: preparation states, delivery means, a target framework whose paragraphs wore the neutral typography of all such documents, and a covering sheet requiring, to activate any of it, one thing only. A request. Initiated at Front level, endorsed by the theater, decided far above — but initiated here, over the signature of the Front commander, on the professional judgment that the conventional means had culminated.

"No one instructs you, Tadeusz Konstantinovich," Kuragin said. "I am asked merely to establish that the Front commander has considered every means available to fulfill his mission. The twenty-seventh, as the chief of staff observes, admits no second attempt. The means in this folder converts one attempt into certainty. It would be" — he chose the word with care, and it was the annex's own word — "irresponsible not to consider it."

Zawadzki looked at the folder for a long moment. Then he did the thing that Vossen — who would reconstruct this scene years later from fragments, from the interrogation protocols and the one memoir and the gaps where a name had been removed — the thing Vossen would come to regard as the finest staff work of the war on either side: he considered it. Aloud. In the language of the trade, with his chief of staff's own instruments, giving the folder the full professional respect that was the only armor against it.

"Very well. As an operational question. Stefan — the framework targets his defense at the neck, his artillery groupings, his reserves in the woods west of the canal. Grant every strike its planned effect. What does the Front hold on the twenty-eighth that it does not hold otherwise?" And Rylski, who had audited everything else in the war, audited this: "Rubble at the neck, Comrade General. His forward battalions destroyed — and his obstacle belt improved by our own cratering. His reserves attrited — and every road through the strike zones degraded for our own movement by a factor the engineer annex declines to estimate. The prize" — he did not need to gesture at the map; the whole operation had always been the map's edge, the ports, the locks, the machinery wanted whole — "the prize is downwind of the means, in the most literal sense available. And the dumps, our own arithmetic: the effort still requires them; the means in the folder feeds no division and moves no railhead. It converts his combat power to zero for some hours. It converts our logistics to nothing at all. We would arrive at the Kattegat, if we arrived, with an army that could not be supplied, through a country that could not be administered, to hand the ministries a port" — the auditor's voice, absolutely level — "that no longer existed in the sense the ministries intend the word."

"And the other column," Zawadzki said. "The one the folder does not print." He closed it, gently, and slid it back across the map the way a man declines a contract, and for once the hand did not lie flat on anything. "The operation exists, Vitaly Sergeyevich — it was approved, in rooms we both know, on one argument: that it would be over before it became the other kind of war. That argument is the operation. The folder does not complete the argument; it confesses its failure — with fire, in the one theater where every government on the far side has spent twenty years rehearsing what our fire means. I am six days late against a closing sea. That is a professional emergency. The folder proposes to solve it by making it a different century." He stood. "The Front attacks on the twenty-seventh with the means the plan was approved on. Record in the journal that the commander considered the full range of options" — the formula, delivered like a coin paid — "and found the conventional means adequate to the mission. We will make it true."

Kuragin gathered his folder without haste, pleasantly as ever. "A complete answer," he said. "I will report that the Front commander's confidence is undiminished." And only at the doorway of the planning cell, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather to no one: "Confidence is a currency too, Tadeusz Konstantinovich. Yours is spent on the twenty-seventh, one way or the other. I hope the dumps are as full as the journal will say they were."

Vossen made his last call to Eckernförde on the night of the twenty-sixth, from the small office, with the corps around him wound to its final pitch — the assembly areas full, the routes swept, Vestergaard's orders issued down to the report lines with the bird names, the whole banked intention lying silent in the woods west of the canal waiting for an enemy who was now, the wall said, thirty-some hours from his one morning.

"I know why you are calling," Rahn said, before Vossen had spoken three words. "You are calling to be blessed. No."

The line hummed. Outside, very far off, the harassing guns.

"You have a headquarters that keeps two calendars and prices its own grief," the old man went on, "a commander who signs what he believes and a wall that remembers what he signed, and tomorrow or the day after you will do the thing the whole apparatus was built to see. What could I add? If I approve, you will lean on the approval, and the first time the morning goes sideways — mornings go sideways, Vossen; mine did — you will reach for me in your head instead of for your own instruments, and the reach will cost you the quarter hour that decides such days. The best thing I taught you was questions. The last question is one you must be asked by nobody: I had it asked me once, by a man in a bunker with a folder, and the whole difference of my life is that I answered it late." A silence with weight in it. "Yours is not my war. Your folder stays shut; I have read that much between the lines of three weeks, and it is the thing I am proudest of, of everything, and none of it is mine to be proud of. So. No blessing. One instruction, as your teacher, the last one: when it is over — over, Vossen, not paused — you will come to Eckernförde, and you will bring the wall. Photographs of it. Every sheet. And we will sit with a bottle of something Danish and read your war the way it was actually written, in the estimates and the two times, and I will tell you then what I think of my student. Not before. Am I understood?"

"Understood, Herr General."

"Good. Get off my telephone. It is past an old man's bedtime, and past a corps'." The receiver went down in Eckernförde, firmly, and the last mentor contact of the war was over — no aphorism, no benediction, the door of that room too held shut on purpose by the man who owned it — and Vossen sat for a moment in the small office, alone with the guns and the ticking pipes, and found that the silence on the line had delivered the only message he had actually needed:

There was no one left to ask. The staff was the answer now. He switched off the lamp and went back to the operations room, where the night shift stood their quiet watch around the plywood, keeping the two calendars of the last morning of the education, and nobody looked up as he came in, because everybody was working, and that — he stood a moment in the doorway, seeing it — that was the blessing.
