# 19 — The Door

The guns opened at 0455, five minutes early by design,
and the north wall of the operations room shivered in its
taped glass.

The corps' artillery fired the program Holt had signed:
every banked shell with a task and a time, three weeks of
refusals converting back into violence in forty minutes
of scheduled fury — the counter-preparation on the gun
lines, the smoke on the Schlei arm, the belts of
interdiction on the two roads out of the Sehestedt
bridgehead. For two hours there was nothing left for the
staff to decide. The machine the wall had built ran, and
the staff listened to it run, and Lammers stood at the
expenditure board with his hands behind his back and
delivered the campaign's shortest situation report:
"Punctual."

The air came in phases as the light came up, the market's
fish sequenced days deep: the first wave onto the gun
groupings caught displacing forward, the second onto the
Schlei bridging — dropped behind the assault echelon like
a latch — the third held on call. And the crossing
discipline of the Eider ran in reverse: bridging down at
the sites the engineers had prepared as exits since the
fifteenth day, the brigades passing through and deploying
off the march, the valve's whole grammar — staging,
call-forward, the bottle and the bridge — conjugated now
in the attacking direction.

What the crest meant, from the inside, the intercept
summaries assembled through the morning. The enemy's
reserves were spent into the night's last assault; when
the shoulders caved there was nothing in hand. His fuel
and shells stood forward, his everything-else two hundred
kilometers back, his roads cratered by his own
preparation and latched by the second wave, and his
orders — the intercept troop copied them going out —
arrived at formations that were no longer where the
orders assumed, in the specific helplessness of a
headquarters whose reporting had been generous to itself
for three weeks and was now being audited, all at once,
by events. His battalions fought hard, as pieces. The
corps fought as an argument.

But the argument paid its own bill first, and the bill
arrived on the western shoulder, at 0800, where the
model said the enemy's guns should have culminated and
the enemy's guns had not.

The dumps had been thinner than his books said.
Three-fifths, the auditable figure, had been generous
by the margin of its own optimistic returns, and the
crest had come and begun to pass in the last hours of
the night — earlier than the computation, exactly as far
inside the error band as Roloff's pinned sentence had
warned it could be — and the westward battalions of
Vestergaard's assault, thinned once already by a raid
that had bought Tuesday's headlines, went in against an
economy already turned to the defense: registered stop
lines, guns gone frugal, a man past his peak husbanding
what remained precisely because there was less of it
than anyone's ledger showed. The western pincer took
its first objective ninety minutes late and its second
at noon, and the door that was to have closed by
midmorning closed at 1500 — and every one of those
insuring hours was paid at the narrows, where Loheide's
worn regulars held the enemy's last assault on their
backs at the meter's full documented rate.

The wall did not permit hiding it. The morning's plot
carried the model's crest in pencil and the morning's
actual in ink, the two curves diverging after 0300 like
a road forking, and Roloff posted both without comment,
and Holt stood in front of his own signed computation
while the reports came in and did not look away from
it, because the wall's first citizen could not be seen
to look away, and because looking away had a price now
that he could state to the decimal.

The door closed at 1500. By dusk the lead army was cut —
its assault echelon on the wrong side of a sealed waist
with four days' ammunition, the December sea at its
back — and the corps stood on the restored neck,
coast to coast, the blue trace whole for the first time
since the eighth of November.

---

Hvidt sent Holt forward at 1600, in the flat order of a
man who had thought about it in advance: "Go and see
what a bought hour looks like. The staff should contain
one officer who has."

The retaken crossing at the Danevirke stood in freezing
sleet. The military police were bringing the prisoners
back in company packets — gray-faced men moving with
the economy of the utterly spent, their NCOs still
counting them, because NCOs count — and Holt stood at
the roadside and watched somebody's intake percentages
walk past him in the flesh. A boy of perhaps nineteen
went by carrying a wounded man's rifle as tenderly as
luggage, and looked at Holt without hostility or fear
or anything at all except the wish to put something
down. These were the other staff's numbers. Someone in
a beech wood had audited this boy at three-fifths.

He was not, then or ever again, tempted to love the
instrument.

---

Vestergaard came up on the command net at 2130, five
hours behind the plan's most hopeful line and entirely
aware of what the five hours had cost, which was in his
voice.

"The door is closed and bolted. The tail of the cut army
is streaming back east along the canal — I am watching
the recovery vehicles' lights from this position. The
road to the canal is open, Herr General. My lead brigade
has fuel and light and the crossing sites behind the
bridgehead are one tide from ours. Request release of
phase line SPERBER."

"Request denied."

"Then the second argument, Herr General, and it is not
appetite. The pocket I have sealed is an army that has
not surrendered. If I stand on the phase line, the corps
must fight that army again, at a time of its choosing,
with my men dug in around it like a fence around a
bull. Release SPERBER and I take the crossing sites
tonight and the pocket starves faster and fights
sooner and smaller. The exploitation is not glory. It
is economy."

The operations room heard Karup break in then — the
relay's flat voice, the capitals' evening appetite
arriving in real time, kilometers on ministers' maps,
the word momentum used twice — and heard Hvidt answer
the theater first, in the tone of a man asking a clerk
to wait his turn: "I have a division commander in
contact on the other net. The alliance's governments
will have my answer when he does. Out to you." And then:

"General Vestergaard. Your second argument is good, and
I have weighed it, and here is the weighing. Your
economy buys the pocket cheaper if everything on your
side of the door holds its edge for one more night — and
nothing on your side of the door has slept for two, and
the crest cost you five hours and a battalion's
freshness we do not have on the books yet, and I have
spent this corps to its last arithmetic on a computation
that has already been wrong once today by exactly the
width of its own warnings. Closing the door was the
victory. There is no second victory east of it tonight —
only kilometers, and kilometers are what the other side
counts. Consolidate on the phase line. Dig. What is
running away tonight is December's problem, and
December, General Vestergaard, is on our side. Out."

He stood afterward at the map for a while, at the
restored neck, and then issued the night's housekeeping
in four sentences. "Casualty returns by 0600. Honest
ones. Lammers — expenditure against the program, both
times. And someone bring the wall up to date: the corps
did not win a war tonight, gentlemen. It made an
argument. Beginning tomorrow we find out who accepts
it."

The last signals of the night read like collapse. White
flags at battalion level on the pocket's southern arc;
radio silence spreading across its nets; a deserter
colonel talking freely about empty dumps. Aakjær
drafted the morning assessment before he slept — CUT
ARMY ASSESSED COMBAT-INEFFECTIVE; CAPITULATION BY
FORMATIONS EXPECTED WITHIN 48 HRS — and it was
reasonable, and it was believed, and at 0200, on his
way off shift, Roloff unpinned a sheet from the
twenty-fifth — the intercept annotation from before the
crest, the enemy's bridging moving in radio silence, the
silence that had meant preparation — and re-pinned it,
without being asked and without a word, beside the
morning assessment, where nobody looked at it that
night.
