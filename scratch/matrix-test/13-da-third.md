# 13. The Visitor

The thirteenth and fourteenth days were quiet, and the quiet was worse than the battle, because everyone above the rank of major could read it.

The enemy paused. Not everywhere and not restfully: the salient at the narrows was given up in good order, the first echelon's spent divisions passed rearward through the Second Army's positions, the artillery kept up a professional unpleasantness against the Danewerk line and, for the first time in the war, against Rendsburg itself — harassing fire at the town's approaches, methodical, at hours chosen to cost sleep; the headquarters moved its rest areas underground and its senior officers stopped remarking on it, which was its own barometer. Deep raids came at the crossings again. The radio war thickened. But the front's great engine had visibly dropped to an idle, and Aakjær's board read the idle with the concentration of a doctor at a chest:

"He is reorganizing, resupplying, and deciding," the G-2 briefed on the fourteenth. "The first two are visible — his bridging is moving forward again, his artillery is thickening on the neck, his engineers work the roads behind him every dark hour. The third is not visible, and it is the only one that matters. A man six days behind his own plan, whose margin is spent, does not pause to rest. He pauses because his next act requires permission — his own, or somebody's — and because the act after a pause like this is not another push. It is an answer to the question his calendar has been asking him for a week." Aakjær laid down the pointer. "I can tell the General what he can still do. I can no longer tell the General what he will do, because from here forward his decision is not tactical. And I remind the staff of the season: from this week, every night is longer than the day, the ground bears tracked vehicles by morning frost, and the Baltic has perhaps three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who intends to use the sea. Whatever he intends, the calendar in his headquarters is now shouting."

Into this quiet, on the afternoon of the fourteenth day, at the commander's formal invitation, transported through the eastbound evacuation flow in a staff car whose driver had orders to treat him like glass, came General a.D. Anselm Rahn — in the wet-slate civilian suit, with a briefcase that proved to contain nothing but a folded 1:250,000 of the Baltic Approaches, 1961 printing, annotated in a hand nobody was shown.

It was Hvidt who had summoned him. Holt had disclosed the Eckernförde calls the way he disclosed everything now, expecting mild censure for the open line — and expecting, too, some flicker at the shape of it, a Danish lieutenant-colonel vouching to a Danish general for a retired German one, forty years of unspoken arithmetic folded into the recommendation. Instead the commander had listened, asked two questions — *what does he refuse to give you* and *how quickly does he see* — and sent the invitation over his own signature: the corps requests the counsel of the General, one afternoon, on matters of operational assessment. "The Americans fly him across an ocean to ask him about encirclements," Hvidt said, flatly, signing. "He lives forty minutes from my war and his own army's protocol office sends him invitations to receptions. I decline to be the third institution that wastes him." He blotted the signature. "You studied under him. I am told he was hard on the allied students." *He was hardest on us,* Holt did not say. *It was the only courtesy he had.*

They gave him the confession wall. It had been Holt's instinct to prepare a briefing; Hvidt overruled it — "no theater; give him the instruments and the room" — and so the staff stood in a loose half-circle in the operations cell, the commander among them and pointedly not foremost, while an eighty-two-year-old man in civilian clothes read their war off the plywood for forty minutes in a silence he made no effort to soften.

He read the two-times sheets. He read the movement log's vehicle-hours, and the rationing order with the protests pinned under it, and the air program's projected-against-observed, and he spent the longest time, hands clasped behind him, before the two calendars — OURS and HIS — hung side by side in Roloff's draftsman hand.

"Who audits the enemy's column?" he asked at last.

"I do, Hr. General," said Aakjær.

"Against what do you audit it? Your evidence is his behavior. Behavior confirms a schedule only when it changes. His has now changed — the pause. So." Rahn turned from the wall, and Holt watched him do the thing he remembered from the lecture hall in Hamburg sixteen years before, when Holt had sat in the third row with the other foreign officers and learned that the man would not spare them for being guests: refuse the lectern, walk into the middle of the students, and begin taking their answers apart with their own tools. "You have written, here: six days behind, margin committed, initiative on the table. Fine sentences. I ask the staff of this corps the question its wall almost asks. You have kept his calendar. Where is his *wall*? What are the two times on the far side — the scheduled and the actual — of the thing he cares for most? Not ground. Ground is his reporting, and you have all learned this month what reporting is. What does his whole operation exist to produce, in one word, no *until*?"

Silence. It was, Holt realized, the ENDEX question, asked of the other army — and it was Roloff, of all of them, Roloff of the graph paper, who answered it, hesitantly, in the German the room shared with the old man and Holt had answered in for a year at Hamburg:

"A port, Herr General. Working. Before the winter closes the sea."

"Whose winter?" said Rahn, instantly, softly. "Whose sea?" — and let it hang, and the room did the work, and Holt felt the answer go through the staff like current through a wire, because the words were on the wall already, in Aakjær's own season brief from that morning: *three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who intends to use the sea.* The enemy's careful fires, the unbroken locks, the ports shelled with tongs: the intact things, promised to somebody — promised to *ships*. And ships obeyed a calendar no headquarters could argue with. The enemy's true wall — the scheduled-against-actual that governed every other number in his ledger — was not drawn on land at all. It was a sea-state table. His operation did not have six days of lag against a phase line; it had a fixed number of days of *Baltic* left, and every day the corps had bought at a company an hour had been bought, all along, though the corps had priced it only this afternoon, against that. It was the same water, Holt thought, that ran up past his own family behind the Little Belt — a home sea to freeze against a stranger's arithmetic — and he put the thought down where it lay.

"Understand what you have and have not learned," Rahn said, into the room's changed silence, and now he was not soft at all. "You have not learned his intention; do not flatter yourselves; intentions are for novelists. You have learned his *constraint* — the one line on his wall that, when it is crossed, makes every other line worthless. His mass, his norms, his patience: they are all creditors, and the sea forecloses first. From this follows the only planning question worth this staff's remaining strength, and I will not answer it for you, because it is your war and my train of thought is eighty years old and stops at every station." He began folding his ancient map, a man closing his own briefcase on the proceedings. "The question. A man who must have a working port before the sea closes, and who is late: what does his last week look like — and on which of its days is he *weakest*, not in strength, in *choices*? Find that day on your wall. Plan the corps backward from it. And permit an old soldier one remark he is too old to be disciplined for" — the pale eyes went around the half-circle, and stopped, without theater, on Hvidt — "I have stood in a headquarters that was six days late against a closing season, on the other end of this sea, a lifetime ago. You know the years I mean; I will not pretty them for this room. I can report to the corps commander what is being said this week in the enemy's operations room, verbatim, in any language he likes, because I have said it myself. It is: *there is still time, if.* Gentlemen, there is a species of officer who can make *if* last until the ice. Your work is to take the *if* away from him — visibly, provably, by his own arithmetic — before his superiors take the pen away from him. Because what his superiors write next" — he shut the briefcase; the small click was the only sound in the cell — "is written with a different pen, and this old man has seen that also, and will not stay to dinner."

He stayed, in the event, twenty more minutes — ten of them alone with Hvidt behind a closed door, a conversation neither man ever summarized — and was driven back west into the evacuation flow at dusk, one small gray car against the mattress-roofed current, and the staff went back to the wall and stood before HIS CALENDAR, on which Roloff, without being ordered, had already pinned a fresh sheet with a heading that had not existed at noon.

It said: THE SEA'S SCHEDULE. And under it, in the G-2's hand, the first entry, transcribed from the meteorological annex with a date against it like a knife against a whetstone: BALTIC AMPHIBIOUS WINDOW, ASSESSED CLOSURE: 8–12 DECEMBER. EN REMAINING MARGIN AGAINST OWN MINIMUM PLAN: 17–21 DAYS. LESS TIME REQUIRED TO RESTORE PORT CAPACITY AFTER CAPTURE: NOT LESS THAN 10. EFFECTIVE DECISION SPACE: 7–11 DAYS.

Seven to eleven days. The corps had found the number under the theology at last — and it was not the corps' number, and it never had been. It was his. And for the first time since the chinagraph went up in October, the staff of Headquarters Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland stood in front of its wall and read, in its own handwriting, not how the war might be survived, but how it might be *won*.
