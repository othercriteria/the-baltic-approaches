# 19. The Door

H-hour was 0500 and the guns opened at 0455, and for the first five minutes of the twenty-eighth of November the corps artillery fired the program Vossen had signed — every banked shell with a task, every task with a time, the whole three-week frugality converting back into violence at the one address where the model said violence now compounded — and in the operations room at Rendsburg, where there was nothing left to decide for the next two hours, the staff stood and listened to the north wall of the building shiver, and Lammers said, to nobody, in the voice of a man watching his savings well spent: "Punctual."

The morning belonged to the instruments first and the soldiers after, which was, Vossen understood by now, simply what it looked like when the staff work had been done. The air came in its phases — the market's fish sequenced days deep: the first wave against the enemy's gun groupings, caught displacing forward into their own assault's momentum; the second against the Schlei bridging, dropped behind the committed divisions like a latch; the third held on call. The crossing discipline of the Eider, three weeks old and grown reflexive, ran in reverse at the narrows: Vestergaard's bridging went down under smoke at sites the engineers had prepared as *exits* since the fifteenth day, and the fresh brigades passed through the funnel's shoulder at intervals a Danish movement lieutenant enforced with a signal lamp — the same lamp, possibly the same lieutenant; the war had gotten small enough to know — and deployed off the march into an enemy who was discovering, hour by hour, what the crest of the curve meant from the inside.

It meant: reserves already spent into the morning's assault, and nothing left in hand when the assault's own flank began to cave. It meant fuel forward and shells forward and everything else two hundred kilometers back, with the roads between cratered by his own preparation and latched by the second air wave. It meant orders arriving at formations that had ceased, in the night, to be where the orders assumed — the specific helplessness, Aakjær observed from the intercept summaries, almost clinically, of a headquarters whose reporting had been generous to itself for three weeks and was now being audited, all at once, by events. The enemy at the neck did not collapse; his battalions fought where they stood, hard, all day, with the competence the war had taught everyone to stop underestimating. But he fought as pieces, and the corps fought as an argument — and by 1400 the argument had reached the coast road at Missunde, and by dusk the lead army of the Coastal Front was cut, its assault echelon on the wrong side of a closed door with four days' ammunition and the December sea at its back.

Vossen went forward once, in the afternoon, at Hvidt's order — "the wall will survive two hours without you; go and see what a bought hour looks like; the staff should contain one officer who has" — and stood at the retaken crossing at Dannewerk in the freezing sleet while the prisoners came back the other way. They came in company packets, gray-faced, orderly, their NCOs still counting them, and what Vossen registered, standing at the roadside with the military police, was not triumph — the education had burned that reflex out somewhere around the second rationing order — but recognition. These were the other staff's numbers. The men in the sodden greatcoats were somebody's intake percentages, somebody's readiness state, fed by somebody's punctual columns through three weeks of green boards toward a phase line drawn in June; and the last hundred meters of all that arithmetic was this: a ditch, a doorframe with no house behind it, an NCO counting in the sleet. He watched one boy, nineteen perhaps, carrying a wounded man's rifle as tenderly as luggage, and heard Aakjær's voice from the first week of the war, filed and waiting: *the price list has names on it.* Both ledgers'. All of them. He got back in the car and returned to the plywood, and worked the night through, and was not, he noted with a distant clinical interest, ever again tempted to love the instrument.

The temptation the order had foreseen arrived on schedule instead, at 2130, wearing the voice of victory. Vestergaard on the command net, five hours ahead of the plan's most hopeful line, the door closed and bolted, and in front of his lead brigade — visible, burning, disintegrating — the tail of the cut army streaming east on the coast road. "The road to Eckernförde is open," the division commander said, and every officer in the operations room heard what stood behind it: the exploitation, the pursuit, the twenty kilometers a day at last, ours this time. "Request release of phase line SPERBER."

Hvidt took the handset himself. "Request denied," he said, without preamble and without apology. "You have closed the door, General. Closing it is the victory; there is no second one east of it, only kilometers — and kilometers, we have it on the best authority" — the room's tired laughter, one beat, the first of the war — "kilometers are what the other side counts. Consolidate on the phase line. Dig. What is running away tonight is December's problem, and December, General Vestergaard, is on our side. Out."

He put the handset down and stood a moment at the map, at the neck of the peninsula restored, the blue trace whole from coast to coast for the first time since the eighth of November, and the staff waited for whatever a commander says at such a moment; and what this one said, being this one, was: "Casualty returns by 0600. Honest ones. Lammers — expenditure against the program, both times. And someone bring the wall up to date." Then, already turning toward the signals waiting from three capitals: "The corps did not win a war tonight, gentlemen. It made an argument. Beginning tomorrow, we find out who accepts it."
