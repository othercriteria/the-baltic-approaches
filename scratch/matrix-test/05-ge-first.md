# 5. Green Against What

Belt BRAVO goes in on the night of the ninth, and by morning I understand that the corps is learning two things at once — what its second defensive belt is worth, and what its own reporting is worth — and that these are not two things. They are one thing in two uniforms. But I do not understand that yet at 0100. At 0100 I only understand the demolitions, and the demolitions are going beautifully.

Forty-one prepared targets on the overlay. Road cratering, the three rail overpasses, the Treene bridges, culverts sown down the secondary routes like buttons on a coat. I watch the acknowledgments come in from the operations room and I feel the mood in the room lift with each one, and I cannot fault the lift, because I am doing it too. Forty of forty-one fired. Confirmed. Logged with the two times the commander requires — scheduled against actual — and for once the columns nearly match. The engineers have done in freezing rain under shellfire the thing they rehearsed for years, and done it almost perfectly. Men are entitled to their competence. I let myself have this. Every metric the corps keeps says we have won the night.

By 0400 the enemy is across the Treene in two places.

Not because the demolitions failed. I keep waiting for the report that a charge did not fire, and it does not come, because that is not what happened. The picture assembles itself through the small hours the way night actions always assemble — wrong, then wrong again, then right — and what comes right is this: the enemy did not treat the river as an obstacle to be repaired. He treated it as a scheduled event. His bridging reaches the water within ninety minutes of our last demolition. Corps-level bridging, far forward, in quantities the intelligence annex listed faithfully and none of us imagined *concentrated*. And while our gunners fight the bridge sites, his infantry crosses elsewhere — assault boats, rubber rafts, and at one recorded place by wading. In November. In ranks. Weapons held over their heads. The covering force's forward observers report it in tones I can hear the disbelief in over the net, reporting losses that should stop a crossing, and the crossing does not stop.

Aakjær reads the night's summaries at 0530 with that fixed attention he saves for enemy behavior that confirms something he already suspected.

"He is paying men to save hours," he says. "Note it. Every other army haggles. This one has a price list, and the price list says an hour is worth more than a company. That is not brutality, Hr. Oberstleutnant, whatever the forward battalions think this morning. It is arithmetic we have not seen the rest of."

I note it. I do not yet know what to do with it.

The staff's answer to the crossing is in the plan, and the plan's answer is BELT CHARLIE — the third trade of space for time — and at 0600 the plan's answer runs into the other thing. The thing that has been waiting in our paperwork since the first chinagraph went up. The line on the map that is not a military line.

It has a name in the operations annex: the political guidance line, coordinated Bonn–Copenhagen–Brussels, updated by diplomatic note. It runs the general trace Husum–Schleswig–Eckernförde. The annex language is a masterpiece of the possible — I could recite it, we all could: *forward of this line, ground will not be voluntarily relinquished except under military necessity, the determination of which is reserved.* Reserved to whom, the annex does not say. In BOLD LANCER the umpires ruled the question out of play. Now it is 0600 on the tenth of November, and the covering force is asking permission to conduct its passage of lines behind CHARLIE — behind, that is, the northern shoulder of the guidance line — and the question is ringing telephones in three capitals, and the corps stands still for five hours while it is answered.

Five hours. I spend them in the operations room doing a G-3's job, which this morning means holding two divisions' worth of movement requests in the air, unsigned, in my hands. The military logic is one page. The covering force is spent. His bridging is across. Every hour I delay the passage of lines I will pay for at the rate the night established — a company an hour. I know this the way I know my own name. The political logic is also one page, and Hvidt — who has spent the five hours on the secure line to Karup, saying little, his hand flat on the desk — puts it to us afterward without editorializing, which is its own comment.

"Copenhagen and Bonn require the line held forward of Schleswig until the civil movement of the border Kreise is —" he consults, deliberately, the exact words — "*substantially complete*. I asked what substantially means. I was told it is being determined. Gentlemen: the corps will hold forward of the guidance line until 1800 tomorrow at the earliest. The military necessity clause is not available to us today. That is an order I have accepted, and it is therefore my order." He stands a moment. "Cost it," he says to me, and leaves the room.

*Cost it.* The phrase lands on me like something in a foreign language — and I think, standing there, that I of all people know what it is to work in the language the manuals arrive in, and still this instruction will not translate. We have a whole directorate for costing things. Lammers' empire costs fuel, tubes, track-miles, bridge tonnage. But the thing the commander wants priced is an *order*. Eighteen more hours forward of a line the trade says to leave, expressed in what the hours will buy and what they will burn. And I go looking for the form, the board, the return, and there is none. That is the discovery of my afternoon. The corps can report readiness, strength, expenditure, position — every noun in the inventory — and it cannot, with any instrument on the boards, answer the commander's actual question. Which is a question about *exchange*. What are we spending, what are we getting, and are the two written in any common currency at all.

Roloff builds the first version that evening, on two sheets of graph paper taped together, because there is no form. Down the left, our spendables at the night's actual rates: companies rendered ineffective per twelve hours forward of CHARLIE; tube ammunition per twelve hours at the current fire program; the two river-crossing sets the enemy has not yet forced. Down the right, our buyables — and here the sheet goes thin, because what the eighteen hours buy sits in three different ledgers kept by three different staffs. Kilometers of civil column cleared eastward off route B76: the Territorial Command's number, telephoned, approximate. Danish brigades closed south of the Belt: Lammers' number, exact, and losing its usefulness by the hour as the roads fill. And something Aakjær writes in at the bottom in his own hand, unquantified, flagged with a question mark. *Time purchased against the arrival of the enemy's REAL mass — value unknown, since we have not located it.*

I take the taped sheets to Hvidt at 2200 and I watch him read them for a long time.

"This is the page I asked whether existed," he says finally. "It did not exist. Now it half exists." He taps the ragged right-hand column. "Everything we spend, we can count. Almost nothing we buy has a number, and the numbers it has are owned by three headquarters and a police president. Put it on the wall of the operations room. Not the tidy boards. This. I want every officer who briefs me to have walked past it."

---

I telephone Eckernförde at close to midnight, on the civil exchange, from the small office with the coffee ring on the map of the Baltic Approaches, and I cannot tell you cleanly why I do it. Only that the taped sheets have a shape, and the shape reminds me of a question I failed to answer at a party three weeks ago, and there is nobody in this headquarters I can say that to.

Rahn answers on the sixth ring, unsurprised, as though eighty-two-year-old generals sit by their telephones at midnight in the second week of a war — which, I think, they possibly do.

"Vossen. You are calling me on an open line from a headquarters at war, so you will say nothing of substance, and I will ask nothing. Good. Then we can talk about the only thing worth talking about." A chair creaks in Eckernförde, and I can see the room. "You have come to tell me the answer to my question."

"I have come to tell you," I say, and I feel the sentence assemble itself out of the graph paper as I speak it, "that I know what we are spending. To the decimal. And that I cannot state what we are buying — not to the decimal; at all — because everything we buy is written in somebody else's ledger, in somebody else's unit of account, and half of it in ledgers I cannot see. And that tonight I priced eighteen hours, on the orders of a man I am beginning to respect, and the two columns would not balance because they are not in the same currency."

The line hisses gently. When he speaks there is something new in his voice, and it takes me a moment — it is satisfaction, strictly suppressed.

"Three weeks," he says. "Faster than I was. — No. I will not tell you the answer, Vossen; be serious; if I tell you, you will write it in an annex and it will die there. I will tell you the shape of the answer, and you will do the work, because the work is the answer. Listen." And I listen, the way I have not listened to anyone since his lecture hall. "An enterprise that cannot state what it produces will always — always, in war as in a factory as in a marriage — fall back on measuring what it consumes, because consumption is countable and production requires you to have decided what you are *for*. Your columns will not balance because you are trying to buy an unnamed good with countable expenditure. So: name the good. One good. Not a list — the moment it is a list, you have decided nothing. And when you have named it, ask of that ragged right-hand column of yours a single question. Which line, if it went to zero, would make all the other lines worthless?" A pause; the chair again. "When you can answer that, telephone me, and we will discuss what your headquarters should stop doing. It will be a long list, and it will be the first cheerful conversation of your war. Good night, Herr Oberstleutnant. My regards to the graph paper."

He rings off. I sit a moment in the small office, hearing the operations room through the wall — the teleprinters, the low bilingual traffic of the night shift, a war being administered — and then I take out my notebook and write, under the day's two-times entries, four words that would mean nothing to any board in this building, and I draw a box around them the way Roloff boxes his totals.

*Name the good. One.*

*Renderer's note. Present tense buys the interior of the mistake honestly: the reader believes the demolitions are a triumph exactly as long as Vossen does, because "I let myself have this" cannot know 0400 is coming. Goldratt's grammar — the sentence assembling itself as it is spoken, "I do not understand that yet" — lives natively here, and the Rahn call gains what the brief asked: "I can see the room," "the way I have not listened to anyone since his lecture hall" are warmth first person earns and third would have had to report. What breaks is the original's retrospective irony. "Wrong twice before it was right" survives only as flattened observation; "men are entitled to their competence" loses the past tense's verdict-from-the-future. The close-third could hold Vossen and the corps in one sentence ("the corps learned… in the same twelve hours"); first person must make the corps a thing I watch, costing a half-beat of institutional scale. My read: the spine chapters gain intimacy and lose altitude — a fair trade here, a real risk where a chapter must survey what the protagonist cannot yet feel.*
