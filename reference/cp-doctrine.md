## 1. CP echelonment at corps and division

### 1a. US corps — the three-CP + command-group model

Source: FM 100-15 (1996 proxy), Chapter 4 "Command and Control" and
Appendix B. ESTABLISHED as US doctrine; **proxy** for 1983.

Four command facilities, each a distinct entity:

- **Command Group (CG).** The commander plus a few staff (G3, G2, fire
  support) forward. "It must be highly mobile to allow the commander to
  move to the point of decision." Small, fast, relies on "its small
  signature and speed" and on collocating with a subordinate HQ for
  security; it is the commander's roving node, not a CP proper
  (p. 4-6/4-7).
- **Tactical CP (TAC CP).** "Primarily concentrates on conducting corps
  *close* operations." Deliberately kept "small and mobile … enhances
  its mobility and minimizes its signature." A single cohesive cell —
  current ops, intel, fire support, CS, CSS — under the corps G3.
  "Traditionally, the TAC CP positions well forward in the vicinity of a
  division main CP." Can operate mounted or dismounted. Secondary role:
  alternate for the rear CP (p. 4-7/4-8).
- **Main CP.** "Synchronizes the battle command system and provides
  continuity for corps operations." Future-oriented; runs corps *deep*
  operations and all planning. Organized by functional **cells** — CP
  headquarters cell (CofS + SGS), current-operations cell, plans cell,
  intelligence cell, fire-support cell, CSS cell (+ in this edition a
  DOCC and a C²W cell). "Controlled by the chief of staff." "It is a
  large organization with attendant mobility and signature problems. It
  normally locates in the general vicinity of the division rear
  boundaries. **If possible, it locates in a built-up area to hide its
  signature and take advantage of fixed facilities.**" Secondary role:
  alternate for the TAC CP and for the rear CP (p. 4-8/4-9).
  → The "built-up area / fixed facilities" line is directly relevant to
  a Rendsburg-fortress-town siting; it is US doctrine, but it is the
  general logic (hide a large signature inside urban fabric), not a
  German-specific rule.
- **Rear CP.** Runs rear operations under a rear-operations commander
  ("normally the deputy corps commander"), executing through subordinate
  Rear Area Operations Centers (RAOCs). "Normally locates in close
  proximity to the COSCOM CP." Organized by cell (HQ, operations, CSS).
  Explicitly "**not 100-percent mobile.**" Secondary role: alternate for
  the main CP (p. 4-12).

**Manning split:** FM 100-15 gives *functions per cell* but no personnel
counts — "the actual configuration and level of manning is based on …
planning factors that the commander determines" (p. 4-13). So a specific
head-count for a corps main CP is UNKNOWN from this source; the split is
qualitative (deep/plans weight at main, close weight at TAC, sustainment
weight at rear).

**Alternate-CP scheme (US, ESTABLISHED-as-doctrine, proxy edition):** the
model is *mutual alternacy*, not a dedicated standby — the main is the
alternate for the TAC and rear; the rear is the alternate for the main;
the TAC is the alternate for the rear. There is no separate "alternate
CP" facility in the corps set; continuity comes from another existing CP
assuming the lost/displacing one's functions.

### 1b. US division — period (1978)

Source: FM 71-100 (1978), p. 3-18. ESTABLISHED, period-correct.

- **Tactical CP:** "a small mobile tactical command post, located forward
  and staffed with selected assistants." Normal occupants: G3, USAF air
  liaison officer, and the DivArty commander or his assistant fire
  support coordinator. When the commander must go further forward he
  peels off a still-smaller **command group** in "one or two vehicles."
- **Main CP:** "located in the division rear area; **normally beyond the
  range of enemy field artillery.**" Occupants: G1/G3/G4, G2 + CEWI
  (combat electronic warfare and intelligence) elements, ADA, aviation,
  fire support, NBC staff, and a tactical air control party. "May be
  supervised by the chief of staff." "Primarily a communication" node.
  The manual's own emphasis line: "**Tight communications discipline and
  command control alternatives must be routinely practiced, and command
  post electronic signatures must be reduced.**"
- CSS is run separately from the division materiel management center
  (MMC), i.e. the sustainment split exists at division too.

So the *division* period picture (TAC + main, main sited beyond enemy FA
range) is the two-CP ancestor of the corps three-CP model. A corps main
sitting ~95-110 km deep (protagonist-echelon.md) is consistent with
"beyond FA range" scaled up a level.

### 1c. German — what the vocabulary is, and the limit of what we can say

Source: de.wikipedia Gefechtsstand (SECONDARY; cites HDv 100/200, **not**
HDv 100/100). INFERENCE-grade for the setting year — German terminology
is stable, but this is not a period regulation.

German-army CP echelonment vocabulary, formed "ab Bataillonsebene
aufwärts" (from battalion level up):

- **Hauptgefechtsstand** — main CP, "für die Gefechtsführung" (for
  battle command). The tactical/operational nerve centre.
- **Rückwärtiger Gefechtsstand** — rear CP, "für die Personal- und
  Versorgungsführung" (personnel and supply command). Same
  ops-forward / sustainment-rear split the US model shows.
- **Reservegefechtsstand** — reserve/alternate CP; "ab Brigadeebene
  aufwärts" both the Haupt- and rückwärtiger CP must have a
  Reservegefechtsstand prepared, and where possible a second
  Hauptgefechtsstand is stood up.
- **Bewegliche Befehlsstelle** — a mobile command element drawn from
  parts of the Hauptgefechtsstand, so the commander "den Gefechtsstand
  zu verlassen, ohne die Führung aus der Hand geben zu müssen" (can
  leave the CP without giving up command) — the German equivalent of the
  US command group.
- Internal organisation by **Zellen** grouped in **Zentralen**
  (Fernmelde-, Operations-, Feuerunterstützungs-, Informationszentrale,
  Führungszelle) — the cellular main-CP layout the US also uses.

**The load-bearing caution:** the German terms map cleanly onto the US
structure (Haupt ≈ main, Rückwärtiger ≈ rear, Reserve ≈ alternate,
bewegliche Befehlsstelle ≈ command group), which is why a
German/Danish/NATO corps at Rendsburg would recognise the whole scheme —
but the *mapping* is our inference from a secondary source, and the
citation behind it is **HDv 100/200, not HDv 100/100.** For prose, the
German nouns above are safe to use; any claim that HDv 100/100
*prescribes* a particular displacement drill or CP manning is UNKNOWN
until the regulation is in hand.

## 2. Displacement — doctrine and practice

### 2a. Why and how (US, proxy + period)

- **Echeloned displacement is the core technique.** FM 100-15: the main
  CP "must be capable of — Displacing while still retaining the ability
  to perform all of its required functions [and] **Echeloned
  displacement (movement of the entire CP by echelon or movement of each
  cell of the CTOC by echelon).**" The point is that the CP is *never
  wholly off the air*: cells or echelons bound while the remainder keeps
  operating (p. 4-8/4-9).
- **Dispersal as the standing alternative to a monolithic CP.** FM 100-15:
  "**Because of the unique signature of massed CPs and their greater
  vulnerability to acquisition and attack, the corps should disperse the
  cells of the command posts whenever feasible.**" A dispersed cell must
  be able to "conduct continuous operations while displacing (split
  operations or operating during movement)," independently enter comms
  nets, carry its own power and local security (p. 4-8/4-9).
- **Continuity via hand-off to another CP.** The mutual-alternacy scheme
  (§1a) is the continuity mechanism: when the main displaces or is lost,
  the rear CP (its designated alternate) can assume its functions; the
  main can assume the TAC's. There is no "the alternate CP takes command"
  as a separate facility — it is a *role* another standing CP picks up.
- **Reconnaissance-and-guide procedure (period, red-side but generic).**
  FM 71-100 (1978): during long moves "command posts may leapfrog
  forward along different routes. They are preceded by small
  reconnaissance parties that select new locations and guide traffic.
  **While on the move, command posts maintain continuous radio contact**
  … Normally an alternate command post moves behind the MAIN, prepared
  to take over control if [the main is lost]." This is described of
  Soviet practice but is the universal leapfrog drill.

### 2b. How often — the cadence figures

- **German secondary (INFERENCE):** de.wikipedia (citing HDv 100/200)
  states, verbatim, "**Gefechtsstände von Verbänden sollen alle sechs
  bis zwölf Stunden verlegt werden**" — CPs of *Verbände*
  (battalion/regiment-scale formations) should displace every 6-12
  hours; the same article reports that *Großverbände* (large formations
  — division/corps) typically relocate on the order of every ~48 hours.
  The 48-hour figure is reported by the article, not quoted verbatim
  here, and is not tied to a 1983 edition — treat as order-of-magnitude.
  → Doctrinal shape this supports: **small forward CPs move often (twice
  a day-plus); a corps main moves rarely — days apart, not hours** —
  precisely because a corps main is the "large organization with
  attendant mobility and signature problems" that wants a built-up-area
  fixed facility (§1a). This is the doctrinal grain that makes *one*
  mid-campaign displacement of a corps main realistic rather than a
  constant churn.
- **US period manuals give NO fixed corps-main displacement interval or
  duration.** FM 100-15 and FM 100-5 prescribe the *technique*
  (echeloned, dispersed, on survivability triggers) but no clock. A
  specific "a corps main takes N hours to displace and re-establish"
  figure is **UNKNOWN from doctrine on the shelf** — it is a
  commander/METT-T judgement, not a published standard.
- **Modern order-of-magnitude only (NOT period, NOT corps):** current US
  writing reports brigade TOCs cutting "jump" times from ~18 hours to
  under 2, and emplace/displace drills to ~30 minutes — evidence that a
  *small* CP moves in minutes-to-hours, a *large* one in many hours. Use
  as sanity-check on scale, not as a period corps figure.

### 2c. Triggers for displacement (US doctrine, ESTABLISHED)

Displacement is survivability-driven, not scheduled: signature
compromise / suspected fix by enemy RSTA, the tactical situation moving
the battle out from under the CP's siting (FM 100-15 notes main and rear
CPs "displace forward into the corps" as the front advances; conversely a
withdrawal pulls them back), and the standing preference to relocate
"as needed to enhance its survivability" (p. 4-8). For the book: a
harassment pattern that reveals the enemy holds the CP's location is
itself the textbook trigger.

## 3. Survivability rationale — the threat that drives dispersal/displacement

US doctrine names the CP as a priority target and builds the whole
echelonment/dispersal/displacement regime around keeping it alive:

- **Massed signature = acquisition + attack risk.** FM 100-15's own
  rationale for dispersing cells is "the unique signature of massed CPs
  and their greater vulnerability to acquisition and attack" (p. 4-9).
- **Electronic signature specifically.** FM 71-100 (1978): "command post
  electronic signatures must be reduced"; comms discipline and "command
  control alternatives" are to be "routinely practiced" (p. 3-18) — i.e.
  radio emissions are how the CP gets found (RDF), and the countermeasure
  is emission control plus a rehearsed hand-off to an alternate.
- **CPs as named targets in the fires plan.** FM 100-5 (1982) repeatedly
  lists command posts among the high-payoff targets to be attacked
  ("destroying his command posts, neutralizing communications, reserves";
  deep attack "targets such as command posts").
- **This project's own red-side sourcing already matches the doctrine.**
  planning/hq-displacement-brief.md §1 records our captured-planning
  extraction (php-maritime-front.md:342, Bange, quote+page) tasking OMGs
  with "destruction of rockets, command posts, reserves," and the Scud
  brigade (5. RBr Demen) is documented. So the threat picture the US
  manuals abstractly describe is, on our shelf, concretely aimed at
  this theatre. The gaps the brief flags remain: **Spetsnaz/diversionary
  (special-purpose forces) threat is absent from our shelf**, and nothing
  yet maps red fires specifically onto the LANDJUT HQ. The four classic
  CP threats — tube/rocket artillery and SSM reach, radio direction
  finding, air attack, and special-purpose ground forces — are all
  doctrinally standard; our shelf documents the first three for this
  theatre and is silent on the fourth.

## 4. What moving costs in communications

- **The area/trunk system is what has to be re-established.** A corps CP
  rides a switched area network (in the proxy edition, MSE; in 1983, the
  AN/TTC-series corps/division area common-user system run by the corps
  **signal brigade** / division **signal battalion**). FM 100-15: the
  signal brigade "provides C² for from one to three **area signal
  battalions**" and the corps area network, fully deployed, covers
  ~37,000 km² (App. A, p. A-26). Moving the main CP means the trunk
  extension into that CP has to be torn down and rebuilt at the new site.
- **Continuity mechanism during the move (proxy):** "The mobile
  telephone can provide some commanders and other selected users
  continuous access to the area communications system **during movement
  and CP displacement**" (p. A-26) — i.e. the commander stays reachable
  on a mobile subscriber terminal while the CP's fixed trunk drops and
  re-homes. **CAUTION: that specific capability is MSE (late-80s/90s);
  in 1983 the equivalent is far more limited** — the command group /
  bewegliche Befehlsstelle carries combat-net radio (VHF FM), not a
  mobile trunk phone, so the 1983 cost of moving is bluntly: **the main
  CP's multichannel trunk goes down for the duration of the tear-down,
  road move, and re-establishment, and command rides on radio and on the
  alternate CP until the trunk is back.**
- **Re-establishment time = UNKNOWN (no shelf figure).** Multichannel/
  trunk emplacement times for the period area-signal battalions are not
  in the documents fetched (FM 24-18, single-channel radio, is on the
  shelf but is not the trunk system). This is a real number the scene
  might want and the shelf cannot yet supply. TO-ACQUIRE.

## 5. Bonus — Soviet/red CP echelonment (useful for the threat picture)

FM 71-100 (1978), opposing-forces chapter (ESTABLISHED as US-assessed
Soviet practice, period). Included because it is what red's own doctrine
expects of *its* CPs and, mirror-imaged, what red's targeteers assume of
blue's:

- Combined-arms army and divisions control from **forward, main, and
  rear** CPs, with **alternate** CPs designated.
- Division **forward CP**: near the rear of the most threatened
  first-echelon regiment, "usually within 5 kilometers of the forward
  edge of the main defense belt."
- Division **main and alternate CPs**: "an additional 5 to 10 kilometers
  to the rear with lateral dispersion of some 5 kilometers." The
  alternate's radio "is used only to monitor activities at forward and
  main command posts" (emission-silent standby).
- Division **rear CP**: collocated with logistics, "10 to 30 kilometers
  to the rear of the FEBA."
- Army/front CPs "are normally more static and are often deployed in
  tentage or abandoned buildings" — the higher the echelon, the more
  static, the same logic as the US corps main preferring a fixed
  built-up facility.
