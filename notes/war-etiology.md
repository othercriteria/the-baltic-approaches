# The war's etiology — authorial position (not canon-for-insertion)

*Ruled 2026-07-27 (DK + entity e3137278@3, in-session
discussion). Status: project background for the record and for
future readers of the making. The text treats causation as
out-of-scope ON PRINCIPLE, and nothing here is to be inserted
into prose. Under the book's epistemic discipline (characters
don't know what they haven't learned), nobody at Rendsburg can
know why the war happened, so nobody may say.*

## The ruling

Not a Red Storm Rising-style rational resource grab under
concealed information. **A red misreading of a closing window,
ratified by an approval machine that could not reverse itself,
against a blue whose contribution was the ambient legibility of
its own deterrent — not a decision.**

## The assembled evidence (all planted, none stated)

**Window, not prize.** Red's object is positional and
instrumental — "the port, and the canal behind the port, the
Atlantic door the operation was approved to open" (ch. 17). No
acquisitive motive anywhere. What red has instead is December,
three times over: the sea closes in December (the operational
clock); the cease-fire comes in December; and — ch. 2's period
furniture — "the missile deployments were due in December."
The last is the quiet load-bearer: a leadership that believes
the board changes irreversibly when the Euromissiles land has a
closing window in exactly November 1983 — the month pinned, the
month history filled with RYaN and the war scare. The approval
argument Zawadzki witnessed ("over before it became the other
kind of war") is preventive-window logic.

**Beyond easy undoing — the book's actual thesis about red.**
The norms as "the rent the operation owed its own approval";
the schedule "built for approval, in peacetime, by men who
needed it to say what it says"; a relief directive that cannot
contain the word relief. Red's war continues because its
bureaucracy cannot draft the sentence that ends it — the
required signature is a certificate of culmination the
incentive structure punishes (Kuragin's dates; "my relief from
command, drafted politely"). The weather signs it instead.
Hvidt's ch. 2 principle scaled to the top: *when a plan fails
it is usually at an orphan* — the war itself is the book's
biggest orphaned number: a misreading nobody owned, adopted by
an approval chain, inherited by everybody, audited by events.

**Blue's contribution: structural, not decisional.** Ch. 2:
the alert catalog, "every numbered measure therefore a signal
the moment it was invoked, with the enemy as the catalog's
first reader" — blue's readiness grammar is legible to red by
design and red's reading of it is beyond blue's control.
Kjeldsen (ch. 6): "I have seen an alert measure invented out
of three coincidences before. By us, about them." Aakjær
(ch. 2): "Almost nothing [would look different if aimed at us].
That is the property of the period we are in." Blue-as-corps
did the opposite of precipitating (the quiet list is a study
in muting); blue-as-alliance spent its autumn emitting exactly
the signal-shaped things (exercise season; the deployments
themselves) a fear-driven watch would misread. Blue could
mute; it could not be silent.

**Why out-of-scope is principled.** The only causation
testimony in the book is red's — "I sat in the room" — and it
describes an approval, not a decision: a room adopting an
argument. The instruments price the war's costs to the
decimal; its cause sits permanently in the unpriceable column
("the operational initiative … is lying on the table," ch. 12
— the war as ownerless object). The book's position in one
sentence: **responsibility for the war's existence is
unknowable from inside it; responsibility for its price is
exactly knowable, and that is the subject.**
