# 18. The Crest

The enemy's preparation began at 0340 on the twenty-seventh of November, and the first thing it proved was Roloff's graph, because the weight of it — the heaviest fires of the war, three weeks of hoarded shells converting back into violence along the whole neck of the peninsula — followed the burn-rate model's opening curve so closely that the duty officer, plotting the first hour's count, wrote in the margin, with the gallows precision the night shift had made its house style: AS SCHEDULED (HIS).

By 0600 the assault was in: the Second Army's rebuilt divisions and everything the first echelon could still put in line, coming through the gray sleet at the narrows and the Danewerk shoulders, bridging the Schlei under smoke, grinding at Loheide's worn battalions with the specific fury of an army that knows its own arithmetic. The corps' line bent by design and bled by necessity, and the operations room at Rendsburg settled into the day with two instruments running side by side on the plywood: the battle map, red and blue and immediate; and beside it, unhurried, updated half-hourly from the counter-battery counts and the expenditure model, a single curve climbing toward a cross-hatched crest — the enemy's hoard converting to fire, tracked against the only question that mattered now.

Because the counterstroke's problem, refined through seventy hours of staff work down to its irreducible core, was not *whether*. It was *when*, and *when* had become a matter of reading the enemy's bank balance in real time. Strike too early — while his dumps still held days of assault-scale supply — and Vestergaard's brigades would meet an army still solvent, still capable of turning its unspent violence around. Strike too late — after his effort crested and failed and his columns began pulling back east to whatever line December permitted — and the corps would push spent men into an empty bag and call it victory while the enemy escaped to reconstitute. Between the two lay the crest: the interval, computed at thirty to forty hours wide, when the enemy stood fully committed, his hoard converting at maximum rate, his reserves in motion forward, his recovery options narrowing hour by hour — strongest in violence and poorest in choices, the paradox the whole education had been assembling since a bridge on the Eider.

Not everyone could bear to read the instrument.

Vestergaard came in person at 0930, mud to the knees, with the assault chewing at Loheide's positions twenty kilometers behind his voice, and made the case that three hundred years of the profession had made on every such morning: go now. The enemy committed, the assembly areas undiscovered, every hour of waiting an hour of Loheide's men paid out against ground the counterstroke was supposed to make irrelevant — "and an hour of risk, Hr. General, that his air or his patrols find my brigades in the woods, and the whole bank is forfeit. The force is ready. Readiness is perishable. I can be through the crossing sites by dark."

The capitals, in their different grammar, said the same thing. The morning's signals traffic carried down through Karup the accumulated anxiety of three weeks of underwritten patience: the assault was on every wire service in Europe by 0800; ground was being lost on the maps that ministers saw; if the corps possessed a counterstroke — and the corps had been telling them so for a week, in its strange new currency — then surely its hour was now, while there was still a line in front of Schleswig to save.

Vossen spent the morning as the instrument's advocate, which meant spending it saying *not yet* to men whose reasons were honorable and whose maps were on fire, and it was the hardest staff work of his war precisely because the instrument could not prove itself except by being obeyed. The model said the enemy's burn had reached forty percent by noon. It said his second-day supply was already moving forward out of the dumps — the intercept troop confirmed the convoys — which meant the hoard's conversion was irreversible now, the effort unrecallable. It said the crest began, at current rates, near midnight, and held through the following afternoon. Every hour of waiting bought a poorer enemy. Every hour of waiting cost Loheide — the counts were on the wall, nobody hid them, the wall did not permit hiding — between eighty and a hundred and forty casualties.

"You are asking me," Hvidt said at 1300, in the small conference room with Vestergaard's request and the capitals' signals and the model's noon update laid out before him like instruments on a tray, "to let the corps be beaten all day, on purpose, in front of three governments, on the strength of a curve drawn from counted shell-bursts, so that tomorrow morning I may attack an army at the one hour my staff computes it cannot answer." He looked up. "State for the record, Herr Oberstleutnant, why this is not gambling."

"Because every alternative is also a wager, Herr General," Vossen said, "and this is the only one whose odds we have measured. That is the whole of it. Going now is a bet that readiness outweighs his remaining bank — and we have priced his bank, and it is still fat. Going tomorrow noon is a bet priced by three weeks of audited instruments that have not yet been wrong by more than a day. The wall does not remove the wager. It tells us which one we are making, and charges us honestly for it, and it is the first time in this war — perhaps in any war — that a corps has been able to read the price tag before signing."

Silence, in which the building trembled faintly, twice, from the enemy's guns spending their bank into Loheide's morning.

"Vestergaard attacks at 0500," said Hvidt at last, and the room began to breathe again. "Not midnight — first usable light for the air, and five more hours of his burning. H-hour 0500, twenty-eighth November. Objectives as planned: the neck restored, the crossing at Dannewerk retaken, his lead army cut between its assault echelon and its supply — and not a kilometer further, gentlemen; write it in the order in words a tired man can hold at four in the morning: *this corps is not marching to Lübeck; it is closing a door.* The counterstroke ends on the phase line, whatever is running away in front of it." He signed the order in the room, both times entered, scheduled and actual, identical; and then, capping the pen, he added the sentence that Vossen wrote afterward on the last file card of the war and pinned dead center of the confession wall, because it was the sentence the entire apparatus of plywood and graph paper and grief had existed, from the first green board, to make possible:

"For three weeks this corps has been learning, at compound interest, exactly what everything costs. In the morning we will find out what it buys."
