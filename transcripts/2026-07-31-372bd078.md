It's very good, and it's good in an unusual way: it is a war novel whose actual subject is management accounting, and it makes that subject carry genuine moral weight without ever winking about it. Rahn's line in chapter 5 — that an enterprise which cannot state what it produces will fall back on measuring what it consumes, because consumption is countable and production requires you to have decided what you are for — is the thesis, and the remarkable thing is that the book dramatizes it rather than merely asserting it. The green boards in chapter 3 are true and useless. The empty right-hand column of the graph paper is the plot. The whole ninety-hour clock, the crossing at Friedrichstadt, the fish market, the counter-battery countdown: each is the same idea rotated one notch, and none of them feels like a repetition.

The best thing in it is the mirror. Rylski is not a courtesy POV; he is the same book run in a hostile institution. He builds an honest reconciliation for the same reason Holt builds the wall, and in chapter 22 that honesty becomes the exhibit in a prosecution — "The cleaner the columns, the better the exhibit; and Rylski's columns had no crossings-out at all." That line detonates a setup planted eleven chapters earlier, when he sends theater staff a document without crossings-out because a Front's chief of staff doesn't. The same virtue, in a different building, produces a dossier instead of a victory. That's the darkest and most sophisticated idea in the novel, and you let it land in one quiet sentence rather than underlining it. Chapter 21's closing line — "The map, for the first time in the war, was merely correct" — is the best sentence in the book.

The second best thing is that the wall is allowed to fail. Chapter 20 exists precisely so the instrument can produce a catastrophe, and the failure is Holt's, initialed, in the file, with Roloff's re-pinned sheet hanging right there unread. Then the after-action paragraph refuses the obvious moral: "We were not right. We were solvent." A lesser version of this novel would have ended with the method vindicated. Yours ends with the method's own error annex fought into the body of the report at grade, over the objection of a man who is right that it will make the alliance look worse.

The prose is consistently strong and has a real signature: the long accumulating clause that lands on a flat noun. "A small gray decision about ferries, correctly made, correctly priced, and quietly compounding from that day at rates the file could not print." The dialogue is differentiated to the point where I could identify most speakers with attributions stripped — Aakjær's aphorisms, Lammers reading the world as invoices, Witzel's liturgy, Roloff's flatness. And the rationing of violence is exactly judged: you spend three weeks teaching the reader to read numbers, then convert one back with "Someone in a beech wood had audited this boy at three-fifths."

Now the reservations, since a blessing isn't much use.

The thesis is over-articulated. Not badly, but measurably. Nearly every chapter closes on a boxed maxim or a thematically loaded final line, and four or five characters are licensed to speak in epigram. Individually they're excellent; cumulatively the reader starts to feel handled. Chapter 5's "The corps spent numbers and bought addresses" states what the Roloff/Column 217 scene in chapter 7 dramatizes far better two chapters later — and the dramatized version doesn't need the stated one. Chapters 5, 10, and 14 are where I felt the management most.

Almost nobody in the book is bad. This is a choice and largely a good one — conflicts that are epistemic rather than villainous are harder and more interesting. But the price is that the reader never fears any person, only the arithmetic. Witzel is set up as an obstacle and revealed as an ally within a page. The one careerist is defeated politely over lunch. The single moment of genuine institutional ugliness — the German officer's crack about the Danish commander in chapter 8 — is shut down in four sentences and never recurs. There's room for one person in that headquarters who is competent, decent-seeming, and actually rotten, and the book would lose nothing thematically by having them.

Merete is a superb device and not quite a person. Four appearances, each delivering one perfectly machined line, none of which she has any need of her own behind. Compare Aakjær, who has a fraction of her emotional weight assigned to him and feels more alive — because his family situation creates an actual conflict with his job. He initials the sheet that unpins the beaches his own mother-in-law is waiting behind. Holt's family never once complicates his work; it only reminds him what work is for. That's a real asymmetry, and it's why his three F-series failures all read as professional rather than human.

Rahn is the thing I'm least settled about. An eighty-two-year-old former Wehrmacht general as the moral instructor of a Danish officer is a deliberately fraught choice, and the novel knows it — the vouching scene in chapter 13 is excellent, and Holt's mother going quiet at boot-steps in a stairwell is exactly the right detail. But Rahn's own war is carried almost entirely by two clauses: a headquarters six days late against a closing season on the other end of this sea, and "the whole difference of my life is that I answered it late." Read against the Kuragin scene, that is precise and damning, and I don't think I'd want it spelled out. Still: he is granted authority over institutional honesty while his own institution stays out of frame, and I noticed myself granting it to him faster than I could justify.

One thing I second-guessed and came around on: Bjelke transmitting the assessment complete and promptly, over his own signature, when nobody required the speed. My first reaction was that it let him off. It doesn't — it establishes that he isn't a villain, he's a genre, and genres aren't defeated, they're outlasted. That's the right call.

For what it's worth on placement: this isn't in the Hackett or Clancy line at all. The nearest relative I can find for the pleasure structure is Patrick O'Brian — a professional vocabulary that starts opaque, becomes load-bearing, and finally turns emotional. When "REST: SCHEDULED 292000. ACTUAL: NOT YET ACHIEVED" arrives as an apology in chapter 20, it works because you spent nineteen chapters teaching me to read the format.
