# 6 — The Locked Door

The folder lived in the second drawer of the safe in the plans
cell, and its cover sheet said REQUEST PROCEDURES, SPECIAL
WEAPONS RELEASE — FORMATS AND CHANNELS, and on the fifth
morning of the war Roloff asked the question that had to come.

"The situation summary in the draft, Herr Oberstløjtnant. It
still describes the line as it stood on the eighth. Under the
two-times order, do I update it?"

Holt had known the folder was there for thirteen months, the
way one knows the household insurance is in the bureau: a
thing maintained, never read twice. Inside it was a message
skeleton, FROM COMLANDJUT TO COMBALTAP, with blanks. It was
the first rung of a ladder that went up through Karup, and
from Karup to the theater command at Kolsås, and from there
out of military channels altogether, into committees whose
names were themselves classified, wearing at every rung a
footnote in which the Danish government reserved its position
on the stationing question — a ladder built deliberately
slow, by serious men, so that no one could run up it in an
afternoon.

"No," Holt said. "The draft is not a living document. File
unchanged."

Roloff filed it unchanged. But a question asked once in a
room does not file. It stood in the corner of the plans cell
all morning, and by evening it had walked down the corridor
on its own legs, because Kjeldsen had asked its cousin on
Tuesday — why is there no gas in this war, when every
exercise scenario of ten years had it by D+3? — and two
questions of that family in one week meant the subject was
loose in the headquarters. Hvidt heard of it, as he heard of
everything, and did the thing nobody expected: he convened
it.

"One hour," he said. "Both trees, walked once, out loud, to
the end of every branch. Then the door is locked and this
headquarters returns to its war. I would rather the subject
stood in this office for one hour than lived in the corridors
for three weeks."

---

Aakjær walked the enemy's tree, and he walked it with
receipts.

"I begin with behavior, because intentions are for
novelists. Point one. He attacked conventionally, owning a
faster timetable. The quick variant exists in his doctrine —
we have read it for twenty years: the strikes come with the
first echelon, the war is over before mobilization matters.
He declined it. That is a decision with a date on it,
gentlemen, taken above him, before the eighth. Point two: his
fires are careful in a pattern. The canal locks are untouched
on the fifth day. The port machinery at Kiel and Flensburg —
untouched, while the rail approaches to both are cratered.
Bridges: dropped at the approach spans, where engineers
repair in days, not at the piers, where they repair in
months. Add it up. That is not mercy; it is a shop kept
ready for a new owner. He is fighting for working
infrastructure, and the weapons in Kjeldsen's question
poison the shop. The restraint is not a policy he holds. It
is a consequence of what his operation is for."

"Then the answer to my Tuesday question," Kjeldsen said, "is
bookkeeping."

"The answer is bookkeeping. His. And note what the
bookkeeping implies: the restraint is rented. It holds
exactly as long as his ledger says the shop can still be
had whole. Point three, the protective tax: we drill in the
suits eleven percent slower — that is measured, it was
measured on us in October. He pays the same tax. A man
racing a mobilization clock does not put on the suit unless
he must. His restraint is cheap for him and expensive to
abandon, which is the most durable kind."

"And the last branch," Hvidt said. "If his ledger changes."

"Then the decision goes back where it came from: above him.
The weapons on his side of the water do not belong to the
men in front of us. The button is further from the fingers
that are bleeding, Herr General. On both sides. It is the
one symmetry in this war I am grateful for."

Holt walked the corps' own tree with Witzel supplying the
rungs, because the rungs were procedure and procedure was
Witzel's mother tongue: request initiated only over the
corps commander's signature; assessed at Karup; forwarded
with theater endorsement to Kolsås; national authorities
concurring — the footnote — and above all of it the alliance
level, where release belonged to a man in another country
answerable to sixteen governments. "The procedure is the
policy," Witzel said. "It was built to be slow in exactly
one direction, like a lobster pot."

"And the operational meaning," Holt said, "is that beginning
the request is itself an act. It cannot be done quietly. The
day this headquarters updates that folder's situation
summary, the update is visible — in our own message traffic,
in the questions we would have to ask the brigades, in what
the staff stops believing about its own conventional
defense. We would be spending belief, ours and the
capitals', at the exact hour we needed it most. The folder's
usefulness is that it exists and is shut. We are defending
three governments from their own decision tree."

"Both trees agree, then," Hvidt said. "Two conclusions." He
held up one finger. "His restraint is rented, and the rent
is his own timetable. Our whole work is to put him behind
it — and gentlemen, note the paradox and then stop looking
at it: the better we fight, the longer his patience is asked
to last. That is not a reason to fight worse." The second
finger. "This headquarters now stops discussing the subject.
The hour is spent. Questions of Kjeldsen's kind get Aakjær's
answer, once, and then silence. The folder keeps two times,
the day it was filed and the word never, and the second time
is an intention, not a prophecy, and every man in this room
will conduct himself so as to make it come true. It is the
most useful hour I have spent in this war, and we will not
spend it again."

He locked the office door behind them, which was theater,
and effective.

---

Past midnight, in the corridor under the blue nightlights,
Aakjær fell into step beside Holt with his hands in his
pockets.

"We answered the old man's question tonight. Yours. From the
reception."

"I noticed."

"Making his schedule fail while ours does not. Everything
else on the boards is expenditure toward one of those two
clauses or it is waste. That is what the corps is for, and
we derived it in an hour, from the worst subject in the
inventory, with the door locked." A few steps of quiet; the
nightlights hummed. "Which means the work divides. Somebody
must learn his schedule well enough to know when it is
failing. That is mine. And somebody must learn ours well
enough to swear to it — actually swear, Niels, in numbers,
not in green — and that one is yours, and I saw the graph
paper in the operations room this evening, and I observe
that the right-hand column is still ragged."

He turned off at the intelligence cell without saying good
night, which was his way, and Holt went on to the operations
room and stood a while in front of the taped sheets.

The good had a name now, or half of one. Time — but not time
in the abstract, not the calendar's time: the distance
between two schedules, his failing and ours holding, a
specific, purchasable, spendable thing. The ragged right
column was where its price would have to be discovered.

Now: who owned it. And what, in God's name, it was costing a
day.
