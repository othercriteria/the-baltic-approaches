# 5. Green Against What

Belt BRAVO went in the night of the ninth, and the corps learned what its second defensive belt was worth in the same twelve hours that it learned what its reporting was worth, and the two lessons were the same lesson wearing different uniforms.

The night began with the demolitions. From the operations room the belt was forty-one prepared targets on an overlay — road cratering, three rail overpasses, the Treene bridges, culverts sown along the secondary routes like buttons on a coat. The execution was a triumph by every metric the corps kept: forty of forty-one fired, confirmed, logged with the two times the commander required, scheduled against actual, and for once the columns nearly matched. Holt watched the acknowledgments come in and felt the room's mood lift with each one, and could not fault it; the engineers had done in freezing rain under shellfire what they had rehearsed for years, and done it almost perfectly, and men are entitled to their competence.

By 0400 the enemy was across the Treene in two places.

Not because the demolitions had failed. Because — the picture assembled itself through the small hours, report by report, in the way of night actions, wrong twice before it was right — because the enemy had not treated the river as an obstacle to be repaired. He had treated it as a scheduled event. The bridging arrived at the water within ninety minutes of the last demolition, corps-level bridging, far forward, in quantities the intelligence annex had faithfully listed and nobody had imagined *concentrated* — and while the German gunners fought the bridge sites, the enemy's infantry crossed elsewhere in assault boats and rubber rafts and, at one recorded location, by wading, in November, in ranks, with their weapons held over their heads, taking losses that the covering force's forward observers reported in tones of disbelief, and continuing. It was 6th Panzergrenadier's ground down there, German soil, and Holt made himself count the hours and not the German division bleeding for them.

"He is paying men to save hours," Aakjær said, reading the night's summaries at 0530 with the fixed attention he brought to enemy behavior that confirmed something. "Note it. Every other army haggles. This one has a price list, and the price list says an hour is worth more than a company. That is not brutality, hr. oberstløjtnant, whatever the forward battalions think this morning. It is arithmetic we have not seen the rest of."

The staff's answer to the crossing was in the plan, and the plan's answer was BELT CHARLIE and the third trade of space for time, and at 0600 the plan's answer met the other thing, the thing that had been waiting in the corps' paperwork since the first chinagraph went up: the line on the map that was not a military line.

It had a name in the operations annex — the political guidance line, coordinated Bonn–Copenhagen–Brussels, updated by diplomatic note — and it ran along the general trace Husum–Schleswig–Eckernförde, and its annex language was a masterpiece of the possible: *forward of this line, ground will not be voluntarily relinquished except under military necessity, the determination of which is reserved.* Reserved to whom, the annex did not say. In BOLD LANCER the umpires had ruled the question out of play. Now, at 0600 on the tenth of November, with the covering force asking permission to conduct the passage of lines behind CHARLIE — behind, that is, the northern shoulder of the guidance line — the question rang the telephones of three capitals, and the corps stood still for five hours while it was answered.

Five hours. Holt spent them in the operations room doing a G-3's job, which that morning meant holding two divisions' worth of movement requests in the air, unsigned. The military logic was one page: the covering force was spent, the enemy's bridging was across, every hour of delay in the passage of lines would be paid for at the rate the night had established, a company an hour. The political logic was also one page, and Hvidt — who had spent the five hours on the secure line to Karup, saying little, listening with his hand flat on the desk — put it to the staff afterward without editorializing, which was its own comment:

"Copenhagen and Bonn require the line held forward of Schleswig until the civil movement of the border Kreise is —" he consulted, deliberately, the exact words — "*substantially complete*. I asked what substantially means. I was told it is being determined. Gentlemen: the corps will hold forward of the guidance line until 1800 tomorrow at the earliest. The military necessity clause is not available to us today. That is an order I have accepted, and it is therefore my order." He stood a moment. "Cost it," he said to Holt, and left the room.

*Cost it.* The instruction landed on the staff like a foreign phrase, and Holt, who spent his days translating one, had a form for it in none. The corps had a whole directorate for costing things — Lammers' empire costed fuel, tubes, track-miles, bridge tonnage — but the thing the commander wanted priced was an *order*: eighteen more hours forward of a line the trade said to leave, expressed in what the eighteen hours would buy and what they would burn. The reporting system did not have a form for it. That was the discovery of the afternoon. The corps could report readiness, strength, expenditure, position — every noun in the inventory — and it could not, with any instrument on the boards, answer the commander's actual question, which was a question about *exchange*: what are we spending, and what are we getting, and are the two written in any common currency at all.

Roloff built the first version of the answer that evening on two sheets of graph paper taped together, because there was no form. Down the left, the corps' spendables, the night's actual rates: companies rendered ineffective per twelve hours forward of CHARLIE; tube ammunition per twelve hours at the current fire program; the two river crossing sets the enemy had not yet forced. Down the right, the corps' buyables, and here the sheet went thin, because what the eighteen hours bought stood in three different ledgers kept by three different staffs: kilometers of civil column cleared eastward off route B76 (the Territorial Command's number, telephoned, approximate); Danish brigades closed south of the Belt (Lammers' number, exact, and shrinking in usefulness by the hour as the roads filled); and something Aakjær had inserted in his own hand at the bottom, unquantified, flagged with a question mark: *time purchased against the arrival of the enemy's REAL mass — value unknown, since we have not located it.*

Holt's eye stopped on the middle line. Of the three it was the only one written in his own country — *Jyske brigader syd for Lillebælt*, whatever a German colonel had typed — and it was the line the eighteen hours were buying, not the line doing the buying. The Kreise emptying eastward were somebody else's home; the covering force dying forward, somebody else's division; and behind both, past the water, Fredericia and the ramparts and Kirsten and the children on the safe side of a bridge, sleeping there because this German ground held one more day. He was costing an ally's soil to finish arming his own, and could do it more cleanly than a man with a house in the border Kreise, and did not trust the cleanness.

Holt took the taped sheets to Hvidt at 2200 and watched the commander read them for a long time.

"This is the page I asked whether existed," Hvidt said finally. "It did not exist. Now it half exists." He tapped the right-hand column, the ragged one. "Everything we spend, we can count. Almost nothing we buy has a number, and the numbers it has are owned by three headquarters and a police president. Put it on the wall of the operations room. Not the tidy boards. This. I want every officer who briefs me to have walked past it."

He telephoned Eckernförde at close to midnight, on the civil exchange, from the small office with the coffee ring on the map of the Baltic Approaches, and he could not afterward have said cleanly why — except that the taped sheets had a shape, and the shape reminded him of a question he had failed to answer at a party three weeks ago, and there was nobody in the headquarters he could say that to. He had argued all day in English and would speak now in German; and it struck him on the ring that the tongue he would have said any of it in — to Kirsten, to himself — was a third one, with no one to hear it tonight.

Rahn answered on the sixth ring, unsurprised, as if eighty-two-year-old generals sat by their telephones at midnight in the second week of a war, which, Holt reflected, they possibly did.

"Holt. You are calling me on an open line from a headquarters at war, so you will say nothing of substance, and I will ask nothing. Good. Then we can talk about the only thing worth talking about." A chair creaked in Eckernförde. "You have come to tell me the answer to my question."

"I have come to tell you," Holt said, and found the sentence assembling itself out of the graph paper, in the German the old man had drilled into him for a year in Hamburg, "that I know what we are spending. To the decimal. And that I cannot state what we are buying — not to the decimal; at all — because everything we buy is written in somebody else's ledger, in somebody else's unit of account, and half of it in ledgers I cannot see. And that tonight I priced eighteen hours, on the orders of a man I am beginning to respect, and the two columns would not balance because they are not in the same currency."

The line hissed gently. When Rahn spoke there was something new in his voice, and it took Holt a moment to identify it as satisfaction strictly suppressed.

"Three weeks," Rahn said. "Faster than I was. — No. I will not tell you the answer, Holt; be serious; if I tell you, you will write it in an annex and it will die there. I will tell you the shape of the answer, and you will do the work, because the work is the answer. Listen. An enterprise that cannot state what it produces will always — always, in war as in a factory as in a marriage — fall back on measuring what it consumes, because consumption is countable and production requires you to have decided what you are *for*. Your columns will not balance because you are trying to buy an unnamed good with countable expenditure. So: name the good. One good. Not a list — the moment it is a list, you have decided nothing. And when you have named it, ask of that ragged right-hand column of yours a single question: which line, if it went to zero, would make all the other lines worthless?" A pause; the chair again. "When you can answer that, telephone me, and we will discuss what your headquarters should stop doing. It will be a long list, and it will be the first cheerful conversation of your war. Good night, Herr Oberstleutnant. My regards to the graph paper."

He rang off — *Herr Oberstleutnant*, as he had called him the whole year in Hamburg, never once the Danish rank. Holt sat for a moment in the small office, hearing the operations room through the wall — the teleprinters, the low bilingual traffic of the night shift, a war being administered in two languages and his own not among them — and then he took out his notebook and wrote, under the day's two-times entries, four words that would have meant nothing to any board in the building, and drew a box around them the way Roloff boxed his totals.

*Name the good. One.*

*What resisted: the resonance beat. Vossen's own Kreis sits inside the hold order — "cost it" prices the evacuation of his own street. Holt's home is behind the Little Belt, not in the hold-line Kreise, so the beat could not stay put; it migrated honestly to the buyables column — the Jutland brigades are what the holding buys, so Holt's stake sits on the ledger's right side, not its left: cooler, mediated, one remove off. That differential is the datum, not a defect. What came free: the triangulation — English by day, German to Rahn, Danish unspeakable — gives the ache a home the German cell lacks, and a Dane watching 6th PzGren bleed forward to finish the Danish mobilization is a clean inversion. Net: a domestic gut-punch traded for a foreigner pricing an ally's ground with a clarity his own stake never clouds. One cost: making that mediated stake explicit ran the body slightly long — the German carried it implicitly.*
