# 11. Days Behind

The evening report at the Coastal Front's forward command post was read at 1900, and on the eleventh night of the war it ran nineteen minutes over, because General dywizji Rylski had appended an annex of his own composition, and insisted, with the courtesy that in that army was a form of armor, on reading it aloud.

He called it, without irony, the reconciliation. Down one column, the plan: the phase lines with their blessed dates, the tonnages the timetable assumed delivered, the crossings assumed open, the rates assumed sustained. Down the other, the Front's own reporting, which Rylski had spent three nights doing something unusual with: not summarizing it — auditing it. Reading it against itself. The Fourth Army reported the Day Six line reached; the Fourth Army's own fuel state, reported two pages later in the same folder, was possible only if its lead regiments stood twenty kilometers short of where its operations map put them. The engineer directorate reported the Owschlag gap bridged in nine hours; the same directorate's equipment return showed bridging losses that made nine hours arithmetically pious. The reports did not lie, exactly. Each staff had reported the version of its day that its own norms required of it, and the versions had been added together three echelons deep, and the sum was a Front advancing on the map at a rate the Front's own logistics could not have sustained if every road in Schleswig-Holstein had been empty and dry.

"By the plan's clock," Rylski finished, "we stand this evening at Day Six positions on Day Eleven. Five days behind, Comrade General — by our own ledger, honestly cast. And the five days are not level; they tilt. Two of them accumulated in the first week. Three in the last four days. The lag is compounding, because every day late at the front is a day the enemy's reserve army spends arriving, and a day our stocks spend being eaten by distance." He laid the annex flat. "I am not reporting failure. The Front advances; the enemy bleeds; his peninsula shortens. I am reporting a *rate*. And I am obliged to remind the Front commander what the plan itself says about rates. The operation was approved against a schedule. The schedule was the argument that won approval — a fast conventional decision, the Straits reached before the Alliance's ponderous bodies could assemble their answer, the theater sealed while the question of wider war remained politically unripe. Slow, the operation is a different operation. Slow was never approved. Every day of the five, we conduct, in effect, an operation nobody authorized — and above us, Comrade General, sit men who will notice this arithmetic later than I do, but not never."

Silence in the beech-wood bunker, and in it, from the visitors' corner, the small sound of General-mayor Kuragin turning a page of his own folder — a sound like a man clearing his throat with paper.

Zawadzki stood at the map for a long time. What Vossen would have recognized, had any channel existed to show him — what did exist, that night, only as an unshared symmetry under the Baltic weather — was the posture: a commander before a wall of numbers, all green by their makers' lights, choosing which of them to believe.

"Your ledger is honest," Zawadzki said at last, "and your conclusion is a resignation letter dressed as an annex, and I decline to receive it." No heat; the two men had soldiered together too long for heat. "Five days behind — against what resistance? Stefan, cast the *other* reconciliation. His mobile division is still not complete in its sector on Day Eleven; our radio intelligence confirms it; his roads are strangled with his own civilians; last night his artillery declined combat on the northern sector — declined, for the first time, rationing shells like a garrison in a siege. He is not ahead of us. He is failing more slowly than we are, and he has less margin, and the arithmetic of margins is the one column your reconciliation does not carry. The schedule bent when it met his covering force. It will straighten when Second Army's lead divisions pass through — tomorrow night, the night after. Mass repairs timetables. It is the oldest sentence in our profession."

"Mass also eats," said Rylski. "The same roads. The same five days."

"Then it will eat him first." Zawadzki capped his pencil; the conference was ending because he ended it. "The reconciliation goes forward to the theater staff — unaltered, Stefan; I do not file cosmetics — with my assessment attached: lag acknowledged, causes identified, correction expected upon commitment of the second operational echelon. The operation continues on the conventional timetable." The last sentence he said with his hand flat on the map, the old gesture, but Vossen was not there to see how much older it looked on Day Eleven than it had on Day Two: "According to plan."

Kuragin closed his folder without haste. "A very complete reconciliation," he said pleasantly, to the room's ceiling. "I will send my own note northward as well, of course. It is a week for arithmetic everywhere."

Aakjær found the mass on the twelfth day, and the finding went through the headquarters like current.

It came the way the real things came — not as a photograph but as a change in the noise. New bridging tonnage on the Rostock line, moving west. Two new army-level radio nets opening in succession east of Lübeck, disciplined, brief, in the pattern the handbooks assigned to a formation staff displacing forward. Prisoner statements from the northern sector, three of them, agreeing on nothing except a phrase: *the second wave*. And the road and rail flow behind the enemy's screen thickening over forty-eight hours to rates the analysts graded at army scale.

"The second echelon," Vossen said, at the morning conference, and heard the whole plan exhale around the words. Doctrine restored. The missing mass, found at last, marshaling exactly where the reference books had always promised — one army's worth of it, moving to pass through the first echelon's tired divisions and fall on a corps whose own reserve was eleven battalions and a banked third of the daily shell scale.

The staff did what staffs do with a confirmed nightmare: they preferred it to the ambiguity. Estimates snapped into focus. The defense's whole remaining arithmetic reorganized itself within a day around the arriving weight — the rationing deepened, the engineer effort shifted to the depth positions, the air request for the thirteenth was rebuilt, over Kjeldsen's misgivings about the weather, into a maximum deep effort against the approach routes. On the confession wall Roloff opened a new sheet with a heading that was pure doctrine: SECOND ECHELON — PROJECTED COMMITMENT.

Aakjær signed the estimate, because the evidence was the evidence. But at the end of the morning conference he stood a moment over his own board with the expression of a man rechecking a sum that keeps coming out right in a way he dislikes, and what he said he said quietly, to Vossen alone, in the corridor:

"It fits the doctrine perfectly. That is what disturbs me. For eleven days nothing this enemy has done fit the doctrine — the thin first echelon, the slow heartbeat, the gentle fires. Now, at last, one piece arrives that fits, and we have reassembled the entire cathedral around it in a morning, because we have missed the cathedral so much." He looked down the corridor, toward the operations room and its comfortable, dreadful new certainty. "Probably it is an army, and probably it is coming here, and probably my board and your wall and the General's calendar are at last all reading the same war. I will brief it so. But do me one courtesy, Hr. Oberstleutnant. Keep the other ledger open — his calendar, the days behind. Because there is a second reading of this movement, and it will not leave me alone. A man five days late does not always send his mass to *widen* the battle." The gray eyes came around. "Sometimes he sends it to *finish* the argument with his own schedule. Those are different deployments, Georg. They point at different places. And we have built tomorrow's air program as if we knew which one we are watching."
