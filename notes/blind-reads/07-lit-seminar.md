# Blind Read 07 — The Lit Seminar

*Instrument: Prof. of literature, small liberal-arts college. ENGL 3xx,
"Paper Wars: The Bureaucratic Mode in Military Writing." Final text of the
term, after Xenophon (Anabasis), Saint-Exupéry (Night Flight), Wouk (The
Caine Mutiny), Forester (The Good Shepherd), Heller (Catch-22). Course
questions all term: what happens to narrative when institutions do the
acting; where does moral weight sit when decisions are procedures; what is
the ledger/list/log as a literary form.*

*No outside context on the book or its makers was sought. Read cold, front
matter through cease-fire, plus the two map plates.*

---

## 1. SYLLABUS PLACEMENT

**It goes last, and it isn't close.** Not because it's the best book on the
list — it isn't; Heller and Wouk are better *novels* — but because it is the
only text that makes the course's three questions its *plot* rather than its
byproduct. Everything before it does the bureaucratic mode incidentally.
This one does it on purpose, to the hilt, and knows it is doing it. Put last,
it reads the whole syllabus backward: after *The Mission* you cannot reread
Xenophon's parasang-counts, Krause's tonnage ledger, or Yossarian's
ever-rising mission quota as neutral bookkeeping again. It retroactively
turns the semester into one argument. That is what a capstone is for.

There's a real case for *not* last, and I'll put it on the table for the
students to knock down: the book is thin where the canon is thick (people,
bodies, comedy, the music of a long sentence), and ending on your thinnest
characters is a risk. My answer is that the course isn't about character;
it's about what survives when character is subtracted, and this book is the
controlled experiment. Last.

**The two it converses with most:**

- **Forester, *The Good Shepherd*.** The nearest cousin by a wide margin —
  war as a procedural grind, attrition kept as arithmetic, an exhausted mind
  pricing decision against decision. But the difference is the whole lesson.
  Forester locks us inside *one skull* (Krause on the bridge, doing the sums
  himself, alone, for days). *The Mission* takes the same material and
  distributes it across an institution's nervous system — the decision that
  would be one captain's agony is here spread over a G-3, a G-4, a G-2, a
  commander, a wall of plywood. Forester's ledger lives in a man's head;
  this book's ledger is a *document on a cork board with a night shift that
  reads it.* Same genre, opposite location of the mind.

- **Saint-Exupéry, *Night Flight*.** The deeper argument. Rivière is the
  manager who must be hard, who *conceals* the human cost so the institution
  keeps its nerve — the mail must fly, and the men who die for the schedule
  die inside Rivière's necessary silence. Hvidt is the anti-Rivière: his
  entire method is to *post the cost on the wall where every briefer walks
  past it.* "Candor before comfort" is a direct rebuttal to Rivière's
  doctrine of protective concealment. *The Mission* is *Night Flight*
  rewritten by a man who thinks Rivière's silence was the sin, not the duty.
  It is the most *quarrelsome* pairing on the list.

**What it does that none of the five canon texts do:** it makes the
**ledger itself the protagonist** — and, crucially, *the moral hero.* In
Heller the list (the mission count) is an instrument of absurd cruelty; in
Forester it is a burden endured; in Xenophon it is a record kept; in Wouk
"the book" is the thing that both saves and damns. In none of them is the
log *elevated to an ethic.* Here the book's actual thesis — stated flat on
page after page and finally in the after-action report — is that honest
bookkeeping is the highest moral act available to an institution: *"We were
not right. We were solvent."* No other book on the syllabus would dare
propose that solvency is a moral category, or build a plot to prove it.
Two further firsts: it gives the *enemy the identical ledger* (the
beech-wood chapters mirror Rendsburg staff-cell for staff-cell), so the
epistemology is symmetrical across the Iron Curtain; and its front matter
makes the book's *own production method* — an attribution ledger of who did
what — rhyme exactly with its theme. That last move is either the most
integrated thing on the syllabus or a hall of mirrors, and the
undecidability is teachable.

**What the canon does that it doesn't:** people. Bodies. Laughter. Yossarian,
Queeg, Willie Keith, Xenophon-the-narrator, even Rivière are *human beings*;
Holt is, deliberately and almost programmatically, an *operations officer*
more than a man — and everyone around him is a *function* who speaks in the
same burnished aphorism. The book has exactly one chapter with a suitcase, a
grandfather, and a body in it (the Lohses, Ch. 7), and it seems faintly
embarrassed by it. Forester gives you a man's feet and his failing bladder;
Heller gives you Snowden's secret spilling out of a flak wound. *The
Mission* keeps the body at arm's length on purpose — that abstraction is its
subject — but it pays for the thesis in viscerality, and it only settles the
bill once, in the last chapter, with a boy carrying a rifle. Also missing:
Xenophon's genuine deliberative democracy (the army *voting*), and any plot
reversal driven by a *person* rather than a *number*.

## 2. THE SEMINAR PLAN

### Session A — "The Two-Times Rule: the ledger as literary form"

**Concept to hang it on:** *parataxis and the catalog* — the "and…and…and"
of the list as an anti-narrative form, and its moral function. (Bridge back
to Homeric catalog and Xenophon's parasang-tally; forward to the confession
wall as a *narrated* catalog.) Sub-concept: **the found document / ekphrasis
of the form** — scenes that end not on a gesture but on a filed piece of
paper.

**Handout passages (photocopy exactly):**

1. **Ch. 1, Brauer's fourth-cost question.** From *"'Eleven hundred tons of
   ammunition, Herr General,' Holt said…'"* through *"'I would like to know
   what forty minutes of massed fires cost the corps at Esbjerg, in
   hours.'"* — the birth of the book's one operation: the wedge driven
   between *bought* and *cost*. Six lines, and the whole novel is in them.

2. **Ch. 5, building "the page."** From *"The left column was easy, and that
   discovery was itself the finding"* through Aakjær taking the pen for
   *"TIME PURCHASED AGAINST THE ARRIVAL OF THE ENEMY'S REAL MASS — VALUE
   UNKNOWN, SINCE WE HAVE NOT LOCATED IT"* and *"That line is the page,
   gentlemen. The other entries are housekeeping."* This is the ledger being
   physically born on taped graph paper — the book showing its own form
   getting invented in-scene.

3. **Ch. 10, die Beichtwand.** The three "fates" (*ESTIMATE… ACTUAL: 41.*)
   and Aakjær's line: *"It is the only document in the headquarters that
   grows more honest as it grows longer. Everything else we produce grows
   more polished."*

**Discussion questions:**
- If the narrative doesn't "happen" inside a person, where does it happen?
  Track *one* decision — the deferred ferry requisition (Ch. 2) — and name
  every agent. Who acted? (Answer that troubles them: a *minute* acted.)
- The two-times rule — scheduled time *and* actual time on every line — is
  it a formal device or an ethical one? What does the *format itself* forbid
  the characters from doing? (It forbids the comfortable elision. The form
  has a conscience the people can borrow.)
- Compare the confession wall to Xenophon's parasangs and Yossarian's
  mission count. When is a list a *reassurance* and when is it an
  *accusation*? What flips it?
- "We were not right. We were solvent." Is solvency a moral category, or a
  way of ducking the moral one?

### Session B — "The author as audit trail, and the body at the bottom of the ledger"

**Concept to hang it on:** **Barthes, "The Death of the Author"** (they read
it week 2) — pushed until it breaks against the ethics of abstraction. The
front-matter disclosure is a text *about* authorship; read it as one.

**Handout passages:**

1. **Front matter, "How this book was made."** From *"kept books on their
   own errors, which survive in the record"* through *"Readers who hold that
   a book's provenance is part of its meaning are invited to weigh this one
   accordingly… the ledger, not the byline, is the honest unit of account."*

2. **Ch. 18, the meter read aloud + "why this is not gambling."** The
   half-hour casualty readings — *"It was the half minute the staff owed.
   The line was paying by the hour for the corps' patience, and the
   operations room could at least stand still every thirty minutes and hear
   the price in its own voice"* — paired with Hvidt's demand, *"State for the
   record, Herr Oberstløjtnant, why this is not gambling,"* and Holt's
   answer: *"the wall does not remove the wager, it tells us which wager we
   are making and charges us honestly for it, and the charge is posted at
   eighty to a hundred and forty an hour."*

3. **Ch. 19, the boy with the rifle.** *"A boy of perhaps nineteen went by
   carrying a wounded man's rifle as tenderly as luggage… These were the
   other staff's numbers. Someone in a beech wood had audited this boy at
   three-fifths. // He was not, then or ever again, tempted to love the
   instrument."*

**Discussion questions:**
- The disclosure claims *the ledger, not the byline* is the unit of account.
  Read against Barthes: the book announces that a machine drafted it and a
  man edited it and a ledger records every session. Is that the death of the
  author — or the author's *bureaucratic resurrection* as an audit trail?
  Complicate: the book's *theme* (honest bookkeeping of a distributed
  process) is *identical* to its stated *method of production.* Integrity, or
  a machine flattering its own procedure?
- The casualty meter: does reading the number aloud every thirty minutes
  *honor* the dead or *launder* them? Can a format do both at once?
- The boy audited "at three-fifths": the whole book runs on that
  abstraction. Is the *novel* tempted to love the instrument? Find the two
  or three places where it disciplines itself — and decide whether that's
  honesty or an alibi.

## 3. THE SEMINAR, IMAGINED

### Session A

It opens slow, the way the parataxis sessions always do, because parataxis
looks like *nothing is happening.* One of the undecided sophomores says the
first forty pages are "a meeting," and he's not wrong, and I let it sit,
because the whole session is going to be about turning that complaint into
the thesis.

The **history double-major** cracks it open, as she has all term, by pulling
the Xenophon handout back out unasked and laying the parasang-count beside
the two-times rule. "It's the same gesture," she says. "You count the stages
so you can *survive* them. Xenophon counts distance because distance is the
one thing an army in hostile country can actually know. Holt's people count
scheduled-versus-actual because the schedule is the one thing they can't
trust, so they make the *gap* the thing they know." That's the session's
spine and she's handed it to me in four minutes. I make her pay for it: *and
what does the form forbid?* She gets there — the format won't let them leave
a space; if the actual time doesn't exist yet, the report has to say so *in
words.* "The form is more honest than the men would be." Good. Written on
the board.

The **creative-writing three** run the technique conversation, and this is
where the room gets loud and useful.

*What they'd steal:*
- **Ending scenes on a document.** One of them — the one who writes tight,
  affectless minimalism — is electrified by how many scenes close not on a
  line of dialogue or a gesture but on *a filed piece of paper*: the ferry
  minute "quietly compounding from that day at rates the file could not
  print"; the two bridge-messages going up on the wall side by side; the
  Danish captain's Friedrichstadt log and Holt's file card, *THIS IS THE
  CORPS DECIDING WHAT IT IS FOR. TWICE BEFORE NOON.* "The document *is* the
  emotional beat. He found a way to make a form feel like a held breath."
  She wants to steal it wholesale.
- **The "both correct" move.** Loheide: *"Both are correct, Herr General. I
  have done the staff work both ways and I certify it."* The CW student who
  writes plotty genre stuff loves that the book generates suspense not from
  *which answer is right* but from *which of two correct answers commands the
  other.* "That's a dramatic engine I've never seen. Nobody's wrong and it's
  still unbearable."
- **The free-indirect staff voice** — the way the third person soaks up the
  headquarters' own idiom (*Endstand, frokost, green against what, the
  bottle pours*) so the institution seems to narrate itself.

*What they'd flag as workshop-able tics* (and they are merciless, correctly):
- **One metaphor, run into the ground.** *Everything* is priced, costed,
  banked, spent, a currency, on credit, at interest, an invoice, a ledger.
  The tightest-eared CW student counts the money-words on one photocopied
  page and reads the tally out like a prosecutor. "It's the book's whole
  idea, so I get it, but after chapter ten the metaphor stops *doing* work
  and starts *being* the work. It's a tic wearing a thesis." I can't defend
  it and don't try; I bank it (their laugh) for the paper topics.
- **Everyone is equally, identically wise.** Brauer, Hvidt, Rahn, Aakjær,
  Lammers, Witzel, the Danish captain, the *elderly Oberstleutnant of
  reserves*, the battalion commander who banks in Kolding — *every single
  one* delivers a burnished, final, epigrammatic sentence, in the same
  register. "It's fifteen mouths and one voice," the minimalist says. "The
  voice is great. That's the problem. Nobody in this book is dumb, or
  boring, or wrong in a *stupid* way." True, and damning, and it's the
  thread that will run into the second session.
- **The recurring "he heard the answer arrive late" body-tic** — the shape
  of the number arriving "as heat along his cheekbones," repeated at Esbjerg
  and again at the bridge. One student flags it as the author's single
  physiological tell, deployed twice because it worked once.

The **econ senior**, who signed up for a distribution requirement and has
been quiet all term, is not quiet today, because somewhere around the "page"
handout he has realized *this is his major.* He starts naming things the
book won't name: the right-hand column is **opportunity cost**; Rahn's "name
the one good such that if it went to zero every other line is worthless" is
**the binding constraint / shadow price**; Aakjær's *"Constraints are not
defeated. They move. You relieve one and the next steps forward"* is —
he actually says it — "that's the Theory of Constraints, that's a *factory*
book." (He is more right than he knows; the ball-bearing line in Ch. 5 is
the book waving at him.) He gets the room to see the crossing-valve chapter
(Ch. 8) as pure **queueing theory** — "the bridge stands empty eleven
seconds in thirteen and every gap is somebody's *compliance*, that's a
utilization problem, that's *literally* my ops-management midterm" — and for
about ten minutes the English majors watch an economics student read a war
novel better than they can, which is exactly the productive humiliation the
last week of a course should contain. I let it run.

Where Session A **splits:** the theory-inclined pair vs. the econ senior and
the history major. The theory students start pushing that the accounting
language is *ideology* — that pricing lives as "days added to his
preparation" is precisely how a bureaucracy *makes killing sayable*, that
the ledger is not honest, it's anesthetic, and the book is complicit. The
econ senior takes it personally and well: "The alternative isn't *not*
pricing it. The alternative is pricing it *badly* and calling it duty —
that's *Night Flight*, that's Rivière. This book prices it *and posts the
price.* That's not anesthesia, that's the opposite." That is the real fight,
and it's the right one to walk into Session B unresolved.

### Session B

The **theory student** — the one who has wanted to talk about the front
matter since I handed out the syllabus — leads, and I let it get as
complicated as it wants, because it should.

His opening is sharp: the disclosure note is a *Barthesian trap.* "Barthes
says kill the Author, the origin, the guarantor of meaning — the text is a
tissue of citations with no father. And this book walks up and says: *fine,*
the father is a machine, the byline is a fiction, *here is the ledger
instead.* It's not the death of the author. It's the author reincarnated as
an *audit trail.* They replaced the Author-God with an Author-*Accountant.*"
The room likes it. Then the second theory student turns it, and this is the
good part: "But that's the *most authored* gesture imaginable. A text truly
without an author doesn't hand you a provenance appendix. This one is
*obsessed* with its own origin — it's the least Barthesian book on the
syllabus. It protests the death of the author by *itemizing* the author."

And then the move that makes the session: someone connects the front matter
to the *content.* The book's whole ethic is *the confession wall* — post
your estimates, keep books on your own errors, let the F-series carry your
initials. And the front matter says the makers *"kept books on their own
errors, which survive in the record."* The production method *is* the theme.
The theory student calls it "the cleanest closed loop I've ever seen — a book
about honest bookkeeping, that keeps an honest ledger of *itself*, written by
a process that keeps an honest ledger of *its own drafting.*" And the second
one, sourly, brilliantly: "which means we can't tell whether it's *integrity*
or whether it's a machine that has been *optimized to look like integrity*,
and the book knows we can't tell, and that's either the point or the con."
Nobody resolves it. I don't resolve it. That undecidability is the most
contemporary thing on the syllabus and I say so.

The **nursing student** has been quiet through the theory jousting, and when
she comes in she changes the temperature of the room. She was assigned the
casualty-meter passage and the boy-with-the-rifle, and she is not interested
in whether the ledger is Barthesian. She is interested in *eighty to a
hundred and forty an hour.* "In a code, in a hospital, you call the time. Out
loud. Time of death, out loud, so it's real, so somebody has to *hear* it.
That's what the half-hour reading *is.* He even says it — reading it aloud
*informs nobody*, the number's already on the wall, it's *the half minute the
staff owed.*" She has found, unprompted, the one place the book's abstraction
becomes a rite of witness rather than an evasion, and she defends it against
the theory table's "anesthetic" charge with the authority of someone who has
watched people call a time. Then she turns on the same book. The boy with the
rifle — *"someone in a beech wood had audited this boy at three-fifths"* —
"That line is the only time the book lets a number and a body be in the same
sentence *and admit it.* One time. In four hundred pages of pricing, *one*
boy gets to be a person and a percentage at once, and the sentence right
after is the whole novel apologizing: *he was not, then or ever again,
tempted to love the instrument.* Why only once? Where were the bodies the
other twenty-three days?" The room goes quiet, because she has asked the
question the CW students were circling in Session A — the thinness — but
asked it *morally* instead of *technically.*

The **creative-writing** thread and the **history** thread converge on her
question. The history major supplies the context the book *assumes*: this is
November 1983, the reader is expected to know the airliner (KAL 007, "the
transcripts of the fighter pilot's radio calls"), the half-million marching
in Bonn, the December missile deployment (Euromissiles/Pershing), the Danish
"footnote" reservations threaded into the special-weapons folder, BALTAP,
Warsaw Pact operational norms, the *Danevirke* as a thousand-year earthwork
that faced south — *and* the Able Archer near-miss the book never names but
sits inside. "It assumes you bring a decade of Cold War with you," she says.
"For me that's a feature. For half this table it's a locked door." (The
non-majors nod, a little wounded; two of them admit the beech-wood chapters
read as a foreign-language exam.) And the CW minimalist lands the plane:
"The reason there's only one body is that the book made a bet — that if it
kept faith with the *form*, the form would eventually *deliver* a body, and
it would hit harder for being rationed. The boy is the payoff of three weeks
of withholding. It's the same bet as the whole war: bank everything, spend
it once, at the one hour it counts." Which is either a defense or an
indictment and is, I think, both.

**The unthought-of comparison.** I had prepped Forester and Saint-Exupéry as
the two conversations. What I hadn't seen coming: one of the creative-writing
students — reading the last chapter's fight between Holt and Bjelke over the
after-action report — says, quietly, "This is the *Caine* court-martial." And
she's right, and I hadn't thought of it. Bjelke arrives with the theater's
draft report, courteous, undefeated, proposing that the error annex be
"housed" in a supplement, that "failures" be restyled "assessment
revisions," that the record say *the system worked.* That is Greenwald's
courtroom run in reverse — the fight over *who writes the official version of
what the institution did*, and whether "the system worked" is the comfortable
lie that buries the men who paid. Only there's no villain: Bjelke isn't
Queeg, he's *reasonableness itself*, "outlasted, not defeated." She's found
that *The Mission* relitigates *The Caine Mutiny*'s central anxiety — the
gap between the record and the truth — as a bloodless editorial dispute, and
that the *absence* of a villain makes it worse, because it means the
laundering is nobody's fault, it's just what records *do.* I gave her the
rest of the class's discussion time. It's the best reading anyone produced
all term and I'd have missed it.

**The quiet-student moment.** Every seminar has one. Ours came from one of
the undecided sophomores — the one who called the opening "a meeting" — near
the end, after the theory table had argued the ledger to a standstill. She
said: "The course kept asking where the moral weight sits when decisions are
procedures. I think this book's answer is that it sits *in the form.* Not in
a person. They gave the conscience to a piece of plywood because none of the
people could carry it." And she stopped there.

That's ninety percent of the thing the whole term was building toward, and
she got there on her own, and I had a choice: close it or leave it. I closed
it, because the last clause is the one that hurts and she'd earned hearing
it: *and the book knows that's a defeat, not a triumph.* A conscience that
has to be exiled to a wall of graph paper is a confession that the humans
failed to hold it — that's exactly why the boy with the rifle is in the book
at all, and exactly why the people are so thin. The plywood conscience isn't
the book's proud answer. It's the book's grief. The wall exists because the
men couldn't be trusted to remember what things cost, and *"nothing on this
wall priced his consent"* — the book's own last confession — is the wall
admitting the one thing it was never able to weigh. She wrote that down. So
did I.

## 4. AFTERWARD

### Three paper topics students actually proposed (private ranking)

1. **"The Women in the Funnel: outsourced interiority in a book with no
   inside."** *(CW student. My private #1.)* The novel exiles nearly all
   feeling, body, and memory to its women and civilians — Merete pricing the
   whole war into a question about which grandmother; Frau Lohse's father who
   walked from Stolp in '45 ("*Weight is time*") repacking the car in
   silence; the mother-in-law rotating her bathwater eleven minutes from a
   named landing beach; the buried tin with the note for the year 2000; the
   schoolroom blackboard with the unwiped homework for the ninth of November.
   The one chapter (7) with a suitcase and a grave is the book's only
   *interior*, and it belongs to people the staff will never meet. Thesis
   available: the bureaucratic mode can only *afford* interiority by
   outsourcing it to the ledger's objects. This is the paper that *fights the
   book*, which is why it's the best.

2. **"The moving constraint: Theory of Constraints as plot engine."** *(Econ
   senior. My #2.)* Argues the novel's structure isn't rising action, it's
   the *migration of the binding constraint* — plank-seconds, then rounds,
   then fuel, then the enemy's dumps, then *the sea's schedule* — and that
   Rahn's Socratic "name the one good" is a shadow-price derivation dressed
   as philosophy. Genuinely fresh; brings a vocabulary the English majors
   can't, and takes the book at its own word about what it is. Risk: he
   flattens the prose into a case study. Coachable.

3. **"The author as audit trail: the disclosure note against Barthes."**
   *(Theory student. My #3 — highest ceiling, real floor risk.)* The best
   possible version connects the front matter's provenance-ledger to the
   confession wall and asks whether a self-auditing text can be read for
   sincerity at all. The likely version is a sophomore hall-of-mirrors that
   mistakes *naming* the paradox for *resolving* it. I'd greenlight it only
   with a hard requirement: no claim about the machine's "intent"; stay in
   the text.

*The one I'd steer someone off:* "Solvent, not right: bookkeeping as ethics."
It sounds like the obvious A-paper and it's a trap — the book already *wrote*
that essay, verbatim, in its own after-action report ("*We were not right. We
were solvent… The discipline of posting them is the only thing this
headquarters proved. It was enough.*"). There's nothing left to add; you'd
be transcribing the thesis, not testing it. I'd send that student to topic 1
and tell them to make the book's confidence *earn* the word "enough."

### The handout that worked / the one that fell flat

**Worked best:** the two bridge-messages in Ch. 8 — *"NOTE THAT A BRIDGE HAS
NEVER COMMANDED A BRIGADE"* / *"NOTE TAKEN. ALSO THAT A BRIDGE HAS NEVER BEEN
A BRIDGE."* I very nearly cut it as minor. It over-performed: it's the one
place the book's austerity *smiles*, and it opened the whole conversation
about wit being the novel's only permitted human warmth — dryness as the
last channel feeling is allowed to use in a headquarters. The room warmed to
the book *through the joke* and argued harder about everything else because
of it. (Runner-up that also landed: the Friedrichstadt captain's log with
Holt's file card — thesis-in-a-found-document, a human at a clipboard
inventing a currency because the traffic wouldn't wait for doctrine.)

**Fell flat:** Kuragin's nuclear-folder refusal (Ch. 17), which I'd bet the
term would be the moral climax and which the room found *abstract and
foregone.* Zawadzki is too noble, the scene does its philosophy out loud
("*the folder confesses its failure, with fire, in the one theater where
every government has spent twenty years rehearsing what our fire means*"),
and the students smelled a set-piece: an enemy commander built to be
admired, making the choice the book needs him to make, narrated in the same
sealed epigrams as everyone else. It's the book's "PowerPoint-in-prose"
moment — the didactic seam showing. The nursing student's flat verdict: "He
was never going to press the button. There's no scene here. There's a
position paper with a greatcoat on."

### Does it hold the table next to the canon?

**Where it's their peer:** as an answer to *this course's actual question* it
is the strongest text on the list. It out-Foresters Forester on the
arithmetic of attrition and out-argues *Night Flight* on the ethics of the
schedule, because it does deliberately, and examines, what they do by
instinct. On "the ledger as literary form," it isn't a peer — it's the
seminar's summit; the others become its footnotes.

**Where it's thinner:** character, body, comedy, and stamina of the
sentence. Fifteen mouths and one gorgeous voice; a metaphor system that
calcifies into a tic; women and civilians doing the emotional labor the
officers are too abstract to perform; a single human chapter the book seems
half-ashamed of. Next to Queeg's strawberries or Snowden's wound, its
suffering is *tabulated* rather than *felt*, on purpose, at real cost. A
whole novel of aphorism fatigues the way a whole meal of salt does.

**Where it does something none of them attempt:** it makes the audit the
ethical protagonist and dares to call solvency a virtue; it hands the enemy
the identical instrument, so the book's subject is finally *bureaucratic
epistemology as such*, not one army's; and its front matter folds the book's
production into its theme so tightly that "is this sincere?" becomes
unanswerable *by design* — the most twenty-first-century problem on a
syllabus that stops, otherwise, in 1961.

### Keep or drop?

**Keep. And keep it last.** With two adjustments: teach the front matter and
