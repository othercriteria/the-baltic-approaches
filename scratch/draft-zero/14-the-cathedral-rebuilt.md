# 14. The Cathedral, Rebuilt

Aakjær called the staff together on the fifteenth morning and began with a sentence Vossen had never heard an intelligence officer speak aloud:

"My estimate of the enemy has been wrong since the first day of the war, and I can now show you the shape of the error, and the shape is worth more than a correct estimate would have been."

He had been up two nights re-auditing his own board — the confession wall's discipline turned at last fully on its keeper — and what he laid before them was not new information but old information, re-founded. "We have called them the first echelon and the second echelon, because our doctrine of him gave us those words, and the words told us what to expect: the deep mass, marshaling far back, arriving late, to be interdicted on its long approach. Every anomaly of this war — the thin opening, the deliberate heartbeat, the mass that was absent and then present — we have filed against those words. Now file them differently. Suppose there were never echelons in the doctrinal sense at all. Suppose what crossed the border on the eighth was simply the *lead* of a single continuous body — a front, marching to the sea, that has been in motion since before the first shot: its armies not stacked in depth, waiting, but strung along the roads of Mecklenburg in one column of march two hundred kilometers long, arriving as fast as two countries' roads could pour them. Then the 'second echelon' we blunted at the narrows was not doctrine's reserve committed early. It was merely the next army in the column, arriving on schedule — on *his* schedule. And then hear what follows." He struck the map, once, along the rail lines east of Lübeck. "There is no deep mass left to interdict. The depth is empty. Everything he owns on this axis is now west of Rostock, inside our reach, standing at the end of a supply line two hundred kilometers long that must feed it across every bridge we have dropped — and his column has no more armies in it. What arrives now is not force. It is only supply. Gentlemen, for two weeks we have flown deep to make him late. That war is over; he *is* late; it cannot be deepened. From today, deep means something else. Deep means his tonnage."

The silence had a quality Vossen had learned to recognize: the room rebuilding itself around a fact.

Lammers spoke first — Lammers, who had priced the corps' own tonnage down to the day, and who now walked to the enemy's side of the map with the expression of a craftsman inspecting a competitor's ruin. "Then his arithmetic is mine, magnified. My convoys cross forty kilometers under air attack; his cross two hundred. My daily lift feeds a corps standing still; his must feed a front that intends to attack — and an attacking front eats at three times the garrison scale. If the G-2's picture is right, the question is no longer whether we can starve his *battle*. It is how many days of assault-scale supply he can accumulate at the front before his sea closes — because he cannot attack out of the daily flow; nobody can; he must save up." The G-4's finger found the coast road, the rail junctions, moving with the certainty of a man reading his own ledgers in mirror-writing. "And a man saving up is a man visible. Dumps grow. Dumps are photographed. Dumps burn."

"And a man saving up," said Vossen — the piece arriving in his hands with the same click he had felt at the Eider embankment, the abstraction growing hands — "is a man *not spending*. Herr General, if the enemy must bank his tonnage for one great effort inside the next seven to eleven days, then between now and that effort his daily violence must fall. The pause we are watching is not only reorganization. It is *saving*. His artillery will slacken by a schedule we can measure — Roloff, the counter-battery counts, daily, on the wall from this morning — and the slackening itself becomes our instrument: the quieter he gets, the closer he is. His economy drive is our countdown clock. He cannot hide it, because the hiding would cost him the very shells he is hoarding."

Hvidt had stood through all of it at the foot of the map, arms folded, letting the staff run — that had become his method in the second week, to be the room's walls and not its voice — and now he stepped in with the flat carefulness that meant the morning was about to acquire consequences.

"Then draw me the day," he said. "The staff has spent two weeks teaching me that every plan is a schedule and every schedule has a weakest hour. His is now bounded: he must attack within eleven days or the sea forecloses; he cannot attack until his dumps are filled; and from the moment his preparation fires begin, his hoard converts to expenditure at a rate — the G-4 will compute it — that gives his effort a fixed burning time, after which he stands at the end of two hundred kilometers of bombed road with empty dumps, spent divisions, a closed sea, and a plan whose own arithmetic has finished it. There." The commander's hand described a short arc across the map's neck, over the Danewerk, the narrows, the funnel. "That day — the day his last effort culminates — is the only day of this war on which that front will be *weaker* than this corps: not in tanks; in choices. On every day before it, attacking him is spending our remnant against his hoard. On every day after it, there is no *him* to attack; there is a withdrawal and an armistice line and a different history. One day, gentlemen. Perhaps thirty-six hours. The corps will now be built backward from it."

And that — recorded in the war diary as COMMANDER'S PLANNING GUIDANCE, 22 NOV, and on the confession wall by a single new sheet, headed, in Roloff's hand, THE DAY — was the moment the defense of the Baltic Approaches stopped being an endurance and became an operation. The staff work that flowed from it filled the next seventy hours and, condensed to the wall's dialect, said: everything serves the day. The air requests rewrote themselves — no more theology; the morning market would be bid tonnage, his dumps and his coast-road convoys, priced in days-added-to-his-preparation, and close support refused except where the line itself was load-bearing. The corps' own hoarding began again, harder — shells, bridging, the three tired battalion groups pulled out of the line into hides and, in an order Vossen signed with both times identical, rested: sleep itself entered the corps' books as ammunition. The engineer effort turned to the crossing sites on the Schlei and the narrows' shoulders — not to hold them; to *use* them, when the day came, in the other direction.

Late that night, at the wall, Aakjær stood beside Vossen looking at the new sheet, THE DAY, still empty of its date, and delivered his benediction in the flat murmur that the staff had learned was the closest thing the G-2 possessed to hope:

"Three weeks ago the General asked what we were looking at that would look different if it were aimed at us. Now we keep his calendar, his tonnage, and his weather, and we are planning our week around the worst morning of his life." The thin smile. "I observe for the record, Hr. Oberstleutnant, that we have become the thin man across the water. I intend to be better at it than he was."
