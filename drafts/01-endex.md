# 1 — ENDEX

The exercise ended at eleven minutes past two on a Friday
afternoon, in the rain.

ENDEX, said the loudspeaker in the operations room of
Headquarters Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland,
in a Danish voice reading an English word, and then said it
twice more, because the signal instructions required three
transmissions and the staff had spent nine days doing what the
instructions required. Oberstløjtnant Niels Holt stood at the
rail of the gallery and watched a German captain climb onto a
chair and begin to take the war off the map.

The captain worked with a razor blade, north to south, enemy
first, because the enemy was on top. Nine days of chinagraph
came away in curls: the red boundaries of formations that had
never existed, phase lines named for birds — HERON, CURLEW,
BRANT — minefields that had been laid only in the notations of
umpires, demolition states for bridges that stood out there in
the weather, whole and wet and carrying the afternoon's traffic.
Under all of it the map of the Baltic Approaches emerged at
1:250,000, clean, the peninsula hanging down into Germany like
something anatomical, and the captain got down off the chair and
took the curls of acetate away in a wastepaper basket.

Around the room the nine days were being put away with the
same unhurried competence that had spent them. The umpires'
chief, a British lieutenant-colonel on loan from the staff
college, was initialing the last scoring sheets at the map desk;
two Danish signalers were winding cable onto a drum with the
rhythm of men who had wound cable in the rain before; somebody's
loudspeaker, left live on the exercise net, transmitted the
particular silence of forty headquarters simultaneously
stopping. In the gallery the division liaison officers were
packing their map boards. The one from Neumünster stacked his
acetates squarely; the one from the Danish division rolled his.
It said something about the two armies that Holt had decided,
over a year of watching, was not worth saying aloud.

Holt had watched the same undressing at the end of every
exercise for a year. It had never before struck him as a
procedure that ought to have a name.

"Endstand, Herr Oberstløjtnant." Major Roloff, at his elbow,
holding the folder. The corps worked in English and called its
final-state report by a German word, the way it called lunch
frokost if a Dane was carrying the tray. Roloff had the folder
before Holt knew he wanted it; that was Roloff. Holt did not
open it. He had written half of it, and the half he had not
written, Roloff had, in a hand so level the typists complained
there was nothing to correct.

"The numbers look fine," Holt said.

"Yes, sir," said Roloff, in a voice with nothing in it either
way.

---

At 1500 the commander came down for the internal wash-up, which
was held in the operations room so that the staff could brief
against the boards it had fought on. Generalleutnant Brauer had
commanded the corps for thirty-four months and would hand it
over in eleven days; the moving crates were already in the
corridor of the commander's villa, and everyone knew it, and no
one said so. He sat in the front row of folding chairs with his
hands on his knees while Roloff ran the map with a pointer and
the assessment was read out, day by day, in the order the days
had been won.

It was a good story. Holt had built it and knew exactly how
good. The covering force had imposed delay sixty percent beyond
the planning norm. The demolition belts had fired on their
staircase, each line buying the next. The Danish division had
married up and closed south into Schleswig by ninety hours,
which was the number in the plan. On D+5 the corps had massed
the fires of two divisions for forty minutes at the Hohn gap and
the umpires had assessed an ORANGE regiment rendered
ineffective. On D+6 the line had held on CURLEW, and the
scenario had ended there, holding, with seventeen percent
friendly attrition against forty-one percent of the ORANGE first
echelon, and the word that ended it was not victory, because the
scoring had no such column, but standard achieved.

Brauer let the assessment finish. Then he asked four questions.

He asked Oberst Lammers whether the demolition targets in the
second belt had been resurveyed since the spring floods or
whether the charges were computed against the autumn's water
table. Lammers said the second belt had been resurveyed in
September, forty of forty-one targets, and named the one that
had not, and why, and what it would cost to do it in November.
Brauer nodded and wrote nothing down.

He asked Roloff what the delay figure assumed about the enemy's
own timetable. Roloff looked at the question for a moment the
way he looked at a column of figures that did not foot.

"It assumes the timetable in the exercise instructions, Herr
General. ORANGE attacked on schedule each morning. The delay we
imposed is measured against the advance he was scripted to
make."

"So the sixty percent is a measurement of our performance
against his punctuality."

"Yes, Herr General."

"And the air," Brauer said, not quite a question, to the air
liaison desk. Major Kjeldsen stood up with the sortie summary
already open, because Kjeldsen translated everyone's questions
into his own trade before answering them and had learned to
begin early.

"Two hundred and twelve offensive support sorties flown across
the nine days, Herr General, of two hundred and forty
allocated. The difference is weather. Of the two hundred and
twelve, the umpires scored effect for roughly two-thirds."

"And the missing third?"

"Flew, dropped, and was scored no effect. In the exercise that
is a number in a column, Herr General. In the autumn we would
have flown them over real gun positions, so I am obliged to say
I do not know what the missing third costs, only that we bought
it at full price." Kjeldsen sat down. Brauer looked at him a
half-second longer than he had looked at anyone, and did not
follow up, which in Brauer was a form of bookmark.

Then he turned to Holt, and Holt, who had briefed generals for
a year from that spot of floor and thought he knew every
question that could come out of the front row, got the third
question.

"The forty minutes at Hohn," Brauer said. "The massed fires on
D+5. What did they cost?"

"Eleven hundred tons of ammunition, Herr General," Holt said.
"Roughly the corps' artillery holdings for two days at sustained
rates. The assessment is that it destroyed a regiment."

"That is what it bought. I asked what it cost."

"The ammunition expenditure —"

"The ammunition was at Hohn because trucks carried it there,"
Brauer said, without heat, the way a man states the day of the
week. "The trucks were on roads. For those days the same roads
were carrying the Danish division south and, from D+5, the
convoys inland from Esbjerg. The ships at Esbjerg began
unloading on D+5 at noon. I would like to know what forty
minutes of massed fires cost the corps at Esbjerg, in hours."

Holt stood with the pointer in his hand and heard the answer
arrive — not the number, the shape of where the number would
have to come from, movement tables against ammunition returns
against port throughput, three staffs' paper that had never been
laid side by side — and heard it arrive late, and felt the
lateness as heat along his cheekbones. The room was quiet. The
scoring had no column for the question either; he understood
that this was the question's point.

"The exercise did not price it, Herr General," he said. "I did
not price it. I can have an estimate by Monday."

"Monday I will be packing," Brauer said, and there was the
smallest movement at his mouth, not unkind. "Give it to my
successor. He is Danish; I am told the Danes are good with
money." A ripple went along the folding chairs, Danish laughter
first. Brauer stood.

He asked his fourth question standing, of nobody in particular.
"The division closed by ninety hours. Whose clock says so?"

"The exercise clock, Herr General," said Roloff, when nobody
else moved.

"Yes," Brauer said. "The one we wind ourselves." He looked at
the boards a moment, seventeen percent, forty-one percent, the
green columns of things done as scheduled. "Real enemies are
less punctual. Note it in the record: the corps performed to
standard, and the standard was ours, and I have signed worse
truths in my time. Thank you, gentlemen. ENDEX."

---

The reception was held in the mess, which had been the riding
hall of a cavalry regiment when the garrison was imperial, and
still had the high brown roof-beams and the smell, on wet days,
of a century of horses somewhere under the floor wax. Holt
carried a beer he did not want past the long buffet and heard,
behind the herring, the G-2 and the G-4 conducting one of their
border skirmishes.

"— all I observe," Oberstløjtnant Aakjær was saying, "is that
for nine days the most heavily armed sea in the world lay forty
kilometers to our right, and nothing came across it, and nothing
was scripted to come across it. A quiet Baltic. It is a
remarkable assumption to find lying in the middle of a corps
exercise, like a sleeping dog no one names."

"It is not an assumption, it is a boundary," Lammers said. "The
sea belongs to the navies. The exercise instructions—"

"The instructions, yes. I have read them. Scenario paragraph
three: maritime situation, stable." Aakjær speared something
small and silver. "In 1943 I believe the maritime situation was
also stable, on the first of the month."

"You were four years old in 1943."

"I was five, and it was stable, and then it was not." Aakjær
said it lightly, the way he said most things. "I am not asking
the exercise to fight the navy's war, Dieter. I am asking who
decided our sea was furniture. Somebody wrote that paragraph.
Paragraphs have authors. When I find him I will buy him a
drink and ask him what he knows that I don't, and I am very
much hoping he has an answer, because the alternative is that
nobody decided it at all."

"The alternative," Lammers said, "is that it is a corps
exercise, and a corps has boundaries, and behind every boundary
there is supposed to be somebody else."

"Supposed," said Aakjær. "There is the sleeping dog's actual
name." And he moved off with his plate, leaving the last word
behind him the way other men leave a coat over a chair, meaning
to come back for it.

Holt put his beer down unfinished. At the far end of the hall,
under the photographs of exercises past, the corps was keeping a
respectful half-circle of empty parquet around a very old man in
a gray civilian suit.

---

General a.D. Anselm Rahn was eighty-two years old and stood the
way he had presumably stood at forty, weight a little forward,
hands folded behind him, examining the framed photograph of the
1978 command post exercise as though it were a prisoner's map
found on a body. The Bundeswehr sent him invitations because his
name was on a founding document somewhere; the Americans, it was
said, sent him airline tickets. He lived forty minutes away, in
Eckernförde, with the Baltic at the end of his street.

Sixteen years earlier, for one academic year, Holt had sat in a
lecture hall in Hamburg-Blankenese as the allied student in a
room of German majors, and this man had walked in without notes
and taught an elective called Operative Probleme in historischer
Beleuchtung, and Holt had understood perhaps two-thirds of it at
the time and had spent parts of every year since understanding a
little more of the remainder.

"Herr Oberstløjtnant Holt," Rahn said, in English, without
turning around. The courtesy of the language went in both
directions: the old man declining to make the Dane work in
German, the Dane declining to notice the declining. Neither of
them had ever named it, in sixteen years. "They have hung the
1978 exercise beside the 1974. Both frames the same size. That
is either modesty or a warning, and the mess officer will not
know which."

"Herr General. I didn't know you had come."

"I am on a list. When the list is read out, I appear; it is very
like the reserve." He turned. The face was creased like the
Schlei on the map, the eyes pale and entirely awake. "I read
your assessment this afternoon. Brauer had it sent to me — he
does that, since I refuse the telephone for anything serious. My
compliments. It was correct throughout."

"Thank you, Herr General."

"That was not praise. I have one question, if the corps can
spare its operations officer for five minutes at a party. You
may consider it an old man's examination; there is no mark."
Rahn clasped his hands again. "Your corps has spent nine days
and, on paper, a great many lives and eleven hundred tons of
ammunition. What was it for?"

"The exercise objective—"

"I have read the exercise objective; I am asking you a smaller
question and you are answering a larger one. The corps, Holt.
Those nine days. Name the single thing it existed, those nine
days, to produce."

"To hold the line CURLEW," Holt said, "until—"

He stopped, because Rahn had raised one finger, not to
interrupt but as a man marks the exact place in a text.

"Until," Rahn said. "Go on. Finish the sentence."

"Until the situation is restored by the arrival of external
reinforcement, or until directed otherwise."

"That sentence has three endings and you have given me two of
them and there is a third nobody briefs. Which of the three did
your exercise rehearse?" Holt was silent. "None. It ended on
D+6, holding, which is to say it ended before the sentence did.
Try again. What does the corps produce? Not do. Produce."

"Time," Holt said slowly. "It buys time."

"Buys. Good, a commercial word, we are getting somewhere. Buys
it for whom? At what rate? Where is it delivered, this time, and
who signs for the delivery?"

"Then the Approaches," Holt said. "The corps exists to keep the
Baltic Approaches closed. That is in the founding directive; it
is on the wall of the briefing room."

"It is a fine sentence and it belongs to the fleet," Rahn said.
"Ships close straits; minefields close straits. Your corps
holds a peninsula so that other men may close a sea. Now you
have told me what the corps is for by telling me what two other
organizations are for, which is what every headquarters does
when this question is asked, which is why I no longer ask it of
headquarters." The old man's voice did not rise; it narrowed.
"Three answers, Holt, and each one larger and emptier than the
last, like rooms in a house nobody lives in. You do not know. I
do not know either, and I had forty years to ask, which is why
I permit myself to be tiresome at receptions. Your exercise
proved the corps can do what it rehearsed. It always goes well.
Nobody rehearses the question."

Across the hall somebody laughed at something; the herring went
round; two colonels altered course to pass the half-circle of
parquet without entering it. Holt heard himself say: "What is
the question, Herr General?"

"You have just asked it," Rahn said. "What is this corps for —
one answer, one sentence, and no until in it. When you have
that, come to Eckernförde and tell me, and I will give you
coffee and tell you whether I believe you. Do not come before.
My wife kept the coffee for occasions; I keep the habit." He
turned back to the photograph of 1978, which was a dismissal,
and then said, to the glass, "My compliments on the assessment,
Herr Oberstløjtnant. It was correct throughout."

---

Holt went back through the rain to the headquarters and stood a
while in the empty operations room, where the cleaners were not
allowed and the night shift had not yet come on. The map was
clean. The Approaches lay under the lamps without a single
mark on them: the peninsula, the two seas, the canal across the
neck like a surgical scar, the towns in their quiet type —
Rendsburg, Schleswig, Flensburg, and away in the top corner, on
the far side of two waters, Fredericia, where at this hour Merete
would be getting the children to bed with the radio on.

To hold. To delay. To buy. Every verb the corps owned went
forward into the same small word and stopped there, the way the
traffic of a whole province goes down onto one bridge.

Until.

He turned off the map lamps and went home to his quarters, and
the word went with him, lodged under the sternum like a
fishbone, and it was still there in the morning.
