# 2. Change of Command

The new commander's English was better than the old one's, and the staff distrusted it for a week.

Generalløjtnant Anders Hvidt took command of Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland on the twenty-fifth of October, on the parade square at Rendsburg, in a wind coming off the canal that took the words of the ceremony and delivered them somewhere south of Hamburg. He was fifty-six, tall in the flat-fronted Danish way, and he had spent the last three years in Copenhagen at Defense Command and the four before that commanding on Zealand, and the file said all the right things. The file also said, without saying it, the thing every officer in the headquarters already knew: he had watched BOLD LANCER '83 from a distance of three hundred kilometers and a desk, and the staff he now commanded had just rehearsed the war they expected under a man who was gone.

Brauer's farewell had been short. Hvidt's acceptance was shorter. Vossen, at attention in the second rank of officers with the rain starting again, caught only one full sentence of it, delivered when the wind dropped: "— and I ask of the staff exactly what the staff is entitled to ask of me, which is candor before comfort."

"Candor before comfort," Aakjær repeated afterward, in the corridor, tasting it. "That is either a motto or a warning. In my experience of Copenhagen, it is usually a decoration."

It took the headquarters four days to learn otherwise, and the learning was uncomfortable.

Hvidt read. That was the first thing. He asked for the BOLD LANCER Endstand on his first afternoon and the full exercise file on his second, and he read it the way Vossen had seen auditors read, from the back, annexes first. Then he began appearing — without appointment, without entourage, at hours that suggested he was working the headquarters' watch bill against it — in the offices where the annexes had been written. He appeared in the G-3 operations cell on the Thursday at 0710, in battledress, with the movement annex under his arm, and put it on Vossen's desk open to the concentration tables for the Danish division.

"Ninety hours," Hvidt said. "Explain to me what this number is."

"The planning figure for the Jutland Division's move to its wartime positions, Hr. General. Mobilization, march, and occupation, from the executive order to the last battalion reporting ready in sector. It was validated —" Vossen heard himself reach for the shine — "the figure has held in three exercises."

"It has held in three exercises in which the division marched empty roads on a timetable the police cleared in advance." Hvidt's finger came down on the sheet, on a column head. "This assumption. Route capacity. Whose number is this — ours, or the Territorial Command's, or the Danish state railways'? Who owns it? If it is wrong by half, who tells me, and on which day?"

"The number is composite, Hr. General. The G-4 —"

"I did not ask what it is. I asked who owns it." He was not hostile; that was the disorienting thing. He asked the way a man asks the price of something he intends to buy. "Oberstleutnant, understand my position. You rehearsed for nine days. I have read the transcript of the rehearsal. A transcript is a photograph of a dance. I can see where everyone's feet were; I cannot feel where the weight was. So I will be asking elementary questions for some weeks, and I require the staff to answer them as if they were not elementary. Where is the weight? What must be true for this plan to work, and who is watching each of those things, by name?"

He gathered the annex, thanked him — he thanked people; Brauer never had; the staff distrusted that too — and was gone toward Lammers' office, where, Vossen learned at lunch, he had asked the G-4 to produce by Monday a one-page list titled: *Things the corps assumes it will be given.*

"He is making a ledger of our assumptions," Lammers said, torn between offense and a craftsman's respect. "Requisitioned tankers. Cleared roads. The Territorial screen in place by hour thirty. Allied air from day one. I have nine items and he says the page is not full."

"He is doing it," said Aakjær quietly, "because he does not know which of our certainties are load-bearing. We know the plan so well we no longer see it. He sees nothing else." The G-2 looked almost cheerful, which in Aakjær was a sign of barometric pressure falling somewhere. "It is the most useful ignorance in the building. It will last a month, and then he will be one of us, and we will all be certain together."

They did not get the month.

---

The autumn had been loud for a long time — that was the thing Vossen found himself explaining, years later in his head, to nobody: that there was no morning when the weather of the world turned. It had been turning all year. The airliner shot down over the Sea of Japan in September and the funerals and the expulsions after it. The missile deployments coming in December and the enormous slow argument about them in every parliament and every square in Western Europe — half a million people in Bonn in the last week of October; Marei's sister had taken the train there to march. The autumn maneuvers on the other side, bigger than any autumn anyone could remember, which Aakjær's board had been tracking since August with the patience of a man logging his neighbor's bonfires: amphibious rehearsals off Rügen in September, a Polish coastal exercise extended twice, the movement rates on the GDR railways east of Rostock holding at levels the reference books called surge.

"Exercises end," Aakjær said, at the morning briefing on the second of November, standing where the ORANGE forces of BOLD LANCER had been peeled off the plexiglass a week and a half before, in front of an acetate that no razor blade was going to clean. "That is their defining feature. This one is not ending. I have the ferry schedules from Sassnitz. I have the hospital notifications from the Rostock Bezirk — categories of civilian admission deferred, which is a mobilization measure, it was a mobilization measure in every Warsaw Pact document we hold. I have the Northern Fleet sailing. Each item has an innocent reading. I am obliged to tell the General that the innocent readings now require more assumptions than the other one."

The room was very quiet. On Aakjær's board the Baltic was not quiet at all.

Hvidt sat through it with his hands flat on the table. Then he asked his question, the one Vossen would hear him ask in a hundred forms in the weeks coming, the one that at the time sounded merely like a new commander's caution and later sounded like the whole war:

"What are we looking at that would look different if this were aimed at us?"

Aakjær considered. It was not the question he had prepared for — *is it real* — and Vossen watched the G-2 rack it and find it better than his own.

"Almost nothing, Hr. General," Aakjær said at last. "That is my difficulty. Against the Central Front, against us, against nobody — the picture is the same picture for another five, six days. The difference comes later, when their covering formations either go home or go to their jump-off areas. By then the difference is not information. It is contact."

"Then the question I must put to Copenhagen and to Karup," Hvidt said, "is not whether it is real. It is what it costs us to act as if it is, against what it costs us to be late." He stood. "Oberstleutnant Vossen. The reinforcement and readiness measures open to this headquarters short of national mobilization — I want them sequenced by political cost. Cheapest first. By tonight. The things no minister has to defend in a newspaper: leave policies, maintenance states, the ammunition that can move to the forward sites under exercise cover. We have just had an exercise. Exercises require tidying up. It would be irresponsible" — nothing moved in his face — "not to tidy up thoroughly."

It was the first order Anders Hvidt gave that the staff obeyed with enthusiasm, and Vossen, building the sequence that evening with Roloff — quiet measures, gray measures, each with its column for *visibility* and its column for *days bought* — understood that he was watching a man learn his corps and arm it in the same motion, three weeks after arriving, using the one tool he had brought with him: the assumption that nothing he had been promised was owned by anybody until a name was attached to it.

On the fourth of November the Danish government authorized the recall of key personnel from leave, described to the press as connected with the recent exercise season. On the fifth, the German territorial authorities began, very quietly, Phase One route measures on the E45 and the federal roads north of the canal. On the sixth, a Sunday, Aakjær came into the operations room at 0640 without knocking, which he had never done, and put a single signal on Vossen's desk, and stood there while he read it, and the signal was from BALTAP, and above the text some communications sergeant at Karup had typed, because forms make people honest, the two words the whole autumn had been circling:

WARNING ORDER.

At 0800 Hvidt convened the staff. At 0955 — Vossen kept the times all his life, the way you keep a stranger's telephone number found in a dead man's pocket — the corps received the NATO alert measure it had rehearsed receiving for thirty years, and a captain in the operations cell took up a chinagraph pencil, uncapped it, and stood in front of the clean plexiglass for a moment, not knowing where to begin.

"Time," said Vossen, hearing his own voice come out steady. "Begin with the time."

The captain wrote the date-time group in the upper corner, small and neat: 060955Z NOV 83. The map was no longer clean. Monday, the crates; Monday gone. Eleven days, the new commander; the new commander was the commander. And the number that mattered now stood invisible over the whole plexiglass where only Vossen and perhaps Lammers could see it, the number Hvidt had put his finger on his first Thursday, asking who owned it:

Ninety hours. And the clock on it had just started, and nobody owned it yet.
