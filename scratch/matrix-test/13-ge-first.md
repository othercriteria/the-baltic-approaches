# 13. The Visitor

The thirteenth and fourteenth days are quiet, and the quiet is worse than the battle. I know this, and so does everyone above the rank of major, because we can all read it.

The enemy has paused. Not everywhere, and not to rest. He gives up the salient at the narrows in good order; he passes his spent first-echelon divisions rearward through the Second Army; his artillery keeps up a professional unpleasantness against the Danewerk line and, for the first time, against Rendsburg itself. Harassing fire at the town's approaches, methodical, timed to cost us sleep. We have moved the rest areas underground and the senior officers have stopped remarking on it, which is its own barometer — I catch myself not remarking on it, and note that I have stopped. The deep raids come at the crossings again. The radio war thickens. But the great engine of the front has dropped to an idle, and I watch Aakjær read the idle off his board the way a doctor reads a chest.

"He is reorganizing, resupplying, and deciding," the G-2 tells us on the fourteenth. "The first two are visible. His bridging moves forward again, his artillery thickens on the neck, his engineers work the roads every dark hour. The third is not visible, and it is the only one that matters. A man six days behind his own plan, with his margin spent, does not pause to rest. He pauses because his next act requires permission — his own, or somebody's — and because the act after a pause like this is not another push. It is an answer to the question his calendar has been asking him for a week." He lays down the pointer. "I can tell the General what he can still do. I can no longer tell him what he will do, because from here his decision is not tactical. And I remind the staff of the season. From this week every night is longer than the day, the ground bears tracks by the morning frost, and the Baltic has perhaps three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who means to use the sea. Whatever he intends, the calendar in his headquarters is now shouting."

I write it down. I do not yet hear it. That is the thing I will have to account for later, to myself: it is on the wall, in Aakjær's own hand, and I copy it into my log like weather.

Into this quiet, on the afternoon of the fourteenth, at the commander's formal invitation, comes General a.D. Anselm Rahn — the wet-slate civilian suit, a briefcase that holds nothing but a folded 1:250,000 of the Baltic Approaches, 1961 printing, annotated in a hand he shows no one.

It is Hvidt who summoned him. I had disclosed the Eckernförde calls the way I disclose everything now, waiting for mild censure over the open line; instead the commander listened, asked two questions — *what does he refuse to give you*, and *how quickly does he see* — and sent the invitation over his own signature. "The Americans fly him across an ocean to ask him about encirclements," he said, signing. "He lives forty minutes from my war, and his own army's protocol office sends him invitations to receptions. I decline to be the third institution that wastes him."

We give him the confession wall. My instinct is to prepare a briefing; Hvidt overrules me — "no theater; give him the instruments and the room" — and so we stand in a loose half-circle in the operations cell, the commander among us and pointedly not foremost, while an eighty-two-year-old man in a civilian suit reads our war off the plywood for forty minutes in a silence he makes no effort to soften.

Forty minutes. I want to fill them and I have nothing to fill them with. He reads the two-times sheets, the movement log's vehicle-hours, the rationing order with the protests pinned under it, the air program's projected-against-observed. He stands longest before the two calendars — OURS and HIS — that Roloff has hung side by side, and I stand behind him and try to read the wall as he must be reading it, and I cannot, and the silence goes on until I understand that the silence is the lesson. He is not withholding. He is showing us how long it takes to actually look.

"Who audits the enemy's column?" he asks at last.

"I do, Herr General," says Aakjær.

"Against what do you audit it? Your evidence is his behavior. Behavior confirms a schedule only when it changes. His has now changed — the pause. So." He turns from the wall, and I watch him do the thing I have carried for sixteen years, since the lecture hall at the Führungsakademie when I was a captain and he was already old: he refuses the lectern, walks into the middle of us, and begins to take our answers apart with our own tools. "You have written here: six days behind, margin committed, initiative on the table. Fine sentences. I ask this corps the question its wall almost asks. You have kept his calendar. Where is his *wall*? What are the two times on the far side — scheduled, actual — of the thing he cares for most? Not ground. Ground is his reporting, and you have all learned this month what reporting is. What does his whole operation exist to produce, in one word, no *until*?"

Silence, and this time it is ours. And I feel the shape of it before I have the word, because it is the ENDEX question — the same question, turned around and aimed at the other army — and I am reaching for it when Roloff, of all of us, Roloff of the graph paper, answers first, hesitantly, in the plain shared language:

"A port, Herr General. Working. Before the winter closes the sea."

"Whose winter?" says Rahn, instantly, softly. "Whose sea?"

And he lets it hang. And I have it now — a half-second late, a half-second after the room, and I feel my own lateness as heat in the face — because the words are already on the wall, in Aakjær's season brief from this morning, the lines I copied down like weather: *three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who means to use the sea.* The careful fires. The unbroken locks. The ports he shells with tongs and will not break. The intact things, promised to somebody — promised to *ships*. And ships obey a calendar no headquarters can argue with. His true wall — the scheduled-against-actual that governs every other number in his ledger — is not drawn on land at all. It is a sea-state table. He does not have six days of lag against a phase line. He has a fixed number of days of *Baltic* left, and every day we bought at a company an hour we have been buying, all along, against *that* — and pricing it only now, only after he has spent it for us.

"Understand what you have and have not learned," Rahn says into the changed silence, and now he is not soft at all. "You have not learned his intention. Do not flatter yourselves; intentions are for novelists. You have learned his *constraint* — the one line on his wall that, once crossed, makes every other line worthless. His mass, his norms, his patience: they are all creditors, and the sea forecloses first. From this follows the only planning question worth this staff's remaining strength, and I will not answer it, because it is your war and my train of thought is eighty years old and stops at every station." He begins folding the ancient map, a man closing his own briefcase on the proceedings. "The question. A man who must have a working port before the sea closes, and who is late — what does his last week look like, and on which of its days is he *weakest*? Not in strength. In *choices*. Find that day on your wall. Plan the corps backward from it. And permit an old soldier one remark he is too old to be disciplined for" — the pale eyes go around the half-circle and stop, without theater, on Hvidt — "I have stood in a headquarters that was six days late against a closing season, on the other end of this sea, a lifetime ago. I can report to the corps commander what is being said this week in the enemy's operations room, verbatim, in any language he likes, because I have said it myself. It is: *there is still time, if.* Gentlemen, there is a species of officer who can make *if* last until the ice. Your work is to take the *if* away from him — visibly, provably, by his own arithmetic — before his superiors take the pen away from him. Because what his superiors write next" — he shuts the briefcase, and the small click is the only sound in the cell — "is written with a different pen, and this old man has seen that also, and will not stay to dinner."

He stays, in the event, twenty more minutes. Ten of them are behind a closed door with Hvidt, and I am not in the room, and neither of them ever tells me a word of it, and I do not ask; I only see the two of them come out, and something has been agreed that I will spend the next week reading off the commander's decisions instead of his mouth. Then Rahn is driven back west into the evacuation flow at dusk, one small gray car against the mattress-roofed current.

We go back to the wall. And I see that Roloff, without being ordered, has already pinned a fresh sheet under HIS CALENDAR with a heading that did not exist at noon.

It says: THE SEA'S SCHEDULE. And under it, in the G-2's hand, the first entry, transcribed from the meteorological annex with a date against it like a knife against a whetstone: BALTIC AMPHIBIOUS WINDOW, ASSESSED CLOSURE: 8–12 DECEMBER. EN REMAINING MARGIN AGAINST OWN MINIMUM PLAN: 17–21 DAYS. LESS TIME REQUIRED TO RESTORE PORT CAPACITY AFTER CAPTURE: NOT LESS THAN 10. EFFECTIVE DECISION SPACE: 7–11 DAYS.

Seven to eleven days. We have found the number under the theology at last — and it is not our number, and it never was. It is his. And for the first time since the chinagraph went up in October, I stand with the staff of Headquarters Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland in front of our wall and read, in our own handwriting, not how the war might be survived, but how it might be *won*.

---

*What the present tense bought: velocity, and a place to put Vossen's lateness. The original's reader-ahead effect was structural — season brief planted, sea harvested later. First person makes the lag personal: he copies the brief "like weather," then names his own half-second delay behind the room as heat in the face. The mechanism becomes character. What it broke: the forty-minute silent read. Third person can hold the ensemble as one instrument; first person routes it through a single skull, so the collective realization became Vossen watching himself fail to read the wall as fast as Rahn — honest, but the room's simultaneity is gone, traded for one man's confession of slowness. The Hvidt-Rahn closed door survives well; "I am not in the room… I do not ask" beats the original's authorial withholding. My read: the set-piece carries, but at a cost — Rahn's aphorisms now compete with a loud narrating I, and his voice does the didactic work regardless of grammar. First person makes the learning vivid and the teaching crowded. Viable; not obviously superior.*
