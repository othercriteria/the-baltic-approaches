## 1. NATO's period belief: integrated, not WMD-gated

This is the load-bearing finding for the book's lampshade, because it
is the belief blue's staff would actually be reasoning from — and it
does NOT match red's own bookkeeping (nbc-conceit.md §4's "chemical
rides the nuclear track" finding, sourced to the 1967 Polish Maritime
Front directive). Blue believes one thing; red's plan-of-record says
another; the corps can only act on the belief it has.

- **FM 100-2-1, *The Soviet Army: Operations and Tactics* (1984; held,
  reference/pdf/fm-100-2-1-soviet-army-operations-tactics-1984.pdf) —
  the single clearest statement of the belief, and ON THE SHELF.**
  Two passages, both load-bearing:
  - p.2-9 (Chapter 2, offensive doctrine overview): *"The Soviets do
    not perceive clear delineations between conventional, chemical,
    and nuclear warfare. It is possible that chemical weapons would be
    used early in an operation or from its onset."* Employment pattern
    given immediately after: nonpersistent agents "across the front of
    a Soviet attack" before persistent agents protect the flanks and
    strike in depth against C2, aviation, and (later) logistics —
    integrated with conventional fires, not gated behind a nuclear
    release.
  - p.16-1 (Chapter 16, NBC Warfare, opening statement): *"The Soviets
    classify both nuclear and chemical weapons as 'weapons of mass
    destruction' relative to troop protective measures, but consider
    chemical munitions to be 'conventional' weapons when discussing
    employment doctrine."* This is the manual naming the exact
    ambiguity the book's lampshade turns on: WMD-for-protection-
    purposes, conventional-for-employment-purposes — a category split
    NATO doctrine writers saw and flagged, not a simplification we are
    imposing after the fact.
  - p.16-3/16-4 (Chemical Weapons section): *"Initially, the use of
    chemical weapons may be subject to the same level of decision as
    nuclear weapons, but they are likely to be used more freely once
    the initial authority for employment has been given. In a nuclear
    war, chemical weapons are used to complement nuclear weapons.
    However, they may be used in a nonnuclear environment against an
    enemy whose chemical defenses are weak or where their use would be
    particularly advantageous."* This is the nuance worth keeping:
    even FM 100-2-1's own belief is not a clean "chemical=nuclear"
    equation — initial release *might* track the nuclear decision
    level, but the manual explicitly allows chemical-without-nuclear.
    **Tag: 1984 publication, marginally post-setting (≤1983 line) — but
    per shelf.md discipline this is "our side of the line" (instrument/
    author reference), not directly character-knowable.** The 1982
    coordinating draft and earlier FM 100-2-1 material (not yet on the
    shelf; TO-VERIFY) would be the character-knowable edition; the
    belief itself, however, is documented as stable across the decade
    by every secondary source found below, so using the 1984 text as a
    proxy for "what a 1983 officer believed" is a defensible, flagged
    approximation, not a silent anachronism.

- **Secondary-source confirmation of the "integrated, not gated"
  belief, independent of the primary docs above:** a WebSearch digest
  of open literature (title strings only; not individually pinned —
  flag for a follow-up pass if a specific citation is needed in-text)
  states plainly that Warsaw Pact forces "developed doctrine,
  organization, training and protective equipment to employ chemical
  weapons in conjunction with either conventional or nuclear weapons,"
  and that NATO worried this gave the Pact "an effective middle option
  between conventional and nuclear warfare" that undercut flexible
  response's credibility — UK Defence Secretary Michael Heseltine is
  quoted (1984) calling NATO's own chemical-retaliation gap a "major
  gap" precisely because the threat of nuclear response to a chemical
  attack "lacked credibility." This is the belief's political
  consequence, not just its doctrinal content: NATO's OWN flexible-
  response logic was seen (in 1984, by a sitting minister) as
  undermined by exactly the same integration NATO believed the Pact
  practiced.

**The belief-vs-reality gap, stated once, cleanly:** NATO's own
doctrine manual (FM 100-2-1) and the IC's assessments believed Soviet
chemical use was integrated with conventional fires and could arrive
"early in an operation or from its onset," largely independent of the
nuclear threshold. Red's actual plan-of-record on THIS front — the
1967 Polish Maritime Front directive, primary-sourced, held as a
facsimile (reference/php-maritime-front.md) — bundles chemical with
nuclear under the single category **ОМП (оружие массового поражения,
weapons of mass destruction)**, and every dual-phase line in the
document reads "без применения ОМП" (without use of WMD): the
conventional-timeline phase lines are explicitly no-chemical too, by
red's own bookkeeping, not by an author's fiat. Blue cannot see this
plan; blue reasons from FM 100-2-1's belief; the corps pays the MOPP
tempo tax against a threat that, in THIS book's canon, was never
coming — and neither blue nor the reader learns that within the story
(nbc-conceit.md's "one earned lampshade, then silence" rule).

### The IC's classified reassessment — the fetch-queue item, now HELD
### and mined (DK browser fetch 2026-07-22)

CIA DI/SOVA, *Soviet Doctrine for Offensive Chemical Warfare
Against NATO* (June 1984, info as of May 1984; sanitized, released
2012; 17 pp;
reference/pdf/cia-1984-soviet-offensive-chemical-warfare-nato.pdf).
**Post-setting by ~7 months — for us, not for a Nov-1983
character** — and it rewrites the belief-vs-reality section above
in the IC's own voice:

- **Key judgment:** "a sharp decline since the mid-1970s in Soviet
  emphasis on using chemical weapons against NATO... we now
  believe that the Soviets are unlikely to initiate extensive use
  of chemical weapons during a war with NATO" [Key Judgments,
  pp. iii-iv]. Before ~1975, chemicals were "gap fillers" bundled
  into NUCLEAR strike plans against NATO (war games "integrating
  large numbers of chemical weapons into Warsaw Pact plans for
  nuclear strikes") — exactly the 46-nuclear+30-chemical package
  our 1967 facsimile shows. After ~1975: offensive chemical
  vanished from writings, General Staff Academy curricula, and
  exercise allocations, while nuclear planning stayed richly
  visible [pp. 1-3].
- **Release, IC-confirmed:** Pact writings treat chemical with
  nuclear "as weapons of mass destruction whose release requires a
  Soviet political decision at the highest level — either the
  Politburo or the wartime Supreme High Command (VGK)"; release
  "would be simultaneous" with nuclear; "no East European
  government or military officials would be involved in this
  decision" [pp. 6-7]. The sole preauthorization exception: a NATO
  surprise attack that decapitates Pact C2, in which case front
  (perhaps army) commanders could order the initial strike
  independently [p. 7]. → The 1967 facsimile's ОМП bundling is not
  an artifact of one exercise document; it is the standing
  architecture, per the IC's "most intensive examination of this
  evidence... ever conducted" [p. 1].
- **The mutual misestimation, documented (third instance of the
  mirror-error theme):** one reason the Soviets shelved chemical is
  that "they seem to believe that the United States and its allies
  have an effective capability to respond in kind" [Key Judgments
  p. iv] — a capability §3 below shows barely existed (aging
  stockpile, nothing in-theater north of Seventh Army). Pact
  estimates even credit **Denmark** among allies "with potential
  chemical warhead options" [p. 6]! So: red deterred by a blue
  chemical capability that was largely phantom, while blue sweats
  in MOPP against a red intention that had atrophied a decade
  earlier. Both G-2s wrong, both in the cautious direction — the
  war stays clean partly because each side overestimates the
  other's dirt.
- **Epistemic timing for the book:** the reassessment's info cutoff
  is May 1984. In November 1983 this document does not exist; the
  IC's standing agreed position was still "the Soviets probably
  expect to use chemicals once the nuclear threshold has been
  crossed," with live debate about nonnuclear massive use [p. 1].
  A 1983 G-2 briefing FM 100-2-1's integrated threat is not
  strawmanned — he is state-of-the-art and wrong, and the
  correction was ~7 months from print. (Also usable: the
  Timoshenko Academy still taught offensive chemical [p. 2-3], and
  Romania kept a small domestic stockpile because it doubted
  Soviet wartime nuclear sharing [p. 7 fn].)

### The community estimate — the interagency split on the record
### (DK browser fetch 2026-07-22, second of the day)

SNIE 11/17-2-84/L, *The Soviet Offensive Chemical Warfare Threat
to NATO* (late 1984 — bibliography entries run to September 1984,
cover annotation reads November 1984; sanitized, CIA HRP release;
22 pp;
reference/pdf/cia-snie-11-17-2-84-soviet-chemical-threat-nato.pdf).
The COORDINATED community position (CIA, DIA, NSA, State + all
four services), i.e. the answer to "did the IC buy SOVA's June
1984 reassessment?" — mostly yes, with the Army's dissent
formally recorded. **Post-setting — for us.**

- **The self-aware break with the past:** "the views expressed in
  this Estimate represent a significant departure from NIE
  11-14-81... The Intelligence Community previously held the view
  that if the Pact were to employ chemical weapons against NATO
  it would do so on a 'massive' scale... We now believe that the
  Pact would not use chemical weapons in that fashion. We have
  chosen the term 'selective'" [Key Judgments pp. 2-3]. And:
  "The use of chemical weapons is not a standard, integral
  feature of the nonnuclear phase of war... initial release...
  would require a decision at the highest Soviet political and
  military levels" [KJ p. 3] — the community formally adopting
  the WMD-gated view in late 1984.
- **"All but Army agree** that, if the Soviets decided to employ
  chemical weapons, such use would most likely occur
  simultaneously with or subsequent to nuclear operations" [KJ
  p. 3]. The Army footnote dissents in exactly FM 100-2-1's
  voice: the decrease is quantity not doctrine, delivery "by
  tube artillery, multiple rocket launchers, and aircraft"
  remains provided for, and "chemical warfare can be separated
  from nuclear warfare and must be considered in that
  perspective" [KJ p. 3 fn. 1]. **The service that writes the
  blue G-2's manual is the recorded dissent from the
  community's benign turn** — so a corps staff briefing the
  integrated threat in Nov 1983 isn't merely behind the times;
  it stands where its own service's intelligence chief still
  stood a year later.
- **The refined threat model** (usable for red's decision tree):
  likelihood of initiation "low as long as their conventional
  operations against NATO proceeded satisfactorily"; selective
  use conceivable "against key targets as a part of a strategy
  to undermine NATO's escalatory capability during the
  transitional period from conventional to nuclear warfare" [KJ
  p. 3] — i.e., even the pessimistic case attaches chemical to
  the ESCALATION seam, not to the conventional campaign. This is
  the conceit's envelope stated by the IC: chemical risk turns
  on (a) red's conventional campaign failing or (b) the nuclear
  transition opening — both of which are exactly what the
  conceit-envelope validity check already watches.
- **The asymmetry, in the scope note's own words:** NATO's
  "limited ability to reply in kind — based on aging US chemical
  munitions — has forced the Alliance to threaten nuclear
  retaliation for a Pact chemical attack" [Scope Note p. v] —
  the official statement of the §3 retaliation gap, and of why
  chemical use anywhere IS an escalation question.
- Successor document named: NIE 11/17-85 (comprehensive CBW
  program treatment, scheduled May 1985) — fetch-queue candidate.

## 2. Blue's defensive posture and its cost

### MOPP levels and documented degradation

**Source note on period-line:** the cleanest sourced degradation
figures below come from FM 3-4-lineage manuals (1992 Marine Corps
edition held in /bulk/dlk/military, and the DoD Gulf War-era "MOPP
Information Paper" series) and the CANE (Combined Arms in a
Nuclear/Chemical Environment) exercise series, which the open
literature dates as running "from the early 1980s until the early
1990s" (ResearchGate listing, "Combat Effectiveness in MOPP 4: Lessons
from the U.S. Army CANE Exercises" — abstract/date not independently
retrieved, ResearchGate blocked the fetch; publication is
post-setting regardless). **All figures below are OURS (instrument/
author reference), not character-knowable** — no period-exact (≤1983)
MOPP-degradation study was located in this pass; the predecessor
manual, FM 21-40 *Chemical, Biological, and Nuclear Defense* (Sept
1966; superseded 17 May 1971 per a bookseller listing,
https://www.serviceofsupply.com/product/1966-us-army-field-manual-fm-21-40-chemical-biological-nuclear-defense-vietnam-era/;
1966 text at https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/ref/FM/PDFs/FM21-40.pdf)
predates the modern MOPP-level formalism (which the same digest dates
to "by 1980") and is not the character's actual 1983 reference —
**open item: the actual early-1980s edition of FM 21-40 or its
successor (period-exact MOPP doctrine) is not yet on the shelf; fetch
queue candidate.**

Figures, from FM 3-4 (1992, USMC edition, held:
`/bulk/dlk/military/1 Survival & Survivalist/Nuclear Biological
Chemical Survival/1992 USMC NBC Protection FM 3-4 196p.pdf`, verified
by direct pdftotext extraction) and the DoD "MOPP Information Paper"
series (Gulf War-era, hosted at gulflink.fhpr.osd.mil/mopp/ and
gulflink.health.mil, undated cover but Gulf War Illness-program
context places it 1990s):

- **The headline multiplier (FM 3-4, p. ~1503/1823/1837 of the
  extracted text):** *"Soldiers wearing MOPP4 will take about 1.5
  times longer to perform most [physical] tasks."* For work requiring
  precision or decision-making (typing messages, aiming), *"the
  normally expected completion time should be multiplied by 2.5 (or
  more, if soldiers have been in MOPP4 for an extended period or are
  overheated)."* Worked example given in the manual itself: a task
  that normally takes 90 minutes takes 135 minutes in MOPP4 (1.5 ×
  90). MOPP1/MOPP2 show no significant task-time penalty except
  extensive foot travel (overboot/glove effects).
- **Broader task battery (DoD MOPP Information Paper, gulflink):**
  "hundreds of military tasks have been tested"; aggregate degradation
  "20 to 50 percent" across task types (paper's own citation 39, not
  independently traced); oxygen consumption +~10% in full protective
  ensemble vs. light clothing (citation 40); manual-dexterity tasks
  1.5-3x longer (citation 42); first-time task performance ~30%
  longer (citation 43); cognitive/encoding tasks ~23% performance loss
  (citation 45).
- **Combat-specific figures (same source, citations 51-55, unverified
  against primary study but consistent with the FM 3-4 multiplier
  order of magnitude):** rifle marksmanship down 15-19%; individual
  weapon firing rate down 20% (defense) / 40% (attack); enemy
  engagement rate down ~one-third; casualties per enemy defender
  killed up 75%; friendly-fire incidents up from ~5% to ~20%.
- **Sustainment/maintenance:** vehicle recovery +20% time; weapon
  repair up to +70% time (both sources agree on these two figures,
  cross-checked against the CLAUDE.md-cited "artillery crew rates in
  MOPP-4" framing — no artillery-specific crew-service-rate figure was
  independently located in this pass; **open item**).
- **Communications:** effectiveness down to ~50% of unprotected
  baseline; transmission time roughly doubles; message volume
  (retransmissions) up ~50% — directly relevant to the book's radio
  traffic register (FM 24-18 is already on the shelf for prowords;
  this is the MOPP tax on top of normal net discipline).
- **Heat stress is explicitly flagged, not incidental:** FM 3-4 states
  performance/heat casualties are "inevitable" in hot-weather MOPP
  wear without rest-work cycling; a 2% body-weight loss from
  dehydration alone degrades performance. **Setting-relevant caveat:**
  the book's setting is provisionally November 1983 — cold-weather
  MOPP wear trades the heat-stress vector for cold/wet degradation of
  dexterity and morale instead; the FM 3-4 heat numbers above should
  NOT be imported uncritically for a November scenario. This is a
  genuine gap in the period sourcing — **open item**: cold-weather
  MOPP degradation studies were not searched in this pass (time
  budget); worth a follow-up if the tempo-tax mechanic is implemented.
- **Work-rest cycle (referenced in one search digest, not independently
  verified against a primary source):** "16 minutes work / ~44
  minutes rest" per hour cited as a prior-generation requirements-
  document figure — flagged as a single-source claim, not corroborated
  here.

### Decontamination burden (Soviet side, for contrast — from FM 100-2-1,

## 3. Blue's retaliatory capability, honestly

- **US chemical stockpile in FRG — two named sites, both confirmed,
  both well south of LANDJUT:**
  - **Clausen** (Kaiserslautern district, Rhineland-Palatinate): a US
    Army site storing ~100,000 GB (sarin) and VX-filled munitions in
    15 concrete bunkers, ~400 tons, in place "since the 1960s," with
    the US only publicly admitting the site's existence in 1983;
    munitions refurbished 1980-82. Sources: *Operation Steel Box*
    (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Steel_Box,
    cites the later 1990 withdrawal); Tampa Bay Times archive
    coverage of the 1990 withdrawal
    (https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1990/07/27/u-s-removes-poison-gas-from-germany/
    and .../1990/09/02/u-s-removes-chemical-shells-from-w-germany/).
  - **Fischbach** (near Pirmasens, Rhineland-Palatinate): West German
    press reporting in 1982 put holdings at 2,000-10,000 tons of GB
    and VX (VetFriends unit-history page,
    https://www.vetfriends.com/branches/army/units/us-army-depot-fischback-germany/
    — low-confidence secondary source, worth a better citation if the
    figure is load-bearing). Both sites' stocks were consolidated at
    **Miesau** and moved out under the 1986 US-FRG removal agreement
    (Operation Steel Box, 1990: 28 road convoys Clausen→Miesau, then
    rail to Nordenham, then sealift to Johnston Atoll) — confirming
    both depots were live, in-country stockpile sites through the
    setting year and beyond.
  - **Geography matters for BALTAP:** both sites sit in the US
    Seventh Army's sector in southern/central FRG, several hundred
    kilometers from Schleswig-Holstein. **No US, UK, or other NATO
    chemical-retaliatory stockpile of any kind was found located in
    or near BALTAP's area of responsibility.** This corroborates DK's
    "almost certainly not" — treat as confirmed, not merely assumed,
    though the negative (absence of a stockpile) is inherently harder
    to source than a positive; no contrary claim was found in any
    search in this pass.
- **UK and other allies: no offensive chemical capability.** The UK
  unilaterally abandoned its offensive chemical weapons program in
  1957, dismantling production and stockpiles (search-digest summary,
  Grokipedia "Chemical weapons and the United Kingdom" and "United
  Kingdom and weapons of mass destruction" pages cited by the
  aggregator — secondary/tertiary sourcing, flagged accordingly;
  cross-check against a primary UK MOD history if this becomes
  load-bearing). Confirmed via a second, independent search-digest:
  NATO's official retaliatory chemical capability rested on the
  **US and France only**; the UK, Denmark, and (West) Germany held
  none. (West Germany was treaty-barred from producing, and did not
  possess, chemical weapons — WEU 1954 protocols; not independently
  re-verified this pass but consistent with the general postwar
  settlement and uncontradicted by anything found.)
- **The binary-weapons debate, Congress 1982-85** — directly the
  period's live retaliation-modernization fight, and useful texture
  for a Washington-adjacent scene or staff aside:
  - Testimony before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee in 1982:
    binary 155mm projectiles were 3-4x more expensive than equivalent
    unitary rounds.
  - Statutory history (50 U.S.C. Ch. 32, via
    https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=%2Fprelim%40title50%2Fchapter32&edition=prelim
    and the Reagan Library's "Message to the Congress on the Binary
    Chemical Munitions Program,"
    https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/message-congress-binary-chemical-munitions-program):
    Congress barred obligating funds for binary production after 24
    Sep 1983 unless the President certified a 1-for-1 unitary-shell
    destruction offset; production itself was barred before 1 Oct
    1985, and required a further presidential certification of
    "national interest" before it could begin.
  - Rep. Dante Fascell (D-FL), House Foreign Affairs chair, warned
    publicly that binary weapons could become "the poor man's bomb"
    and would restart a superpower CW arms race (C&EN coverage,
    https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/cen-v062n019.p024).
  - A "force goal" for NATO binary modernization was submitted to and
    adopted by the North Atlantic Council (same statutory-history
    digest) — i.e., the alliance-level paperwork for a modernized
    retaliatory capability existed and was in motion during the
    setting window, but **actual binary production had not yet begun
    as of the Nov 1983 setting date** (the earliest possible
    production date under the statute was Oct 1985, itself gated on a
    further certification). **This is clean, sourced furniture for
    the book: in November 1983, NATO's retaliation-in-kind capability
    is aging unitary stock plus a modernization program that exists
    only on paper and in congressional testimony — not yet in a
    magazine anywhere.**

## 4. Period incidents feeding the ambient-reports texture

What a November-1983 G-2 shop would plausibly have crossing the desk
as unconfirmed or contested chemical-use reporting from OTHER
theaters — the real-world referents for nbc-conceit.md's "unconfirmed
reports of chemical use on other fronts may drift through the G-2
summaries" clause:

- **"Yellow Rain" (Southeast Asia, alleged Soviet/Vietnamese/Lao use
  against Hmong populations), 1981-83, PUBLIC and squarely
  character-knowable:**
  - US Secretary of State Alexander Haig announced in 1981 that the
    USSR had supplied the agent to Vietnamese/Pathet Lao forces
    against Hmong villages; State Dept. later claimed >10,000 killed.
  - A formal congressional hearing — the "Yellow Rain Hearing," 10 Nov
    1981 (Subcommittee on Arms Control, Oceans, International
    Operations and Environment) — put the allegation on the public
    record early in the window. (EBSCO Research Starters summary,
    no direct primary transcript pulled this pass.)
  - **The scientific rebuttal is ALSO period-live by 1983**, which is
    the useful texture for a book that wants "unconfirmed" to feel
    genuinely unconfirmed rather than a settled matter offstage:
    Matthew Meselson's team's 1983 Thailand fieldwork found the
    "yellow rain" samples were bee feces / pollen residue from giant
    Asian honeybees, not a toxin weapon — this is discussed at length
    in Science History Institute's "The Mystery of Yellow Rain"
    (https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-mystery-of-yellow-rain/)
    and the Harvard Meselson CBW Archive
    (https://meselsonarchive.hsites.harvard.edu/yellow-rain). **By the
    setting date (Nov 1983), the allegation and its leading scientific
    rebuttal are BOTH already in play** — a G-2 summary mentioning
    Yellow Rain in Nov 1983 would be citing a contested claim, not a
    settled one, which is exactly the "ambient, unadjudicated" texture
    the conceit wants.
- **Afghanistan (alleged Soviet chemical use against mujahideen),
  1980-83, PUBLIC:**
  - State Department claims escalate through the window: "several
    dozen chemical attacks," >300 dead cited in 1982; earlier claims
    of >3,000 Afghans affected also dated 1982 (UPI archive summary,
    https://www.upi.com/Archives/1984/02/22/Soviet-use-of-chemical-weapons-in-Afghanistan-and-the/3471446274000/,
    itself a Feb 1984 retrospective — just past the setting line but
    describing the 1982-83 claims).
  - A State Dept. retrospective (per the same UPI digest) states
    **reported Soviet chemical use in Afghanistan "declined in 1983"**
    — a useful, period-plausible detail: the ambient reporting a
    November-1983 G-2 shop sees could plausibly be *thinning out* on
    the Afghan axis even as attention shifts toward the newer
    Iran-Iraq reports (below).
  - The CIA's own contemporaneous analytic product exists and is
    citable by title even though not read in full this pass: *Soviet
    Motivations for the Use of Chemical Weapons in Afghanistan and
    Southeast Asia*
    (https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/node/1632491,
    PDF: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/soviet%20motivations%20for%20th%5B13984256%5D.pdf)
    — cia.gov-blocked from this network, fetch-queue/DK-browser item.
- **Iran-Iraq War, 1983-84 — the newest and most consequential
  ambient item for a Nov-1983 G-2, PUBLIC and dated right at the
  setting line:**
  - Iraqi mustard-gas use against Iranian forces is dated to
    **August-November 1983** in multiple sources (mustard use "in
    mid-1983," specific battles named: Piranshahr and Haji Omaran,
    Aug 1983; Panjwin, Nov 1983 — Wikipedia, "Iraqi chemical attacks
    against Iran," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_chemical_attacks_against_Iran,
    cross-checked against the Wilson Center's two-part blog series on
    Iraqi records, https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/part-ii-we-attacked-them-chemical-weapons-and-they-attacked-us-chemical-weapons-iraqi).
  - **Iran's formal UN complaint is dated 3 Nov 1983** — i.e.,
    essentially contemporaneous with a November-1983 setting. This is
    the single most period-precise, on-the-nose item found in this
    pass: a G-2 summary in the book's own setting month could
    plausibly reference "Iran's complaint to the UN" as breaking news,
    not history.
  - Formal international confirmation (UN specialist team observing
    mustard-gas casualties in Iranian hospitals) comes **13-19 March
    1984** — just past the setting line, but close enough that a
    late-set scene could plausibly reference the UN team's dispatch
    as pending/imminent rather than concluded. Iraq's first nerve-agent
    (tabun) use is dated **March 1984**, definitively post-setting —
    if the book wants to keep the ambient reports to blister agents
    only (mustard), Nov 1983 is clean; a nerve-agent reference would
    require either a later scene date or an explicit "not yet"
    framing.

## 5. Consequences for the instrument

**Recommend, do not implement (per task discipline).** MOPP is a
reasonable small blue efficiency tax to model IF the instrument ever
wants a tempo cost for "protective posture" as a standing condition,
not an event:

- The FM 3-4 multiplier (1.5x task time for physical work, 2.5x for
  precision/decision work) is clean, sourced, and simple enough to be
  a single scalar applied to blue throughput while "protective
  posture: on" is toggled — consistent with the instrument's existing
  preference for measurement-only NBC additions (nbc-conceit.md §6
  already queues two: red's norm-shortfall metric and a conceit-
  envelope validity check; this would be a third, on blue's side).
  **Caveat carried forward, not resolved:** these are 1990s Gulf-era
  hot-weather figures; a November-1983 LANDJUT scenario is a
  cold/wet-weather MOPP problem, and no cold-weather degradation
  figure was located this pass. Using the FM 3-4 multiplier as a
  stand-in is a flagged approximation, not a verified cold-weather
  number — if the instrument implements this, the flag should travel
  with the constant, not just this document.
- The communications-effectiveness figure (~50% of baseline, doubled
  transmission time) is arguably the more narratively useful one:
  it's a friction the reader feels in dialogue/message traffic (FM
  24-18 prowords delivered slower, garbled, re-sent) rather than an
  abstract throughput multiplier — cheaper to dramatize than to
  simulate.
- **Not recommended for modeling:** the combat-effectiveness figures
  (marksmanship, friendly fire, casualties-per-kill) — these compound
  with the Dupuy/QJM attrition bench in ways that would need their own
  calibration pass and are far more sourcing-fragile (single-digest,
  uncorroborated citation numbers) than the task-time multiplier.
  If wanted later, source them properly first (the CANE exercise
  reports themselves, not a paraphrase of them).

## 6. Consequences for the book

then silence") now has furniture:

  this book's canon, unknown to them) to be wrong about THIS front.

## 7. Addendum (2026-07-23): the DIA public pamphlet — the maximalist voice, held

**DIA, *Soviet Chemical Weapons Threat*, DST-1620F-051-85 (1985;
26 pp; local file reference/pdf/dia-soviet-chemical-weapons-threat-
dst-1620f-051-85.pdf — the CIA-RDP88B00745R000100140027-5 string is only
CREST's archival ID of CIA's file copy, "COMIREX FILE COPY"; the
originator is DIA/DST, preface signed Lt. Gen. James A. Williams,
Director).** This was §1's flagged fetch-queue item; DK browser
fetch 2026-07-23; distilled same day (Opus subagent, visual read of
the image scan).

**What it is:** an UNCLASSIFIED public threat pamphlet in the
Soviet-Military-Power genre — and, decisively for us, an "updated
version of a document issued as a Department of Defense publication
in **October 1983**" (preface, p. iii). So the maximalist
integrated-chemical-threat picture was not merely an internal Army
belief (FM 100-2-1) or a footnote dissent (the held SNIE): it was
**institutionally mainstream and publicly promulgated, in
period-exact form, one month before our setting.**

**Headline claims (page anchors in the distillation record):**
"formidable capability"; recent use claims (Afghanistan, SE Asia,
mycotoxins/"yellow rain"); near-universal delivery compatibility;
storage capacity growing since the late 1960s; **"chemical weapons
are collocated with conventional weapons in forward areas facing
NATO" (p. 1)**; closing line: any force fighting the Soviets "will
run a substantial risk" of chemical use. This is the direct
opposite of the held SOVA June-1984 reassessment — the
state-of-the-art-and-wrong belief of §1, in an authoritative public
artifact.

**The buried concession:** even this maximalist product carries the
release gate — targets are struck "once the approval to use
chemical weapons has been given by the highest Soviet authority"
(p. 5), consistent with the held Politburo/VGK finding — buried in
the Targets section while the collocation language rides the
headline. For the book's one earned lampshade this is gold: the
document blue's staff would cite for the integrated threat
CONTAINS, in its own text, the gate that red's bookkeeping actually
ran on. Also useful: the Soviet protective-suit heat-burden norms
table (p. 19: ≥30°C → 15-20 min; 50% work-pace loss after 6h) —
the red-side mirror of §2's MOPP tax; both armies pay the same
degradation.

**Period line (split verdict):** the 1985 edition as an object is
post-setting (OURS): the 1985 storage-chart endpoint, the
"11 nations" proliferation count, and the Iraq-Iran use line must
not appear in 1983 mouths. The doctrinal content — integrated
threat, forward collocation, agent/delivery/target picture,
"substantial risk" register — is character-knowable via the
October 1983 DoD original, and is the strongest provenance yet for
what a November-1983 G-2 briefs in good faith. Shelf tag: doctrinal
content ≤1983 = character-knowable; 1985-specific figures = OURS.
Complements, does not duplicate, the classified 1984 pair: this is
the public maximalist voice; those are the classified corrective.
