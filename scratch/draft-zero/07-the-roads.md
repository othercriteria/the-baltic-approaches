# 7. The Roads

The Territorial Command's evacuation returns came in on the teleprinter at 0415 each morning, because the Kreis officials worked at night, after the roads had been counted. On the sixth morning of the war the return for Kreis Schleswig-Flensburg ran to nine pages, and on the fourth page, in the alphabetical monotony of assembly points and column numbers, stood a line that Vossen read three times:

SAMMELPUNKT 14 (SCHLESWIG-FRIEDRICHSBERG). KOLONNE 217. ABMARSCH 130700. ROUTE GELB-3. ZIEL: AUFNAHMEKREIS RENDSBURG-ECKERNFÖRDE. FAHRZEUGE 31. PERSONEN 118.

Column 217. Thirty-one vehicles, one hundred eighteen persons, departing 0700 from the assembly point at the Friedrichsberg school, four hundred meters from his own front door, on Route Yellow-3, which he had personally argued into the annex in a peacetime meeting because Yellow-3 kept civilian traffic off the B76 that the corps would need. His own family was three lines of that arithmetic. The return did not say so. The return said 118 persons. The machinery he had helped build was working, and it reported his wife and children to him as a passenger count, one day after the fact, at four in the morning, on paper that smelled of ozone.

Marei had not telephoned. That was the annex too — OUTGOING CIVIL CALLS FROM THE FORWARD KREISE: RESTRICTED — and it was also, he knew, Marei: she would not ring the headquarters to make the G-3 of the corps ask the switchboard for a private line while the switchboard was fighting a war. She had packed the car they had agreed on, on the timetable the loudspeaker vans announced, and taken her place in Column 217 behind some neighbor's Opel, and was now — he checked the received stamp; the column had closed its movement at 1940 — in her mother's flat in Rendsburg, eleven kilometers from where he sat, in the same town, unreachable by anything but the thing he could not do, which was leave the building.

He put the return in the out-tray. He noted — the noticing was becoming a reflex, the war's whole curriculum in a twitch of the eye — that the return's columns were all inputs again: persons moved, vehicles counted, routes assigned. Nowhere on nine pages did the Territorial Command's paperwork record the number that had governed the corps' entire yesterday: how many hours Route Yellow-3's crossing points had been blocked at the Eider, where Column 217 and eleven columns like it had met the tail of the Danish division's artillery regiment coming the other way.

Fourteen hours. Lammers had the figure, because Lammers' movement control teams had spent the fourteen hours making it no worse. The Jutland Division's closing — hour one hundred thirty of the ninety-hour clock now, sixty percent complete, the brigades feeding into the northern sector piecemeal as they arrived — was no longer being paced by mobilization, or by the bridge, or by anything the plan had a table for. It was being paced by the fact that Schleswig-Holstein's road net was carrying, in opposite directions at once, the two heaviest movements in its history: a division going to the war and a population leaving it. Every solution was a trade. Hold the civil columns to let a brigade pass, and the columns backed up into the towns the enemy's guns were registering. Pass the columns, and the brigade stood still, burning its march tables, while forty kilometers south the covering force paid a company an hour for the delay.

The movement control log had begun, in the dry hand of some Danish captain at the Eider control point, to keep the exchange rate: COLUMN PRIORITY DECISIONS, 13 NOV — MILITARY PRECEDENCE GRANTED 11 (EST CIVIL DELAY AGGREGATE 2,300 VEHICLE-HOURS). CIVIL PRECEDENCE GRANTED 4 (EST MILITARY DELAY: 2/3 JYSKE ARTY GRP, 3 HRS; ENGR BR COLUMN, 5 HRS.) Somebody at a bridge with a clipboard had invented, under pressure, the currency conversion the corps' whole reporting system lacked: vehicle-hours of the defended against march-hours of the defenders, priced against each other, decision by decision, in ink.

Vossen had the page photostated and pinned beside Roloff's graph paper on the operations room wall. Under it he wrote, on a file card, the caption that stayed there for the rest of the war, and that he was never afterward sure whether he had written as instruction or confession:

THIS IS THE CORPS DECIDING WHAT IT IS FOR. TWICE BEFORE NOON.

---

Aakjær found him by the wall at midday. The G-2 stood reading the movement log with his hands in his pockets, and Vossen knew enough by now to wait.

"Korsør," Aakjær said at last, conversationally, eyes still on the photostat. "My wife's town. Her mother, her sister, the sister's boys. West Zealand — the invasion coast, if there is to be one; my own board gives the landing threat against it a probability I decline to brief because the confidence interval is wider than the Belt. There are no columns for Zealand, you understand. An island does not evacuate. It waits, and listens to the radio tell it that the Jutland battle is going —" he considered the boards across the room, all green — "according to plan." He turned from the wall. "I tell you this for one reason, Hr. Oberstleutnant. Your family drove past mine's fate yesterday and both of us signed the paperwork that arranged it. Officers in our positions do not get the other kind of war, the kind with a farmhouse to defend. We get the annexes. So when I am slow to bless a green board, or you are slow to release a road, the staff may think us cautious men." The thin smile, briefly. "Let them. We are merely the two officers in the building who know the price list has names on it."

He was gone before Vossen assembled an answer, which was, he had learned, how Aakjær preferred all his exchanges to end.

At 1750, movement control forwarded for the G-3's counter-signature the route order for the fourteenth: Yellow-3 closed to civil traffic from 0500, all Eider crossings military-precedence for twelve hours, to pass the last two battalions of the First Jutland Brigade and the corps' bridging reserve south in one push. The staffing was correct; the trade had been costed; the Territorial Command had initialed; somewhere down the line of consequence, tomorrow's Column 231 and 232 — some other school, some other 118 persons — would stand an extra day in some assembly point within range of the guns, because the road they had been promised now belonged to the war.

Vossen read it twice and signed it once, and dated it with the two times, scheduled and actual, as the commander required. They were, for once, identical. He put down the pen with the care of a man setting down a glass, and went back to the boards, and the war.
