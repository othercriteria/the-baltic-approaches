# 9. The Morning Market

The theater's air was divided every morning at Karup, at a conference the corps attended as a voice on a secure line, and by the second week Vossen had come to think of it as a market — the morning market, Kjeldsen called it, without affection — where three customers bid every day for the same fish.

The mechanics were simple and the simplicity was the trap. The air commander at Karup owned the sorties: the Danish F-16s and Drakens, the German fleet — what the first week had left of all of them — and the promised reinforcing squadrons arriving on schedules the corps had learned to read with Hvidt's question in hand: *who owns this number*. Each morning the theater commander apportioned: so much to air defense, without which there was no second week; so much to the maritime war, the invasion fleet that Aakjær's board kept assembling and disassembling off Rügen; so much to the land battle. And of the land battle's share, the corps requested, nominated, argued — and received what it received, hours later, weather permitting, which in a Schleswig November meant that the market dealt in a currency the sky devalued by noon.

On the fifteenth, with the crossing running well and the ammunition curves newly on the wall, the corps made its first great bid, and the bid was the plan's own doctrine speaking. Vossen built it with Kjeldsen through the small hours: a coordinated interdiction program against the follow-on forces — the second echelon that doctrine promised, the mass that Aakjær could not find but the plan insisted was marshaling behind the screen of the first, somewhere east of Lübeck, on the rail lines the target folders had memorized for a decade. Strike the movement before it deployed. Buy the defense a week in the enemy's depth instead of a day at the front. It was the best staff work the operations cell had produced all war — Vossen believed that at 0400 pinning the target list, and believed it still, years later, which was what made the lesson stick — and at the morning market it won: the theater commander, pressed by the corps' liaison with an eloquence Kjeldsen reported almost shyly, allocated the land battle's whole share and a slice of the maritime reserve to the deep program for twenty-four hours.

What came back, across the next two days, was an education in three parts.

Part one: the sorties flew late. Not through sloth — through arithmetic the bid had never priced. The deep targets required particular loads, and the loads required turnaround, and the turnaround required the very airfields the enemy's own air had spent a week cratering on a schedule of their own; and the November sky opened its windows when it opened them, not when the target list did. Of the program's planned weight, perhaps three-fifths flew, most of it six to ten hours behind the timings the corps' fire plan had built around it.

Part two: the strikes that flew, worked. The evidence arrived over the following days in the sober gray of post-strike assessment: rail cuts east of Bad Kleinen, a bridge down at Ratzeburg, a march serial caught in open column and gutted. Delay was imposed. Somewhere behind the enemy's screen, somebody's movement tables were being rewritten in the night, and Vossen read the assessments with the specific satisfaction of a man whose theory has printed money.

Part three: the front did not notice.

That was the part that had no page in the doctrine. The pressure on the covering positions did not slacken on the sixteenth, nor the seventeenth. The enemy's lead formations attacked with undiminished weight, on their same deliberate ten-kilometer heartbeat, fed — Aakjær's traffic analysis was unequivocal — from stocks and columns already west of everything the deep program had struck. The corps had reached into the enemy's depth and found it, hit it, delayed it; and the battle in front of the corps was being fought and supplied from somewhere nearer than the depth. The interdiction was a check drawn on the future, and the front was being lost in the present, and on the seventeenth the price of the check came due in the plainest possible form: the day's air, spent deep, had not flown against the artillery groupings that spent that same day working, methodically, tube by tube, through the forward positions of the 6th Panzergrenadier Division.

Loheide sent for no liaison officer this time. He asked for the corps commander, and Hvidt went, and took Vossen, and the division's forward command post received them in a farm complex south of the Danewerk with the courtesy of a man receiving creditors.

Generalmajor Loheide was sixty, square, and had been fighting for nine days on ground where his soldiers' families had lived for three hundred years. He did not reproach. He briefed — and the brief was the reproach. Here the division's obstacle system, the belts his engineers had cut and mined and wired through a decade of quiet autumns, functioning exactly as designed: the enemy slowed, canalized, held close — "held close, Herr General; I invite the corps to consider the phrase" — against positions that were being milled flat by an artillery mass that stood, all of it, inside the belts' own delay, comfortably within range, firing from grid squares the division could name and could not silence. "My belt is a masterpiece," Loheide said, standing at his map with his hands behind his back. "It holds him near his railheads. It shortens the road his shells travel. It gives his guns time to displace forward at their leisure while my anti-tank screen wins engagements and my infantry dies of the counter-preparation. Yesterday I was told the corps' air went deep, against his railheads and his reserves, to make him late next week. I do not doubt it succeeded. Herr General, with respect: my division is being destroyed by the part of his army that is already early. The corps' arithmetic and mine are both correct. I request that someone above me decide which of the two correct answers commands the other, because from where I stand, the deep battle is a rumor and the close battle is a fact, and the fish" — the phrase had traveled, then; the whole theater knew about the morning market — "the fish is being sold to the future while the present starves."

The drive back ran west into a low red evening, along a route where the military police were passing a civil column eastward at a crossroads, thirty vehicles with mattresses on the roofs, and neither Hvidt nor Vossen spoke for twenty kilometers.

"Both correct," Hvidt said at last, to the windscreen. "The division wants the sorties close, and is right. The plan wants them deep, and was right on the page. Karup splits the difference each morning and is right by the arithmetic of committees. And I signed the deep bid, Vossen, on your staff work, and your staff work was sound." He watched the column of taillights ahead. "At Friedrichstadt we learned the corps had a clock nobody owned. I begin to suspect the air question is the same question in a worse disguise — that somewhere under the doctrine there is a number that would tell us on which days the deep battle buys more than the close one, and that nobody at this morning's market — not Karup, not the plan, not I — knows what it is. We argued doctrine against doctrine. Doctrine against doctrine is theology." He turned from the window, and in the dark of the car his voice had the flat carefulness it took on when he had found the next thing he intended to be unreasonable about. "Find me the number under the theology. What, exactly, must be true of *him* — his stocks, his echelons, his timetable — for a deep sortie to outbid a close one? Because if the answer depends on facts about the enemy we do not hold, then the corps has been bidding at that market every morning without knowing what it was buying. And so, Herr Oberstleutnant, has he."

In the operations room that night, Roloff added a third sheet to the wall: the air program, scheduled against actual, projected effect against observed. The projected column was doctrine's handwriting. The observed column was the war's. Between them, unmistakable now that the sheets hung side by side — the crossing, the shells, the sorties — ran the same silent lesson, and Vossen stood before it a long time after the night shift changed:

Every number the corps had trusted was a number about itself. Every number that had turned out to govern events was a number about the enemy — and those, the corps did not keep. It borrowed them from doctrine, at interest, and the interest was compounding at the front, daily, in men.
