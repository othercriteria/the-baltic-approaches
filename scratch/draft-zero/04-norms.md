# 4. Norms

The forward command post of the Coastal Front stood in a beech wood south of Güstrow, forty meters of it underground, and at 0530 on the second morning of the war its commander stood before a map on which the war was going according to plan, and said so.

"According to plan," said General broni Tadeusz Zawadzki, and touched the map with the flat of his hand, gently, the way you touch a machine that is running well. "The covering battle develops according to plan."

The map said it too. The map at Güstrow was a beautiful thing, kept by three draftsmen in relays: the two German divisions of the first echelon — the screen-breakers, the border-eaters — shown biting into the peninsula north and south of Flensburg; the phase line for Day Two drawn where the planning norm put it, ten kilometers a day for the conventional variant, a clean blue line through Schleswig-Holstein like a tide table. The norm was the plan's heartbeat. Zawadzki had commanded the Front for two years and had spent both of them defending that heartbeat against men who wanted it faster: ten kilometers a day, the deliberate variant, the variant that arrived at the Kiel Canal with bridges standing and arrived at the Belts with ports worth having. The other variant — the one written in the annex with the different classification, the one that opened with thirty-one strikes and crossed the same map in half the time through country that would glow — the other variant Zawadzki had spent two years declining, and the declination was the proudest work of his life, though it appeared on no map at all.

"Day Two positions by eighteen hundred," said his chief of staff. General dywizji Stefan Rylski was ten years younger, thin as a file, and had the habit — Zawadzki tolerated it the way you tolerate a good dog's barking — of reading the reports from the bottom. "According to the divisional reporting. I note for the record what the divisional reporting counts. It counts the head of the column at the phase line. The Fourth Army's lead regiments report the line reached at Jübek. Their artillery is on the line. Their fuel is not on the line. Their bridging is eleven kilometers behind their fuel, behind a demolition the Germans fired at Owschlag, on the one road our own movement table assigns to two divisions." He let the paper hang a moment. "The head of the snake is punctual, Comrade General. I am paid to report on the length of the snake."

"You are paid," said Zawadzki, without heat, "to remember that no offensive in the history of this century has ever been on time by the accounting of its own chief of staff." There was long custom in it; the staff around the map permitted themselves the eighth of a smile that was permitted. "The norm is ten. The reporting says ten. The Germans are trading villages exactly as their doctrine requires them to — you have read the same doctrine I have, Stefan; they will trade until the canal, and stand on the canal, and we will be through their trading space by Day Four with the Front's arithmetic intact. What does the enemy have in front of Fourth Army? Territorials. A screen of schoolteachers with panzerfausts and three thousand real soldiers whose division is still on the wrong side of the water. The clock we are racing is not German. It is in Copenhagen and it is called mobilization, and this morning we are ahead of it."

"This morning we are ahead of the part of it we can see," said Rylski, and folded his paper, and that was the end of it, because the second morning of a war that is going according to plan is not the morning for the other conversation.

At the back of the bunker, in the visitors' corner with its own better coffee, the Soviet representative to the Front, General-mayor Kuragin, listened to the exchange with the expression of a man at the theater in a foreign city: attentive, benevolent, keeping his own program folded in his lap.

Four hundred and ten kilometers north-west by road — none of it road you could presently drive — the same morning looked like this:

BATTLE UPDATE 090600Z. COVERING FORCE ENGAGED FULL SECTOR WIDTH. NIGHT POSITIONS HELD OR ADJUSTED PER PLAN. ENEMY PENETRATION AXIS FLENSBURG–SCHLESWIG 14 KM, AXIS SÜDERLÜGUM–HUSUM 11 KM. KILL RATIOS (UMPIRED FROM UNIT CLAIMS): 1:4.2 ARMOR, 1:3.1 ALL SYSTEMS. OWN LOSSES WITHIN PLANNING RATE. DEMOLITION BELT ALFA EXECUTED 100 PERCENT, BELT BRAVO PREPARED.

Vossen read it aloud at the morning conference, and heard the room receive it the way the room had received the Endstand of BOLD LANCER, and heard, underneath his own steady voice, Brauer asking his mild question about the forty minutes and the port of Esbjerg. *Kill ratios umpired from unit claims.* On the ninth of November there were no umpires. There were tired company commanders in the rain reporting what burned, and every army in every war had counted what burned generously, and the corps' reporting formats — he had helped write them — took the claims, applied a reduction factor agreed in committee in 1979, and produced a ratio with one decimal place. The decimal place was the shine. Nothing with a decimal place looks like a guess.

"One to four point two," said Hvidt, from the head of the table. He had taken to repeating numbers back into the room, without comment, like a man testing coins on stone. "Oberstløjtnant Aakjær. If that ratio is true, what should I see happening on your board within two days?"

Aakjær stood. He had been up all night with the traffic — not road traffic; the other kind, the kind that came through the intercept troop at Stadum and the Törsbøl direction-finding baseline and arrived on his board as call signs with bearings and habits. "If the ratio is true, Hr. General, the enemy's first-echelon regiments are at half strength by Thursday and we will see it in their radio order of battle — nets amalgamating, regimental nets going quiet, replacement of call signs at a rate that indicates command casualties. What I in fact see this morning," and here he moved his hand across the board's north-east corner, over the water, "is a first echelon that is not spending itself like a first echelon. Two divisions attack us. Two. Everything in their doctrine, everything in the correlation the General Defense Plan is built on, says five to seven in the first operational echelon on this axis, with a second echelon behind it that our air forces have been begging to be allowed to find. Where is the mass? Either the ratio is flattering us, or the enemy in front of us is not the enemy the plan was written against. I can support both readings from this morning's traffic. I am not yet prepared to choose."

"Choose by Thursday," said Hvidt. "The corps' reserve exists to be spent against the enemy's mass. I would prefer to know which enemy owns the mass before I spend it."

It went into the record as a routine tasking. Vossen, writing it into the operations log with the two times the commander now required — *tasked 090640Z; due 100600Z* — did not know he was logging the first move of the argument that would decide the campaign; nobody knows that at the time. What he registered instead, with a professional discomfort he could not yet name, was the shape of the morning as a whole: at Güstrow, though he could not see it, a commander looking at punctual phase lines while his chief of staff counted the length of the snake; and at Rendsburg a commander looking at flattering ratios while his intelligence officer counted the silence where the mass should be — two headquarters, both green, both ahead of schedule by their own arithmetic, and between them, in the rain along the Treene, the actual war, keeping its own books.

The division liaison came at noon: an Oberstleutnant from 6th Panzergrenadier with mud to the waist and the particular courtesy of a man determined not to say aloud what he has been sent to say. Generalmajor Loheide's compliments. The division would hold Belt BRAVO through the night as ordered. The division wished the corps to be aware — *aware*, the word did its work — that the covering battalions were now trading at one-to-one in some sectors. Not vehicles. Positions for hours. The enemy did not maneuver against a blown bridge; he arrived, he massed his guns with a speed the manuals had promised and nobody had believed, he fired for eleven minutes, and he was across in assault boats while the rubble was still moving. "The Herr General asks me to say it in one sentence," the liaison officer said, at the door, permission granted, the courtesy finally set down like a heavy case. "The sentence is: we are not delaying them, Herr Oberstleutnant — we are spending men to make their punctuality expensive, and the corps should not confuse the two when it reads our reports."

Vossen took that upstairs himself. On the stairs he passed the wall where the framed photograph of the exercise of 1978 had hung in the mess a hundred meters and three weeks away, and the thought arrived unbidden, in his own voice reading the Endstand: *delay imposed on the enemy: sixty percent beyond planning norm.* Somewhere on the far side of the water was a man with a phase line called Day Two, reading a report that said the norm was met. Here was a liaison officer saying the delay was real and was not delay. Both were reading truthfully from books that measured different wars.

He put the sentence in front of Hvidt without smoothing it. The commander read it twice, and looked up, and what he said was not about the division at all.

"Three weeks I have asked this staff who owns each number," Hvidt said. "I begin to think I asked the smaller question. Tonight, when the boards are green, I want you to ask of each green light: green *against what?* Against the plan's expectation. And who wrote the expectation, and in which October." He handed the message back. "The Landjut Division buys hours. The reporting sells me ratios. Somewhere in this headquarters, Vossen, there is a page that says what an hour is worth. Find out if it exists. If it does not, that is the first thing I have learned in this war."
