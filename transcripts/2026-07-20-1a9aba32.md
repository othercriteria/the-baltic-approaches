## ASSISTANT

Top-down, firmly — with one refinement about where the top is and one about where the bottom stops. Reasoning:

**The quantities that must be honest are aggregate quantities.** The book's arithmetic lives at division/corps grain: road-march tables, daily attrition under contact, frontages, ammunition and fuel throughput, decision-cycle lag. That's also exactly where the period models natively operate (QJM and Epstein take divisions as inputs; Dupuy's historical calibration data — advance rates, daily casualty percentages — exists *only* at that grain). A bottom-up build has no ground truth to verify against: nobody has validation data for "10,000 synthetic platoon fights aggregate to this corps outcome," so you end up verifying your model against your own assumptions, with aggregation error compounding at every level. Bottom-up is how you get a physics engine that's confidently wrong at the operational level — and ours would be confidently wrong *about the thing the book is about* (tempo, throughput).

**The POV argues the same way.** A division G-3 or composite-formation CoS experiences the war as aggregates and reports — strengths, spot reports, movement tables, the map at 1:250,000. The model's job is to generate what the staff sees. Below battalion, the book doesn't need a model at all; it needs research and prose. Wargaming a fire team adds nothing to a scene that the shelf and the author's eye don't add better.

**Where the top is:** not ACE, if I'm reading you as Allied Command Europe. Anchor the playable frame at the theater command that owns the front — BALTAP/AFNORTH for the LANDJUT candidate — and represent everything above it as *boundary conditions*: air apportionment arriving (or not), the reinforcement release that doesn't come, SACEUR's attention elsewhere. Scripted or stochastic, not played. This is cheap to build, honest about what higher echelons are to a peripheral theater, and it *is* the reader contract in mechanism form — the top of the model is weather. Nobody is coming; you can see it in the input file.

**Where the bottom stops:** battalion as the atomic counter, the standard atom of operational hex games at this scale, and tractable for LANDJUT (order ~50–80 maneuver battalions across both sides in the opening frame). Two-level resolution: the campaign frame moves battalion/brigade counters on the theater map, and an engagement-resolution layer (the period models) adjudicates contacts — with the option to zoom a *named* engagement for chapter purposes by decomposing that one battalion's fight into companies, by hand, informed by the model's outcome rather than generated by it. Decompose on demand, never wholesale.

**Verification is also top-down:** calibrate against Dupuy's historical rates and the period's own published cases (the 3:1-rule exchange conveniently comes with worked examples), then cross-check by running the same scenario through Lanchester, QJM, and Epstein and treating disagreement as a sensitivity brief rather than picking a winner — evaluations are briefs, per house rules.

The one thing bottom-up genuinely buys — emergent tactical surprise — we get instead from the LC-style structures (deception counters, priced enabler decisions) and from the dice at engagement resolution, which is where Convoy-84's honest surprise actually came from too.

# Survey: /bulk/dlk/military — Logistics &amp; Sustainment Texture for a 1980s NATO-Corps "The Goal"

**Corpus:** 22,008 files / 15 GB, 118 top-level entries. Provenance confirmed: these are survivalebooks.com repackages of genuine US Army correspondence-course reference texts and school POI (Program of Instruction) packages — mostly US Army Transportation/Quartermaster/Signal/Ordnance schools. Content is authentic doctrine; only the PDF wrappers are commercial. Filenames are highly descriptive (year + "US Army TITLE Np.pdf", or "YYYY TITLE N FILES" course folders).

## Headline finding on the rail cluster
**Yes — the rail-operations cluster is the single most on-theme asset in the entire corpus, and it is worth mining hard.** It is genuinely period (1969–1976), it is doctrinally about *throughput and capacity of a constrained network in a theater of war*, and it explicitly uses the vocabulary of a NATO/Warsaw-Pact continental theater (COSCOM, TASCOM, COMMZ, "rail net vulnerability to enemy penetration"). This is Goldratt-in-uniform: `Rail Ops Planning` is literally a course in **determining the bottleneck capacity of a rail network** ("Tonnage Capacity Determination," drawbar pull, line capacity). Everything a novelist needs to make a corps rail-movement officer sound real is here. Detail below.

**First, the device is cheaper than you framed it.** Since the officeholders are fictional under no-borrowed-furniture, we don't actually deviate from history by moving the cut-over — we only set the *phase* of our fictional alternation; the pattern (alternating nations, multi-year tours) stays historical furniture. **The option space is bigger than settled-vs-October**, so I forced four alternatives: (A) settled commander — the baseline to beat; (B) October cut-over, handover complete; (C) war *freezes* the handover — successor named, perhaps in the building, transition suspended mid-stride; (D) the *protagonist* is the new arrival, staff settled. C is the freshest and most paralyzing but flirts with command melodrama; D is the cheapest and puts "adrift" on the protagonist personally.

**Why B is my recorded lean**, with three interactions doing the work:

1. **The November season sharpens it into one sentence:** a commander who takes over in October took command *three weeks after the corps' autumn exercise* — he is the only man in the headquarters who missed the rehearsal for the war he must now fight. The staff rehearsed under the other man, in the other man's emphases. That's the confusion clause as an initial condition, not a contrivance.

2. **The persuasion engine inverts, and the inversion is fresher than the classic.** A settled commander must be argued out of settled views — standard Rogo-Peach. A new commander *must* rely on a staff he doesn't yet trust; the G-3 becomes the corps' institutional memory overnight — authority without rank, load-bearing by construction rather than by talent, which is exactly what the no-anointed-protagonist clause wants. Strongest asymmetry: a G-3 one year in post (long enough to know the corps, short enough to own nothing) serving a commander three weeks in post.

3. **The wargame already owns the parameter.** Recognition lag *is* the transition made mechanical — a new command team initializes with high `ca_recognition_days`, and campaign 1 priced each day of lag at 2–4 km of theater. The device and the instrument agree about what a green headquarters costs, which means the book's opening-act failures can be *derived*, not asserted.

Plus the nationality algebra: B with a GE→DA succession puts a Danish general over a half-German staff mid-mobilization with both capitals watching — and if the protagonist is the German G-3, every relationship in the book crosses the national boundary. The mirrored version is available by flipping the phase. This now forms a single coupled decision — year ↔ commander's nation ↔ protagonist's nation ↔ succession direction — that character sketches can settle in one pass.

One genre hazard flagged in the doc: new-commander-arrives is a war-fiction trope. The mitigation is discipline about what the device *is* — the initial condition of the confusion, never the plot. The doctrine stays the story.
