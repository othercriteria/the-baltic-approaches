# 15. Sequencing

The corps went back to the morning market on the seventeenth day with a bid unlike any it had made, and the difference was one word. It did not ask for a share. It asked for a sequence.

Vossen and Kjeldsen built it through the night of the sixteenth, with Aakjær's new architecture pinned above the desk and Lammers' mirror-ledger of the enemy's tonnage open beside it, and the build kept producing the same discovery from different directions: priced in the new currency — days added to the enemy's preparation — the theater's three eternal customers were not competitors at all. They were the same customer at different hours.

The mathematics of it had the cruel neatness of things seen late. The enemy's great effort needed two accumulations, not one: the dumps behind his land front, and the amphibious lift assembling in his Baltic ports — the invasion fleet that had pinned half the Danish army to beach watch since W-day, the threat priced daily at Karup against Zealand's open coast. Both accumulations rode the same tonnage system: the same rail lines to the coast, the same port machinery, the same closing sea. Which meant — Kjeldsen got there first, at 0300, with the grease pencil — that the theater did not have to choose between the land battle's deep bid, the navy's strike bid, and Zealand's insurance. It had to *order* them. Strike the loading ports and the assembling lift this week — while the fleet sat concentrated, before the weather scattered the photographic evidence — and one effort served every customer at once: the invasion threat burned at its moorings, which released the beach-watch brigades the land battle would need; the port machinery that loaded the lift was the same machinery that shipped the land front's saved-up tonnage westward, so every crane dropped was a day added to the enemy's preparation clock; and the closer his amphibious option came to impossible, the more completely his remaining hope loaded itself onto the one great land effort whose date the corps was already counting down on a wall.

"We have been asking the market to divide the fish," Vossen said, looking at the finished request in the gray of first light. "The fish was never divisible. It was sequential. This week the sea war *is* the land war, a week early."

The conference at Karup ran forty minutes that morning, and the corps attended as always as a voice on the line, and for once Vossen stayed at the air desk through the whole of it with the second handset to his ear, listening to the theater think.

He heard the claims made in their three currencies, as they had been made every morning of the war — hulls, kilometers, credibility. He heard the corps' liaison lay the sequence into it, in the fourth currency, the one with days in it, and he heard the quality of the pause that followed; and then he heard the theater commander at Karup — a tired Danish voice the corps knew only as a signature block — begin asking questions down the line in the new currency himself, converting each claimant's case into days-of-his-preparation as if testing a rate of exchange; and Vossen understood, listening, that the currency was doing the arguing. Nobody at that table had ever been able to weigh a sunk landing ship against a struck rail junction against a reassured government. Days, everyone could weigh.

The allocation came back at 1130: the theater's strike weight committed for seventy-two hours against the embarkation complex — ports, lift, and the coastal rail approaches — with the land battle's close support reduced to the load-bearing minimum, and a sentence appended in the dry Karup house style that Kjeldsen read aloud twice for the pleasure of it: FOLLOW-ON APPORTIONMENT WILL BE PHASED AGAINST THE ENEMY'S PREPARATION TIMELINE AS ASSESSED BY COMLANDJUT. The corps' wall had just been promoted to theater instrument.

The seventy-two hours that followed were, in the corps' war diary, somebody else's battle — squadron strikes through the broken November weather, the navy's fast boats and the mining effort weaving into it, losses taken over the Belts and the Bight that the corps could read only as numbers on the morning states — and Vossen, who had spent the war learning what his own numbers concealed, made the operations cell keep the other ledger honestly: not claims, but observables. The observables accumulated. Fires visible from Fehmarn burning through the second night. The lift's assembly anchorage dispersing, then reassembling smaller, then dispersing and not reassembling. Prisoner traffic and signals both speaking, for the first time, of embarkation formations moving *inland*. And on the wall, on Aakjær's THE SEA'S SCHEDULE sheet, the entry that quietly closed a theater question that had governed the Danish half of the war since the first chinagraph:

23 NOV: AMPHIB THREAT VS ZEALAND ASSESSED NO LONGER EXECUTABLE WITHIN REMAINING WEATHER WINDOW. RECOMMEND RELEASE OF EARMARKED FORCES TO FIELD ARMY TASKS.

The beach watch began to move on the twenty-fourth — brigades that had stood three weeks on the Zealand and Fyn coasts staring at an empty sea, now crossing the Belt bridge westward in the corps' direction with their frustration intact and their strength unbled: the first fresh formations the defense had gained since W-day, paid for entirely in someone else's sorties, purchased by sequencing. Lammers routed them by the crossing discipline like a man dealing himself aces he had earned.

And on the land front, through those same three days, the countdown instrument performed exactly as the fifteenth morning had promised. The counter-battery counts, posted daily now in a column beside THE DAY, fell — eleven hundred enemy rounds recorded on the seventeenth, nine hundred on the eighteenth, six-forty on the nineteenth — the enemy's guns going frugal on their fixed schedule, banking against their one great morning, each day's new silence moving the pencil line on Roloff's graph one day further down its slope toward the region, cross-hatched in red, where Lammers' dump-accumulation model and Aakjær's weather table intersected.

The staff watched the line descend with an emotion Vossen could not name until Aakjær, standing at the wall on the evening of the nineteenth day, named it for the record in his flattest voice:

"For the first time in my professional life I am watching an enemy obey my estimate. I feel I should thank him. Note the projection, Hr. Oberstleutnant: at present rates of quieting, his preparation completes between the twenty-sixth and the twenty-eighth. The moon is dark on the twenty-seventh." He capped his pen. "We have perhaps one week to become the corps that morning requires. And there remains" — the gray eyes went, not unkindly, toward the commander's office where the light still burned — "one officer in this headquarters who has not yet said aloud that he intends to spend his last three fresh brigades on an inference drawn on plywood. He will have to say it soon. Walls do not sign orders."
