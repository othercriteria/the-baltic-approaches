**What *The Mission* Is Actually About (In My Opinion)**

When I started this book I thought it was going to be a war novel with battles in it, and technically it is, but honestly it is mostly a book about meetings. It takes place in 1983 in Denmark and northern Germany, and the main character is a Danish staff officer named Holt who works at a NATO headquarters when World War III breaks out. I expected tanks and explosions, and there are some, but almost everything happens in an operations room where officers argue about numbers. The weird part is that by the end I think that's the whole point. The book is about how an army can have every number "green" on its charts and still be losing, because it's measuring the wrong things. A retired old general named Rahn asks Holt at a party, basically, "what is your corps FOR?" and Holt can't answer it, and the whole rest of the book is him figuring that out.

The thing that worked best for me was the wall. Partway through the war, the staff starts writing down every prediction they make and then what actually happened next to it, so they can't lie to themselves. Like "ESTIMATE: crossing restored in 24 hours. ACTUAL: 41." The soldiers nickname it the confession wall. That could be boring but it's actually kind of the main character of the book. There's a scene near the end where Holt's own decision goes wrong (he sends artillery fire to the west side when the real attack was coming on the east side, and a battalion loses its position by three minutes, the report literally says fires granted 1213, position lost 1210) and he has to stand in front of the wall he built while it basically convicts him. I didn't expect a book about spreadsheets to make me feel bad for a guy, but it did.

I'll be honest about what I skimmed. There's a whole chapter about a morning conference where three groups bid for airplanes like it's a fish market, and another one about ferry schedules, and I read those the week we had our tournament so they kind of blurred together. I also had trouble telling some of the officers apart. There's Lammers (supplies guy?), Kjeldsen (air force guy), Witzel (paperwork guy), and Aakjær, the sarcastic intelligence officer, and for the first hundred pages I mixed up which was which because they're all colonels with hard names. Aakjær ended up being my favorite though — he has a speech about how his mother-in-law lives eleven minutes from a beach the enemy might invade, and her whole "evacuation plan" is a government pamphlet that says fill the bathtub, and she keeps refilling it weekly because she already lived through one occupation. That detail stuck with me more than most of the battles. Also confusing: the chapters that switch to the enemy side, with a Polish general (Zawadzki, I think) and his chief of staff Rylski. At first I didn't get why they were there, but their story mirrors the main one — Rylski proves with fuel receipts that their army is five days behind schedule, and at the very end he gets rewarded for his honest bookkeeping by having it used as evidence against his own commander, which is genuinely dark.

The most powerful scene for me was small. Early in the war, a message comes in from some middle-aged reservists defending a bridge, asking which headquarters they're actually dying for, "respectfully, sirs, decide." The book keeps doing that — reminding you that the numbers are people. Holt's coworker Roloff has to process the evacuation paperwork for the column that contains his own wife and kids, and he just initials it and keeps working, and Holt says nothing except confirming the column arrived safely. Nobody cries or gives a speech. I think the author trusts you to feel it, which mostly works, although sometimes I wished someone WOULD give a speech, because the emotional stuff is so buried you can miss it if you're reading fast (which, sometimes, I was).

So did it work for me? Mostly yes, with reservations. It's slow, the names are a workout, and if you skim you get lost because the plot is basically logistics. There's no single villain and honestly not that much "action" for a war book. But the ending earns it. The final report the corps writes says "We were not right. We were solvent" — meaning they never actually predicted anything correctly, they just kept honest books about being wrong and paid their debts on time. That's a strange moral for a war novel, but it feels more true than "the good guys were smarter." I don't know if I'd reread it, but I've thought about the confession wall during actual school assignments, like when I tell myself an essay is "basically done." Scheduled: Tuesday. Actual: not yet achieved. So I guess the book worked.
