**REVIEW: *The Mission* (unpublished manuscript, ~60k words) — WWIII on the Baltic flank, fought with a grease pencil**

A friend slipped me this draft with the warning "it's not Team Yankee." He undersold it. This is a Third World War novel about LANDJUT — the Danish-German corps at Rendsburg that every one of us who ever ran the Northern Flank on a map has treated as an off-board holding box while the real game happened in the Fulda Gap. The author has written the box.

**What it is**

November 1983 — KAL 007 autumn, the Bonn marches, Pershing deployment pending — and a Warsaw Pact conventional offensive up the neck of Jutland. The protagonist, Oberstløjtnant Niels Holt, is the corps G-3, and the book never once leaves the operational level. The opening chapter is an ENDEX: a captain razoring nine days of exercise chinagraph off the plexiglass, and a departing German general asking four questions the scoring system has no columns for ("The forty minutes at Hohn... I asked what it *cost*"). That cold open is the whole book in miniature. The war, when it comes, is the exercise's questions collecting their debts.

And here I have to name the thing nobody else on this forum will: this is Goldratt's *The Goal* in field gray. The bottleneck (a bridge at Friedrichstadt passing 290 vehicles an hour with eleven-second gaps of empty plank), the mentor who refuses to answer (retired General Rahn, an unapologetic Jonah figure — "Name the good. One."), the constraint that moves the moment you relieve it (plank-seconds, then shells, then sleep), even the explicit "what is this corps *for*" catechism. The mentor-who-only-asks-questions is a genre trick and I'm naming it. It also *works*, because the author does what Goldratt never had to: he charges the protagonist for the theory. More below.

**Checking the arithmetic**

I did what I always do — ran the numbers the text lets you run — and the manuscript survives it, which almost none of the genre does.

The mobilization race is the real GDP shape: 6th Panzergrenadier at 51 hours, Jutland Division at 90, enemy attack at H+66, division actually closed at H+180. The great scene in Chapter 3 — Lammers pointing at "ELEMENTS 1 JYSKE BRIGADE (RECCE SQN, ADV PARTY) CROSSED LILLEBÆLT 072240" as "the war's first honest sentence" while the depot intake boards glow green — is the best dramatization of the input-metrics fallacy I've read anywhere, fiction or JOPES. Brauer's 1,100 tons for forty minutes of massed fires pencils out to about 1.5 rds/min/tube across a two-division corps' artillery — fine. The bridge throughput (290/hr to 410 at half spacing, claimed "forty percent" gain) is internally consistent. The Sea's Schedule sheet — amphib window closing 8–12 December, port restoration ≥10 days, decision space 7–11 days — foots exactly against its own dates. The casualty meter's two denominations (80–140/hour; "a company's fighting strength every ninety minutes") roughly reconcile. Civil twilight at 0742 in late November at Schleswig's latitude: correct, and I checked. One ding: Aakjær's "the moon is dark on the twenty-seventh" — my almanac makes 27 Nov 1983 a last-quarter moon, rising before the pre-dawn H-hour it matters for. Somebody verify.

The scene where Hvidt interrogates the 1:4.2 exchange ratio down to its 1979 umpire-calibrated correction factor — "And this morning's umpires are where?" — should be laminated and issued to every J2 on earth.

Where the book leans on invention, it mostly says so. The load-bearing premise — that the whole Pact operation forecloses without a working Baltic port before the December gales — is the manuscript's one big gimme. The western Baltic doesn't "close" in any hard sense; the amphib-window argument is defensible, the sea-as-creditor rhetoric pushes past it. The text half-knows this: the after-action audit admits the sea's schedule was wrong by four days. The Great Belt ferry lift in week three also crosses more unmolested than Baltic Fleet air should permit, though the book has at least burned the enemy's air effort down plausibly by then.

**The other side of the hill**

Here's where the manuscript beats its 1980s ancestors on published-since evidence: the enemy is the **Polish Coastal Front** — Zawadzki and his chief of staff Rylski — with a Soviet theater representative sitting silently in the corner, which is what the actual Pact planning documents (the ones we got after 1991) say the Denmark job looked like. Hackett and Clancy gave us monolithic Soviets; this gives us Rylski reconstructing his Front's true positions *backward from fuel consumption* because "a tank that reports its position optimistically still burns diesel from where it actually is." Chapter 11, where he proves his own Front five days behind and his commander declines to receive the conclusion, is Peters' *Red Army* territory and holds its own there.

The best intel beat in the book is Chapter 14: Aakjær discovering there never were echelons — "I mistook a traffic jam for a doctrine. That is not an army forming in waves. That is an army arriving in a queue." That's a real analytic controversy of the period (the echelonment-versus-flow argument) dramatized better than the professional literature ever managed. And the paired nuclear chapters — NATO's locked folder walked once and shut (Ch. 6), Kuragin's folder considered for eleven minutes and refused *on logistical grounds* ("the prize is downwind of the means") in Chapter 17 — are the most honest treatment of theater release I've seen in fiction. Both refusals are bookkeeping, not sentiment. That's correct, and it's chilling in a way mushroom-cloud chapters never are. One loose thread: a two-line report of "unconfirmed employment" in the CENTAG sector appears mid-book and is never resolved. Bold background or dropped stitch — the revision needs to decide.

**What it refuses, and whether the refusals work**

Be warned: there is not one scene inside a tank. No cockpit, no CIC, no sonar shack. Hardware is nearly noun-free — no Leopards, no T-72s, just "tonnage" and "tubes," which is authentically how a G-3 sees it and will cost the book half its natural audience. Combat arrives as *formats*: the Heimatschutz company at Reinfeld asking "which of you we are dying for, respectfully, sirs, decide"; the hinge company's entire battle rendered as an eleven-line radio log ending in HOLDING, then silence ("The silence was not the end of the company. It was the end of the radio"). I have never seen the genre do this, and it hits harder than any autoloader porn I've read. The tension instruments are countdowns and meters — the counter-battery count dropping 1,100 → 900 → 640 → 410 as the enemy banks shells for his last throw is genuinely nail-biting, *because the quieter he gets, the closer he is*.

The other thing I've never seen: the decisive instrument is allowed to fail. The wall's model misses the enemy crest by fourteen hours; Holt signs the computation anyway, with the dissent pinned to it, and Loheide's division pays the difference at a posted rate. The error ledger — the F-series, three of seven entries carrying the protagonist's initials — goes into the record over a theater staffer's silky objections, and the final battle of the book is *an editorial fight over an annex*. "We were not right. We were solvent." When did this genre last have the nerve to end there?

**Pacing and gripes**

Chapters 1–3 are a slow burn that rewards patience; 5 (the two-column graph paper), 8 (the bridge), 11, 12 (the narrows — the one conventional battle chapter, fought entirely from the plexiglass, containing Holt's costly wrong call on the fires release), 14, and 17 are superb. The air-market chapters (9, 15) sag slightly on their second trip to Karup. The chapter numbered "19a" sits *before* 19 in the draft — a flash-forward that half-works and half-reads as a collation error. And the manuscript's one systemic flaw: everyone talks the same. Aakjær, Rahn, Hvidt, Witzel, a battalion commander quoting his grandfather's banker in Kolding — every officer in two armies delivers polished aphorisms on demand. By Chapter 16 it's a war between two faculty senates. Sorkin syndrome. Real staffs contain at least one fool, and this book has none on either side.

**Where it sits on the shelf**

Above Hackett for prose, below him for scope. Nowhere near Clancy for hardware and unashamed of it. Alongside Peters for the enemy chapters. Coyle's readers will bounce off it at speed. Its actual nearest relatives aren't in our genre at all — it's van Creveld's *Supplying War* and Goldratt with a plot, the first WWIII novel whose subject is *measurement itself*. The rivet-counters will find maybe five rivets to count, and four of them are correct.

Recommended without reservation for the operationally minded. 8.5/10 as drafted; a voice pass and a decision about 19a gets it to 9.
