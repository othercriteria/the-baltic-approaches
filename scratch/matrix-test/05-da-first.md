# 5. Green Against What

Belt BRAVO goes in the night of the ninth, and before morning I have learned two things wearing the same uniform: what our second defensive belt is worth, and what our reporting is worth. It takes me most of the night to see they are one lesson. I keep filing them separately.

The night starts with the demolitions. From the operations room the belt is forty-one prepared targets on an overlay — road craters, three rail overpasses, the Treene bridges, culverts sown down the secondary routes like buttons on a coat. And the execution is a triumph by every number we keep: forty of forty-one fired, confirmed, logged with the two times the commander demands, scheduled against actual, and for once the two columns nearly agree. I watch the acknowledgments come in over the net — English on the boards, because the corps works in English, the one language two armies can share — and I feel the room lift with each one, and I cannot fault it. The engineers have done in freezing rain under fire what they rehearsed for years, almost perfectly, and a man is entitled to his competence. I let myself have the feeling. I am wrong to.

By 0400 the enemy is across the Treene in two places.

Not because the demolitions failed. The picture assembles itself the way night pictures do — wrong twice before it is right — and what it shows me is that the enemy did not treat the river as an obstacle to be repaired. He treated it as a scheduled event. The bridging reaches the water inside ninety minutes of the last demolition. Corps-level bridging, far forward, in quantities the intelligence annex listed faithfully and nobody imagined *concentrated*. And while our gunners fight the bridge sites, his infantry crosses elsewhere in assault boats and rubber rafts and, at one logged location, wading — in November, in ranks, weapons held over their heads, taking the kind of losses the covering force's observers report in tones of disbelief, and coming on.

Aakjær reads the night's summaries at 0530 with the fixed attention he saves for enemy behavior that confirms something. He says it in Danish, quietly, the two of us in the corner of a German-run room. *Han betaler mænd for at spare timer.* He is paying men to save hours.

"Every other army haggles," he says. "This one has a price list. And the price list says an hour is worth more than a company. It is not brutality, Niels, whatever the forward battalions think this morning. It is arithmetic. Arithmetic we have not been shown the rest of."

The staff's answer to the crossing is in the plan. The plan's answer is BELT CHARLIE and the third trade of ground for time. And at 0600 the plan's answer meets the other thing — the thing that has been waiting in our paperwork since the first chinagraph went up. The line on the map that is not a military line.

It has a name in the annex. *The political guidance line*, coordinated Bonn–Copenhagen–Brussels, updated by diplomatic note, running the general trace Husum–Schleswig–Eckernförde. Its language is a small masterpiece: *forward of this line, ground will not be voluntarily relinquished except under military necessity, the determination of which is reserved.* Reserved to whom, it does not say. In BOLD LANCER the umpires ruled the question out of play. Now it is 0600 on the tenth of November, the covering force is asking to conduct its passage of lines behind CHARLIE — behind, that is, the northern shoulder of the guidance line — and the question rings three capitals awake, and the corps stands still for five hours while it is answered.

Five hours. I spend them doing a G-3's job, which this morning means holding two divisions' worth of movement in the air, unsigned. The military logic is one page: the covering force is spent, his bridging is across, and every hour of delay in the passage of lines gets paid at the rate the night just set. A company an hour. The political logic is also one page. Hvidt spends the five hours on the secure line to Karup, his hand flat on the desk, and afterward he puts it to us without a syllable of editorial, which is its own comment. He does it in English, for the whole staff. I translate it into Danish inside my head as he speaks, and it does not resolve any better in Danish.

"Copenhagen and Bonn require the line held forward of Schleswig until the civil movement is —" he consults, deliberately, the exact words — "*substantially complete*. I asked what substantially means. I was told it is being determined. Gentlemen: the corps will hold forward of the guidance line until 1800 tomorrow at the earliest. The military necessity clause is not available to us today. That is an order I have accepted. It is therefore my order." He stands a moment. "Cost it," he says to me, and goes.

*Cost it.* Two English words, and they land on the room like a phrase from a third language. I reach for the Danish and it will not come clean — *gør det op*, settle it, tot it up — none of it fits, because we have a whole directorate for costing things. Lammers' people cost fuel, tubes, track-miles, bridge tonnage. The thing the commander wants priced is an *order*. Eighteen more hours forward of a line the trade says to leave, expressed in what the hours buy against what they burn. We have no form for it. That is the discovery of the afternoon, and it frightens me more than the crossing did. We can report readiness, strength, expenditure, position — every noun in the inventory — and we cannot answer the commander's actual question, which is about *exchange*. What are we spending. What are we getting. Whether the two are written in any currency a man could add.

Roloff builds the first answer that evening on two sheets of graph paper taped together, because there is no form and he will not wait for one. Down the left, our spendables, the night's real rates: companies rendered ineffective per twelve hours forward of CHARLIE; tube ammunition per twelve hours at the current fire program; the two crossing sets the enemy has not yet forced. Down the right, our buyables — and here the sheet goes thin, because what the eighteen hours buy stands in three ledgers kept by three staffs. Kilometres of civil column cleared eastward off route B76, the Territorial Command's number, telephoned, approximate. Danish brigades closed south of the Belt — Lammers' number, exact, shrinking in usefulness by the hour as the roads fill. And at the bottom, in Aakjær's own hand, unquantified, flagged with a question mark: *time bought against the arrival of the enemy's REAL mass — value unknown, since we have not located it.*

I read the right-hand column and I understand, in a way I have kept from myself all day, what one of those lines is. *Danish brigades closed south of the Belt.* That is my country arriving — the mobilization closing behind Lillebælt. Kirsten is in Fredericia with the children, behind the Little Belt, behind the water, and all day I have told myself she is safe because she is behind the Belts. Here on Roloff's ragged sheet is the truth I preferred not to price: the Belts are only a line until the brigades close them. Until then Fredericia is an assumption. My home is not in the cost column, the way a German officer's would be — his own district in the evacuation. Mine is in the buy column, the thing the eighteen hours are for. I do not know whether that is mercy or distance. I know only that I am spending companies I can count to purchase a line my family lives behind, and that I would not have seen it if Roloff had not run out of room on the page.

I take the taped sheets to Hvidt at 2200 and watch him read them a long time.

"This is the page I asked whether existed," he says at last. "It did not. Now it half exists." He taps the right-hand column, the ragged one. "Everything we spend, we can count. Almost nothing we buy has a number, and the numbers it has are owned by three headquarters and a police president. Put it on the wall. Not the tidy boards. This one. I want every officer who briefs me to have walked past it."

I telephone Eckernførde near midnight, on the civil exchange, from the small office with the coffee ring on the map of the Baltic Approaches. I could not say cleanly why. Only that the taped sheets have a shape, and the shape reminds me of a question I failed to answer at a party in Hamburg three weeks ago, and there is nobody in this headquarters I can say that to. Rahn was my instructor at the Führungsakademie — I was the allied student, the Dane in the back row. He answers on the sixth ring, unsurprised, as if eighty-two-year-old generals sit by their telephones at midnight in the second week of a war — which, I think, they possibly do.

We speak German. It is my third language of the day and it comes to me tired, exact, the seminar German he drilled into me.

"Holt. You are calling from a headquarters at war on an open line, so you will say nothing of substance and I will ask nothing. Good. Then we can speak of the only thing worth speaking of." A chair creaks in Eckernførde. "You have come to tell me the answer to my question."

"I have come to tell you," I say, and the sentence assembles itself out of the graph paper, "that I know what we are spending. To the decimal. And that I cannot state what we are buying — not to the decimal, at all — because everything we buy is written in someone else's ledger, in someone else's unit, and half of it in ledgers I am not permitted to see. And that tonight I priced eighteen hours, on the order of a man I am starting to respect, and the two columns will not balance, because they are not in the same currency."

The line hisses. When he speaks there is something new in the voice, and it takes me a moment to name it as satisfaction, strictly held down.

"Three weeks," he says. "Faster than I was. — No. I will not give you the answer, Holt; be serious; if I give it to you, you will write it in an annex and it will die there. I will give you the shape, and you will do the work, because the work *is* the answer. Listen. An enterprise that cannot say what it produces will always — always, in war, in a factory, in a marriage — fall back on measuring what it consumes. Because consumption is countable, and production requires you to have decided what you are *for*. Your columns will not balance because you are trying to buy an unnamed good with countable spending. So. Name the good. One good. Not a list — the moment it is a list, you have decided nothing. And when you have named it, ask that ragged right-hand column one question: which line, if it went to zero, would make all the other lines worthless?" A pause; the chair again. "When you can answer that, telephone me, and we will discuss what your headquarters should stop doing. It will be a long list, and it will be the first cheerful conversation of your war. Good night, Herr Oberstløjtnant. My regards to the graph paper."

He rings off. I sit a moment in the small office, hearing the operations room through the wall — teleprinters, the low traffic of the night shift in three languages, a war being administered — and then I take out my notebook and write, under the day's two-times entries, four words. I write them in English. Not in Danish, which is where I think, and not in German, where he said them to me — in English, the tongue the corps shares, because a good that only one of two armies can name is not yet named at all. I draw a box around them the way Roloff boxes his totals.

*Name the good. One.*

*Renderer's note. The two swaps do not compound; they trade. First-person-present dissolves the close-third irony — I cannot watch Holt fail to see the day's unity, so I make him confess the not-seeing in real time ("I keep filing them separately"), which is a different, more anxious pleasure and a thinner one. The nationality swap, though, is where present tense earns its keep: the "cost it" beat and the notebook climax become live acts of translation — Danish-thought, English-worked, German-mentored — that past-tense reportage would have smoothed flat. What this cell uniquely gains is the buy-column reversal: Vossen's home is a cost he holds; Holt's is the good the cost purchases, safe behind the Belts only until the brigades close them. That is a stronger thematic rhyme with "name the good" than the original owns. What it loses is the original's held-breath restraint — first person cannot resist narrating its own epiphany, and Holt says aloud what Vossen was permitted to leave in the graph paper.*
