# 6. The Locked Door

The folder existed. That was the thing Vossen had known for thirteen months the way a man knows the wiring in his walls, and had to look at, for the first time, on the fifth day of the war.

It was gray, it lived in the second drawer of the safe in the plans cell, and its cover sheet bore a NATO classification, a national caveat, and a title of perfect bureaucratic composure: REQUEST PROCEDURES, SPECIAL WEAPONS RELEASE — FORMATS AND CHANNELS. Inside were forms. Message skeletons, half-filled: FROM COMLANDJUT TO COMBALTAP, prefixed fields for date-time group and situation summary, blank lines of a specific length for the military justification. A staff at war maintains its drafts the way a household maintains insurance. On the fifth day, in the course of the daily plans review, Major Roloff — following the commander's standing order that every document in the cell carry its two times — asked, with no more weight than he gave the engineer stores return, whether the situation summary paragraph of the draft request should be updated to reflect the loss of the Treene line.

The plans cell went quiet in a way that had physics to it.

"No," said Vossen, and heard how loud it wasn't. "The draft is not a living document. File unchanged."

But it was too late, in the useful way; the question had been asked in a room, and questions asked in rooms have to be answered somewhere, and Hvidt — told of it that evening, because Vossen told him everything now, that had become the working arrangement of the war — looked at the two of them, the G-3 and the G-2, summoned after the 1800 conference, and said: "Good. It is the fifth day and the subject is in the building. I would rather it stood in this office for one hour than lived in the corridors for three weeks. Walk me through it. Both trees, theirs and ours, from the root. Once. Thoroughly. And then we lock the door and we do not open it again, because a headquarters that discusses this weekly is a headquarters that has already begun to lose."

So they walked it, in the commander's office with the map of the Approaches and the bad coffee, and Vossen remembered the hour afterward not as dread but as a kind of terrible bookkeeping — the most orderly hour of the war so far. Which was, he understood later, the point of it.

Aakjær took the enemy's tree, because the enemy's tree was his trade. He built it from the floor: not from intentions, which he refused like a workman refusing bad timber, but from behavior with receipts. Item. The enemy had attacked conventionally, on a front where every doctrinal template the corps possessed — captured manuals, defector studies, twenty years of exercise analysis — gave him a faster opening written in a different ink. "He owns a quicker timetable. We have taught it to our own officer courses for a decade. He did not use it. A man who takes the stairs when he owns the lift is telling you something about the building." Item. The enemy's fire plan had been, by the standards of his own norms, abstemious around precisely the things that made the theater worth having: port machinery at Flensburg shelled with what the damage assessments suggested was deliberate care; the Kiel Canal locks untouched — untouched, on day five, when every analysis said their destruction would cost the defense a week — the bridges bombed at their approaches, recoverable, and not at their piers. "He is not breaking the shop window. He intends to trade in the shop. That is a limited-aims signature, Hr. General; it is the most reassuring thing on my board and I hate it, because it is reassurance resting entirely on his continuing to believe his plan is working." Item — and here Aakjær paused, and chose the words with tongs — "and his slower timetable and his careful fires are one decision, not two. In his own planning culture, as far as we understand it from the documents we hold, the quick variant and the special weapons arrive bundled. Declining one is declining both. Which is why —" the tongs again — "the answer to Major Kjeldsen's question of Tuesday, which I am aware has gone around the building, *why no gas, when every assessment promised gas* — the answer is not mercy and not fear of our masks. The answer is bookkeeping. His. He has chosen the ledger in which those columns do not appear. Our protective posture nonetheless stays as it is, and we pay its tax — the masks, the rotations, the eleven percent we lose on every timed drill — because we are defending against his choices, not his ledger, and choices can be remade."

"By whom," said Hvidt. Not idly; it was the hinge question, and he asked it like a man putting his hand on the hinge.

"Not by anyone in front of us," Aakjær said. "That is the one comfort in the entire subject. The formations fighting us do not own those weapons and never have. The request would go up out of this theater entirely, to a level where our peninsula is one line in a different calculation. Their lift shaft has more floors than ours, Hr. General, and the button is further from the fingers that are bleeding."

"And ours," said Hvidt, and turned to Vossen.

Vossen took the corps' own tree, and found, walking it aloud, that it was shorter and worse. The gray folder was its root. The channel ran upward through Karup and Kolsås to committees whose composition was itself classified, through a consultation procedure whose duration no exercise had ever dared to time honestly, wearing a Danish government footnote like a chain on its ankle — and every station of it was, deliberately, slow. "The procedure is the policy," Vossen said. "It was built slow so that no one could arrive at the end of it in the heat of a bad week. Which means the request only makes sense if it is begun early, before the need is proven — and beginning it early is itself an act, Hr. General. It would be seen. It would mean the corps had stopped believing in its own conventional defense. Every officer who touched the paperwork would know it, and within a day the divisions would know it, and the corps would begin to fight like a formation waiting to be rescued." He heard, as he said it, the reader-contract of his whole profession snap into place around the gray folder like a frame. "The conclusion I would offer stands upside down from where I expected it. The folder is not our escape from conventional failure. Keeping the folder shut is a conventional mission. Every day the line holds is a day nobody in three capitals is asked the question none of them can answer well. If the corps has an unwritten task above all the written ones, it is that: to make the question unnecessary. We are not defending Schleswig-Holstein with those weapons in reserve. We are defending three governments from their own decision tree."

The office was silent. On the map the Approaches lay in their patient blues and greens, ports and locks and bridges, the shop the enemy intended to trade in.

"And so both doors are locked," Hvidt said at last, "and both keys are visible, and each side's best reason for leaving its own door shut is its belief about its own position — his, that his plan is working; ours, that our defense is holding." He stood, which was how his conferences ended. "Then the operational conclusions are two, and I want them written nowhere and understood everywhere. First: the enemy's restraint is rented, and the rent is his own timetable. Our business is to put him behind it — and gentlemen, note what follows: the better we do our work, the longer his patience is asked to last. There will come a week when his staff walks his tree the way we have walked ours tonight. What arrives at the end of that week I cannot control. I can control whether his answer to it profits him. Second: this headquarters now stops discussing the subject. Herr Oberstleutnant, the folder's two times are the time it was filed and the word never. Questions of the Kjeldsen type get Aakjær's answer, once, and then get silence." He looked at them both, and for the first time since the parade square the flat Danish composure opened a centimeter, and what stood behind it was not fear; it was a man carrying a locked box and declining to shake it to hear the rattle. "Thank you. It is the most useful hour I have spent in this war, and we will not spend it again."

---

Afterward, past midnight, Vossen and Aakjær stood a moment in the corridor under the blue nightlights, two staff officers in a headquarters gone quiet, and it was the Dane who said it, in his flat murmur, in the shared language nobody could be eloquent in:

"You noticed what we did in there. We answered the General's first question at last — your general's question, the old one from the reception, I have heard the story." A thin smile. "Nine days of exercise, and what is the corps for. It took a locked drawer to say it. We are for making his schedule fail while ours does not — everything else on the boards is expenditure. So." He pushed himself off the wall, courteous, gray, already walking toward his traffic. "Now we are obliged to learn what his schedule actually is, and what ours actually is, since as of tonight both are load-bearing. I will start with his. Yours, Hr. Oberstleutnant, is on graph paper on the operations room wall, and I am told the right-hand column is still ragged."

*Name the good. One.* Vossen stood under the nightlights and found the box in his notebook filled at last, in the enemy's ink and his own: the good was time — not time in general, the annex-word, but a specific, purchasable, spendable thing: the distance between two schedules. Theirs, failing. Ours, holding. One currency after all.

Now: who owned it. And what, in God's name, was it costing a day.
