# 16. The Bank

The building of the counterstroke force appeared in no single order. It accreted, the way the staff had learned to do everything now, as a discipline of refusals — and the refusals, entered daily in the corps' reports in the standard formats, produced in the following days a picture that every headquarters above Rendsburg was trained to read as decline.

Ground was given at the margins. Not much — the funnel's geometry was the funnel's geometry — but where the line's forward trace bought nothing but its own defense, Hvidt let it come back, village by village, to spare the battalions that held it, and the daily situation reports registered each adjustment in the column that measured the war the old way: TERRAIN VOLUNTARILY RELINQUISHED. The corps' activity indices fell. Fires refused, patrols economized, the artillery's daily expenditure dropping down the same deliberate slope as the enemy's — two frugalities facing each other across the Danewerk, banking against different mornings. On the plywood at Rendsburg this was THE DAY's arithmetic, posted and understood. In the formats that went upward to Karup and Kolsås and onward to two capitals, it was a corps going passive while an enemy prepared, and by the twentieth day the signals coming back down had acquired the tone the staff had been braced for: requests for clarification. Then requests for intentions. Then — relayed through BALTAP with a Danish dryness that did not conceal the weight behind it — a question from the political level, asked almost in the annex's own voice: whether the corps was aware that ground relinquished had a meaning in negotiations that ground held did not.

Hvidt answered them all in the wall's currency, patiently, repetitively, like a man teaching a bank to read a balance sheet — assets banked against a dated intention, the enemy's preparation curve enclosed with each reply, the same sentence recurring until it became the headquarters' catechism: *the corps is not declining combat; it is scheduling it.* But he confided to Vossen, signing the third such reply in two days, the cost that the catechism did not carry: "Every one of these I sign spends trust I have banked nowhere. Three weeks is not long enough to owe me a morning like the one I am asking them to underwrite. If the day comes late, or comes wrong, the file of these signals becomes the case against the man who hoarded a corps while the front bled." He blotted the signature, methodical. "Note that I am not asking for sympathy, Herr Oberstleutnant. I am teaching you the last course in the syllabus. The wall prices everything except what the commander pays. There is no instrument for that column. There is only the date."

The force itself grew in the woods and villages west of the canal with a husbandry that Lammers ran like a religion. The two Zealand brigades, crossing westward — fresh, full, humiliated by three weeks of empty horizon and desperate to spend it — were broken of their eagerness and hidden; the three rested battalion groups came up to strength with the last of the replacement flow; the tank transporters worked shuttle by night; the banked shells crossed the thousand-vehicle mark, then the fifteen-hundred. Vestergaard got the command — the Jutland Division's staff to run the counterstroke, the division that had closed late, bled at the narrows, and earned its arithmetic the hard way — with Loheide's worn regulars holding the funnel's jaw, and the choice, which the staff had expected to be politically national, Hvidt made in one sentence at the evening conference: "The division that has paid the most for our education will conduct the examination."

And the routes for it — the assembly areas, the approach marches, the report lines of the whole banked intention — ran, because geography is indifferent to delicacy, through the evacuated Kreis. Through Schleswig. Vossen laid the movement overlay across the town on the twentieth night and found himself assigning a brigade hide to the Friedrichsberg school — Assembly Point 14, Column 217's school, four hundred meters from his own front door — and a report line, in the neutral bird-names of the corps' convention, down the street where his children had learned to ride bicycles. He wrote the annex in the plain dialect the war had taught him, both times on every line, and if his hand paused once over the traced streets, the pause is not recorded in any document, which was, he had come to understand, what the documents were *for*.

Hvidt ordered him to the rest billet on the twenty-first day, for six hours, in writing, with the two times on the order. "The wall says sleep is ammunition," the commander said. "You wrote the wall. Comply with it."

The billet was Marei's mother's flat in Rendsburg, eleven kilometers and twenty-one days from the house inside the annex. He arrived without warning in the middle of the afternoon, in a staff car he sent straight back, and the three hours that followed put no strain on the corps' communications security because almost nothing was said in them that the war could have used. Marei had the radio on low, as instructed by a voice on a Sunday that neither of them mentioned. The children were at the school the receiving Kreis had improvised in a gymnasium. She made coffee that was mostly chicory, and asked no questions of substance, and told him no fears of substance, and they sat at her mother's small table like two adults of a serious country, holding what could be held. Only at the door, in the stairwell smelling of other families' cooking, did she put one finger on his chest, on the map pocket, and deliver the entire domestic front's assessment of the campaign in eleven words:

"You look like a man who has finally found out what he does." And, having inspected the finding the way the umpires used to inspect a defense: "Good. Finish it."

He was back at the headquarters by 1900. On the wall, the counter-battery count for the day had been posted — four hundred ten, the lowest of the war; the enemy's guns nearly silent now, his bank nearly full, the red cross-hatched region on Roloff's graph beginning at the twenty-sixth — and beside it Aakjær had pinned the evening's last intercept summary with a single annotation in the margin. The summary reported bridging and assault-crossing equipment moving forward into the enemy's line opposite the narrows, by night, under radio silence broken only twice. The annotation said:

HE IS SPENDING FROM THE HOARD. THE DAY IS BOUGHT. — A.

Vossen stood before it, in the operations room's low nighttime hum, feeling the thing he had no instrument for and no longer needed one: two clocks, wound all the way, facing each other across a funnel of frozen ground. He took out his notebook, and against the day's date — both times, scheduled and actual, at last converging on the same number — he boxed the last entry of the corps' long apprenticeship:

*Everything the corps does now, it does on purpose.*
