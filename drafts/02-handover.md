# 2 — Handover

The corps changed commanders on the parade square at Rendsburg
on the twenty-fifth of October, in a wind off the canal that
took most of the ceremony away with it. Holt stood in the second
rank of the staff and watched two generals exchange a corps the
way careful men exchange a ladder, neither letting go until the
other had it. Of Brauer's farewell the wind left only fragments.
Of Generalløjtnant Anders Hvidt's first address it left, by some
acoustic accident, one whole sentence, delivered while the flags
stood straight out from their poles:

"— and I ask of the staff exactly what the staff is entitled to
ask of me, which is candor before comfort."

"Candor before comfort," Aakjær repeated in the corridor
afterward, tasting it. "In my experience of Copenhagen, that is
usually a decoration. We will see whether this one is
load-bearing."

Hvidt was fifty-six, tall, with a long Danish face that gave
nothing away and asked for nothing back; he had spent three
years in the Defense Command in Copenhagen and four before that
commanding on Zealand, and he had watched BOLD LANCER '83 from
three hundred kilometers and a desk. He had not seen the corps
fight, even on paper. The staff knew it, and knew he knew it,
and everyone was waiting to learn what he proposed to do about
it.

What he did was appear in the G-3 operations cell at ten past
seven on the Thursday morning, unannounced, alone, carrying the
movement annex of the General Defense Plan open to the Danish
division's concentration tables.

"Sit," he said, when Holt began to stand. "This is your office.
I am the visitor." He laid the annex on the desk and put one
finger on a line of figures. "Ninety hours. The division closes
south into its positions in Schleswig by ninety hours. Who owns
this number?"

"Herr General?"

"It is a simple question about property. Somewhere under
ninety hours there is a route table, and under the route table
a capacity figure for the roads and the crossings, and under
that an assumption about what else is on the roads. Each of
those was written by a man. I want to know whether the men are
watching their own numbers, or whether the numbers have been
orphaned — inherited by the plan as a whole, which is to say by
nobody. When a plan fails it is usually at an orphan."

Holt looked at the figure he had briefed without discomfort for
a year. "The route tables are Movement Control's, under the
G-4. The crossing capacities came from the engineer staff. The
civil traffic assumption—" He stopped, because he did not know
its author, and heard himself not knowing.

"Yes," Hvidt said, without triumph. "That one. Find its owner.
If he is dead or posted, appoint a new one. I read your
exercise assessment on the aircraft; it was well written, and
it is a transcript, and a transcript is a photograph of a
dance. I am not interested in how the corps danced in October.
I am interested in what must be true for this plan to work in
any month, and who is watching each of those things, by name."
He closed the annex and stood. "Thank you, Herr Oberstløjtnant."
He thanked people; Brauer never had. The staff did not yet know
what to make of that either.

---

The chief of staff made his own arrangements about the new
commander's questions. Brigadegeneral Ernst Witzel ran the
headquarters the way a good stationmaster runs a junction — by
the board, to the minute, with a conviction that everything,
including grief, had a proper channel — and on the Friday Holt
found in his morning folder a tasking that had gone from the
commander's office to the G-3 plans desk directly: Roloff, to
consolidate the plan's route and crossing assumptions into a
single annotated list by Monday.

He took it across the corridor. Witzel did not look up from his
signature file.

"Item three of this morning's taskings," Holt said. "Plans work,
routed past the acting G-3."

"Routed to the desk that will do the work," Witzel said. "The
commander asked the staff a question. The staff will answer at
its best speed."

"If the answer comes from the desk and not through the chair,
Herr General, then the chair is furniture. If the chair is
furniture, say so, and I will brief Major Roloff every morning
and sign nothing."

Witzel initialed one more page, squared the file, and looked up
for the first time. "The chair is not furniture. The chair is
vacant, Herr Oberstløjtnant, which is a different administrative
condition, and Bonn has been asked twice for the colonel who is
supposed to fill it, and Bonn is busy. Until Bonn is less busy,
the question of whether the deputy fills the chair or merely
sits in it is decided by the deputy, daily, in view of the
staff. This morning you have decided it one way. Continue." He
returned the tasking to Holt's side of the desk, drew a line
through the routing, and wrote through the G-3 above his
initials. It was the most Witzel ever said on the subject, and
it was, Holt understood later, the only welcome he got.

---

The assumptions ledger was built on the first Sunday of
November, at a table in the conference room, by three men and a
pot of coffee that outlasted them.

Hvidt had tasked it as one page: things the corps assumes it
will be given. Lammers arrived with a draft of six items and
the flat expression he wore over his best work; Witzel came with
the plan's annexes tabbed in three colors; Holt kept the pen,
because the commander had said the list would live in the
operations room and that made it G-3 property.

"Item one," Lammers said. "Requisitioned civilian tankers,
eleven hundred, for the fuel lift. The plan assumes them at
mobilization plus twenty-four hours. The requisition machinery
is a Land ministry in Kiel and, for the Danish vehicles, a
directorate in Copenhagen. I have never seen the two exercised
in the same year."

"Write: two owners, no rehearsal," Hvidt said from the end of
the table. He had said he would only listen; nobody had
believed him. "Item two."

Item two was cleared roads — the assumption, nowhere stated
and everywhere load-bearing, that the routes north of the canal
would carry military traffic at planning rates while the
civilian population of the border districts stayed home. Item
three was the Territorial screen: companies of reservists,
none of them the corps' to command, holding the covering
positions by mobilization plus thirty hours. Item four was
allied air from the first day. Item five was the weather. By
midafternoon the page held nine items, and the page was not
full, and Lammers said so.

"Leave the space," Hvidt said. "A full page would mean we had
found the bottom of our own credulity. I have never met the
staff that has." He stood, and stopped at the door. "Have it
photographed and posted where the duty officer briefs. Not the
tidy version. That one, with the crossings-out."

---

The readiness measures began the same week, and they began
cheap.

That was Hvidt's word for it. Every measure on the quiet list
was priced twice — once in readiness bought, once in political
noise made — and the list was run strictly in order of the
second price. Leave was recalled by telephone, individually,
without a signal that anyone could file. The ammunition
transfer to the forward sites went out as a routine exchange of
stocks, under the cover of the winter maintenance program. The
harbor survey party went to Esbjerg in civilian clothes with
the annual port-facilities inspection, three weeks early, which
the port authority did not remark on because the inspection was
never on time anyway.

And one measure did not go out at all.

It sat fourth on Lammers' priority list: pre-requisition of the
Great Belt ferry fleet — notice to the State Railways that on
proclamation the six road ferries and the rail ferries of the
Halsskov–Knudshoved run would pass to military movement
control. Lammers had priced its absence without being asked:
every hour of delay in the requisition after mobilization
began was an hour added to the tail of the Zealand brigades'
crossing, and the hours compounded, because the ferries' own
schedule was the only machine that could reabsorb them.

"And its presence," Hvidt said, "is priced in what?"

"In headlines, Herr General," said Witzel. "The Belt run is
the country's front door. Serve that notice and every
householder in the kingdom knows it by supper."

Hvidt looked at the item for a moment. "Defer it," he said.
"Note on the minute: deferred on my judgment, against the
G-4's stated price, to be revisited at each escalation. Herr
Oberstløjtnant — your pen. I want the price in the minute, in
Oberst Lammers' figures. If this comes back to cost us, it
will not be because the file was polite about it."

Holt wrote the minute, and the deferral, and the price. It was
the first paper of the war, though nobody called it that, and
it went into the folder without ceremony, a small gray decision
about ferries, correctly made, correctly priced, and quietly
compounding from that day at rates the file could not print.

---

Outside the headquarters the autumn got louder.

The airliner had been down since September, and the transcripts
of the fighter pilot's radio calls were still being excerpted
on the evening programs. The missile deployments were due in
December and half a million people had marched in Bonn in the
last week of October; Roloff's wife's sister had taken the
train from Schleswig to march with them, which Roloff reported
to nobody, and which the security section noted and filed
without comment, because it was that kind of autumn. In the
Baltic the enemy's autumn maneuvers had been extended twice.
The amphibious rehearsals off Rügen had ended, and the shipping
had not dispersed to its home ports; the railway traffic east
of Rostock had thickened week over week since August; and on
the second of November Aakjær stood up at the morning briefing
with the indicator board and said, in the tone of a man
declining to raise his voice on purpose, that the exercise
across the water was no longer shaped like an exercise.

"Sassnitz is running ferry schedules with no timetable
relationship to trade. The Bezirk hospitals around Rostock have
deferred civilian admissions — that is a mobilization measure,
gentlemen; it has no other reading. The Northern Fleet sailed
on Friday. Each of these has an innocent explanation. I have
been writing the innocent explanations for nine weeks, and this
morning I observe that the innocent readings now require more
assumptions than the other one."

Hvidt asked one question into the silence: "What are we looking
at that would look different if it were aimed at us?"

"Almost nothing, Herr General," Aakjær said. "That is the
property of the period we are in. The difference arrives later,
and when it arrives it is no longer information. It is
contact."

"Then the question changes," Hvidt said. "Not whether it is
real. What it costs to act as if it is, against what it costs
to be late. Herr Oberstløjtnant, the quiet list continues, next
items, same discipline. We will be irresponsible about nothing
except headlines."

On the fourth of November the Danish government recalled key
personnel from leave. On the fifth, the German territorial
command activated Phase One of the route measures on the
federal roads north of the canal, and the first traffic signs
went up in their gray covers. And at twenty to seven on the
Sunday morning, the sixth of November, Aakjær came into the
operations room without knocking, which he had never done,
and handed Holt the signal.

BALTAP WARNING ORDER. The alert measure the headquarters had
rehearsed receiving for thirty years followed at 0955, and a
German captain — the same one who had razored the exercise off
the plexiglass sixteen days before — wrote the date-time group
on the clean map in chinagraph, 060955Z NOV 83, in a hand
that was steady until the last figure.

Ninety hours, said the plan, from proclamation to a division
standing in Schleswig.

The clock on it had started. Nobody owned it yet. In the
operations room the day shift was coming on early, one by one,
called by nothing anyone had sent, hanging their coats on the
hooks with the particular quietness of men arriving at a
sickbed, and the morning's first returns were already coming up
the teleprinter—
