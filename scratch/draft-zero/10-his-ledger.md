# 10. His Ledger

By the tenth day the wall had a name. The night shift called it *die Beichtwand* — the confession wall — and the name traveled, as soldiers' names do, until even the Danish officers used it, gravely mispronounced, and Roloff pretended not to be proud.

It had grown to nine sheets. Scheduled against actual for the crossing, the closing, the convoys; projected against observed for the fire plans, the air programs, the engineer works; the movement log's photostats with their vehicle-hours; and at the center, in Roloff's draftsman hand, the master sheet he updated at 0500 and 1700 — every estimate the staff had committed to paper since W-day, each with its date, its author's initials, and its fate. The fates were the confession. ESTIMATE: CROSSING RATE RESTORED IN 24 HRS — ACTUAL: 41. ESTIMATE: EN ATTACK NORTH OF TREENE NOT BEFORE 15 NOV — ACTUAL: 14 NOV 0330. ESTIMATE: DEEP PROGRAM DEGRADES EN ARTY RESUPPLY 30 PCT — OBSERVED: NO MEASURABLE CHANGE FWD OF LÜBECK.

It had been Hvidt's two-times order once, a discipline imposed. Somewhere in the second week it had become something else — the staff had begun to *consult* it, the way you consult an instrument, before committing a new estimate; had begun, without anyone ordering it, to write the next projection while standing in front of the fates of the last ten. Aakjær, who blessed little, blessed it in his fashion: "It is the only document in the headquarters," he said, "that grows more honest as it grows longer. Everything else we produce grows more polished."

At 2130 on the seventeenth, between the evening conference and the night's first crisis, Vossen telephoned Eckernförde.

"You are calling more quickly this time," Rahn said. "Good. Report."

"The good is named." Open line; he built the sentences carefully, in the abstract dialect they had fallen into, a staff college of two. "One good, one currency, as instructed. And a wall of instruments that measures our own side of it — spending and buying, scheduled and actual. The wall works. It is changing how the staff thinks faster than any order I have seen issued." He paused. "And it has shown me the boundary of itself, which is what I am reporting. Every number on that wall is about *us*. The three decisions that matter most in the coming week all turn on numbers about the other man — what he has spent, what he has left, what his timetable demands of him — and for those, the house has no instruments at all. We borrow his numbers from doctrine. From the reference books. At interest."

"At interest," Rahn repeated. The chair creaked in Eckernförde. "Whose phrase?"

"Mine."

"Keep it. — So. You have discovered that you are playing against a hand you cannot see, and you telephone an old man to say so. Vossen, every staff officer since Gilgamesh has known the enemy's hand is hidden. That is not your discovery. Think. What did you actually build this week? Not the wall. The wall is plywood. What did you *institute*?" He did not wait; the impatience of a man with a fixed number of evenings left. "You instituted the confrontation of promises with outcomes. You began keeping books on your own headquarters' beliefs. Now hear the only thing I will give you tonight, and it is a question, as always, because answers die in annexes. *He also keeps books.* Somewhere east of you tonight there is a wall like yours — tidier, I promise; that culture builds tidy walls — and a man in front of it. What is written on it? Not what does he have, Vossen — your Danish colleague can count trucks better than the two of us. What does he *measure himself against*? A man is what he measures. An army doubly so. When you can write down the enemy's confession wall — the norm he is failing, in his own units of account, and by how much, and what his own doctrine tells him follows from failing it — then you will not need to see his hand. You will know what he fears is in it, which in war is the better knowledge, because men play their fears, not their cards." The line clicked, hollow; somewhere an exchange fell over to another route. "Also, practically: intact things. Your enemy has been strangely gentle with certain expensive things, I read between the lines of the radio. What a man refuses to break, he has promised to somebody. Promises have dates. Find the date. — There. Two questions instead of one; inflation reaches Eckernförde. Good night, Herr Oberstleutnant."

Vossen wrote it on the pad before the receiver was cold: *His confession wall. His norm, his shortfall, in his units. What he refuses to break, he has promised — promises have dates.*

He took it, not to Roloff, but across the corridor. Some doors you learn to knock on late. Aakjær was at his own boards with the intercept summaries in the small hours, and he listened to the whole recitation without moving, and then stood for a while looking at his map of the enemy — the boxes and bearings, the punctual phase lines the enemy had bought at a company an hour.

"His units of account," the G-2 said finally. "Kilometers per day. It has been in front of me the whole war, filed as a curiosity. On the fourth day their lead divisions' rate of advance fell below any figure in their norms — and their behavior did not change. No fury, no reinforcement of failure, no shift of axis. They simply continued, deliberate as a tide table. I filed it as discipline. But you do not measure a tide against a stopwatch, Hr. Oberstleutnant — unless your own plan is the stopwatch." He turned, and there was something in the gray face that had not been there since W-day; it took Vossen a moment to name it as appetite. "Suppose the man across the water is not reading his advance against *our* resistance at all. Suppose he reads it, every evening, against a written line — his plan's line, blessed at some level we would both need permission to imagine — and files a figure: days behind. Days behind, accumulating. Then the question that decides this campaign is not the one I have been asking my board, which is *where is his mass.* It is: how many days behind is he — by his own count — and what number is written in his plan, in *his* ledger, next to the words: this many, and no more."

Down the corridor the teleprinters ratcheted. Aakjær pulled a fresh pad toward himself, wrote three headings across it in his small hand, and Vossen, reading them upside down, felt the wall in the operations room quietly double in size, though nothing had been pinned to it yet: HIS NORM. HIS ACTUAL. HIS PATIENCE.

"I withdraw a week of skepticism," said Aakjær. "Go to bed, Georg. One of us should be wrong about something by morning, and I intend it to be me."

---

He did not get to bed. At 0140 the G-4's night duty officer found him: the evening counts were in, and Lammers' nine-day date had become a seven-day date — the artillery's expenditure had risen again, against the deliberate assault now developing on the northern sector, and the convoys had lost eleven hours to a bombed marshalling yard at Neumünster. The first true rationing order of the war went out over Vossen's signature at 0300, and its language was the wall's language, plain as plank: batteries would bank one-third of the daily scale against the corps commander's future intent; fires in defense of ground would be refused where the ground itself was not load-bearing to the defense — and the order named, for the first time in any corps document, which ground was which.

By 0900 the protests were in, and they were correct, and they said men will die today who would not have died, and Vossen carried them to Hvidt with the ink of his own signature still bitter in his mouth. The commander read the protests standing. Then he initialed each one — *seen, sustained* — and looked up.

"Now the staff learns the price of naming the good," Hvidt said. "Yesterday we were spendthrifts and nobody had to choose. You have made the corps solvent, Herr Oberstleutnant, and solvency" — he handed back the folder — "solvency is the state in which every bill is somebody's grief. Post the order on the wall with the others. Under it, this: the corps banks today because it intends something. The staff may not yet know what. The enemy assuredly does not." A beat; the flat eyes almost warmed. "Neither entirely do I. But the shape of it is becoming visible, and the shape has a date on it, and Aakjær tells me this morning that from now on we are keeping the enemy's calendar as well as our own. Good. For ten days this headquarters has been learning to see one war clearly. It is time it learned there are two clocks in it — and only one of them is ours to slow."
