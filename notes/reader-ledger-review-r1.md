This report is written after reading the front matter and all
twenty-one chapters in order, and nothing else.

**The disclosure, first, since it's the first thing the book hands you.** The front matter says the prose was drafted by a language model, directed and edited by a human, with the whole making kept in a public ledger. What that did to my trust at the start was simple: it turned me into an auditor. I began hunting for tells — uniform competence, borrowed texture, the absence of lived grain — and I want to be honest that the hunt cost me most of chapter 1, which I read with my arms crossed. By the ENDEX wash-up scene, when Brauer asks what the forty minutes at Hohn cost at Esbjerg, I had uncrossed them, because that question is a real question, specifically imagined, and no pastiche generator asks it. By the end the balance had moved a long way back — but not all the way, and the residue is precise: the book's one genuine stylistic vice (see below, the maxim monotone) is exactly the vice I'd predict from the method, and the disclosure means I can never fully rule that it's a default rather than a choice. Also noted: a book about honest ledgers, error annexes, and provenance, prefaced by a note declaring its own ledger honest, is either elegant or a little too pleased with the rhyme. I'll call it elegant, narrowly, because the book earns the theme in blood before it claims it in front matter.

**1. THE SPEND.** The heaviest tax was geography, and it ran from chapter 3 to about chapter 13. Lammers' six-places speech (ch. 3, the midnight conference) — bridge pair, ferry sets, Vamdrup, the E3 — asks you to hold a transport network in your head that the page never draws. It got worse at Friedrichstadt (ch. 7): road bridge, rail swing-bridge, Nordfeld ferry, Yellow-3 crossing the war's grain — I was sketching on a receipt. The narrows day (ch. 12) taxed me hardest of all: the western shoulder versus the Schlei shoulder is the hinge of the whole chapter, of Holt's error, and I had to reread the 0805 paragraph to be sure which side of the map I was standing on. The second tax is a sentence-type: the coined maxim. This book mints aphorisms at a rate no real headquarters ever achieved — "a claim is a hope with a format," "a transcript is a photograph of a dance," "credit is trust with a date on it" — and each is good, and the density is a spend, because by chapter 15 every scene detonates its exit-line on schedule and my attention flinched in anticipation instead of surprise. Third tax, mild: keeping the day-of-war count aligned with November's dates around chapters 10–12.

Where the spending was effortless: the cast, surprisingly — twelve-odd recurring names carried by staff function (the G-2 is the wit, the G-4 the accountant, plans is the silence), never once confused; the teleprinter capitals, which read faster than prose and pay compound interest by chapter 19a; and the enemy chapters (4, 11, 17), which cost nothing and returned everything.

**2. THE BUY.** What I own now, in my words: a working understanding of why organizations die of their own reporting — that a depot counting heads is not a division standing in Schleswig, that every green board is green *against* something, and that the something is usually the plan's opinion of itself. I own the difference between being right and being solvent, which chapter 20 states and chapters 12 and 19 prove — the corps wins while its prize instrument is wrong by fourteen hours, and the book makes you feel the fourteen hours in a specific division's casualty meter. I own Roloff: a man who inks a report line down the street where his son learned to ride a bicycle and does not let the pen pause, and whose one sentence about himself ("The report line down my street held... I would like that noted somewhere it counts," ch. 19a) is the best line in the book. And I own the bleakest good on the shelf: the mirrored ledger. Rylski keeps honest books in the beech wood for the same reasons Holt keeps them at Rendsburg, and in December (ch. 20) Rylski's honesty is collated into a prosecution exhibit while Holt's is fought into the record over Bjelke's courtesy. Same virtue, two institutions, opposite prices. I'll be telling friends about that, and about Zawadzki putting on his reading glasses to consider the folder (ch. 17) — the most quietly frightening nuclear scene I've read in years precisely because it's conducted as staff work.

**3. OVERPAYING.** Chapter 6, the middle stretch — Witzel walking the request-procedure ladder rung by rung ("the procedure is the policy... like a lobster pot") — is the one place the book briefs at me. The locked-door conceit is honest about what it is, but I paid seminar rates for material chapter 17 later delivers as drama, and I'd skim it on reread. Chapter 10, the second Rahn telephone call: the mentor delivering enumerated wisdom ("First... Second, practically...") for the third time in ten chapters; Rahn is the book's most conventional device, the oracle in Eckernförde, and each call costs a little more suspension than the last — the ch. 17 refusal-to-bless redeems the device, but the ch. 10 installment is the book servicing its own structure. Chapter 14, the wall's relocation ("the plywood stayed at Rendsburg, and the wall arrived entire") and chapter 15's "promoted to theater instrument": here the book audibly admires its own central prop. The wall is a great invention; by the fourth time a character tells me it's the most honest document in the headquarters, I'm being sold something I already bought. And the Aakjær exit-line — the coat left over the chair in chapter 1 is lovely; by his fifth over-the-shoulder curtain line (ch. 15, "Walls do not sign orders") it's a mannerism the book performs for its own satisfaction.

**4. UNDERSERVED.** I'd have paid for a full extra chapter of Rylski. His end — dictating his own filing dates while Kuragin builds the wall stone by stone, then returning to a map room with "no column for it" — is two pages doing the work of ten, and I wanted the ten. I wanted one more page of Merete, whose single letter (ch. 7) is the finest domestic writing in the book — the household's order of battle in schoolmistress hand — and whose door-step verdict in chapter 20 earns more scene than it gets. I wanted the inside of one civil column, once: the book prices the families in the Opel from the staff side with great scruple, and I accept the withholding as design, but at chapter 7's deferral of Columns 231 and 232 I'd have paid a page to ride in the car. And the sea war is entirely reported — the seventy-two hours against the embarkation complex (ch. 15) arrive as observables on a wall, which is doctrinally correct and slightly stingy.

**5. APPARATUS.** A map: **yes, emphatically.** First actively wanted at chapter 3, Lammers' six-places speech; wanted hardest at chapter 7 (the Friedrichstadt geometry) and chapter 12 (west shoulder versus Schlei shoulder, where the ambiguity actually degraded my reading of the book's most important error). The want faded around chapter 13 — once the war contracts to the narrows and the Danevirke, the book has taught its ground by repetition — but the first half overcharges the reader for the lack, and a single front-map would have refunded hours. A cast list: **no.** Function carries the names; I never once lost anyone. A calendar: **marginal no.** Wanted once, around chapters 10–12, when day-count and date drift apart; the book's own two-times discipline otherwise keeps the reader current — this is a novel that timestamps itself.

**6. THE VERDICT.** Good deal — clearly. Five to six hours of attention against goods I expect to keep for years, at a length (short) that respects the clock: the book never once padded on me, and my complaints above are about texture, not waste of volume. It is the rare war novel whose didactic freight is carried in-scene, paid for by named characters at posted rates, and its restraint — no Lübeck march, no winking, the war "closing" rather than ending — is adult in a way this genre almost never is. I'd recommend it to the reader who owns both Forester's *The Good Shepherd* and a shelf of staff-college history; to anyone who runs an organization on dashboards; and to the Clancy reader ready to graduate from hardware to arithmetic. I would not hand it to a reader who needs interiority, blood at ground level, or more than one woman with lines. The one-sentence warning: everyone in this book — Danish, German, Polish, Russian — speaks in the same burnished lapidary maxim, and the battles happen offstage, on paper; if you can't love a war told entirely through its receipts, this ledger will not open for you.
