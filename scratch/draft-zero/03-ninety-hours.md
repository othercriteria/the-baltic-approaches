# 3. Ninety Hours

The plan was called GDP — General Defense Plan — and it existed in eleven copies and one belief. The belief was that the plan described the first four days of a war. What the plan actually described, Vossen understood by the second night, was a race it had scheduled against a competitor it had invented.

The corps' war began not with fire but with arithmetic. At 060955Z the clock started on every table in the movement annex at once. The German division — 6th Panzergrenadier, the household troops of the peninsula, garrisoned where it would fight — had the short race: out of barracks at Neumünster and Wentorf and Kellinghusen, shake out into wartime configuration, take up the covering positions its officers could see from their kitchen windows. Fifty-one hours on paper. The Danish division had the long race, the one with the ninety on it: mobilize a division that existed on Tuesday as five thousand regulars and a filing system, feed it forty thousand reservists through depots built for peacetime convenience, marry men to vehicles and vehicles to units and units to a road plan, and pass the whole assembly south across the Little Belt bridge and down the E45 into Schleswig — into another country — to positions between the Eider and the border that most of its soldiers had seen only as an overlay.

Ninety hours. Vossen had briefed the figure so many times it had stopped being a duration and become a name. Now, hour by hour through the sixth and seventh of November, he watched it turn back into time.

The first returns were beautiful. That was the trap, and he did not see it as a trap until much later, which was the point of the whole education that followed. The mobilization reporting system had been designed by conscientious men to report what could be counted: personnel reported to depots, percent; vehicles issued, percent; units at readiness state, list. By 070600 the boards in the operations cell showed the Danish depots running at hundred-and-three, hundred-and-seven percent of scheduled intake — reservists arriving early, unbidden, some before their telegrams, men who had heard the Sunday broadcasts and put on their own boots. The staff passed the numbers around like photographs of a child. Roloff pinned the intake curve to the board with the pride of a man pinning up his own.

"Every number green," said Vossen to Lammers at the midnight conference, hour thirty-eight, and meant it as good news.

"Every number that measures an *input*," said Lammers. The G-4 had not slept and had reached the stage of exhaustion where his sentences got better. "Intake, issue, readiness declarations. Inputs. Ask the boards a different question. Ask them what has crossed the Little Belt."

Vossen looked. The answer was on the board — it had been on the board all evening, one line among sixty, and he had read it without hearing it: ELEMENTS 1 JYSKE BRIGADE (RECCE SQN, ADV PARTY) CROSSED LILLEBÆLT 072240. Elements. An advance party. Hour thirty-nine of ninety, the depots bursting with punctual men — and what the corps could actually put its hand on south of the water was a reconnaissance squadron and some quartering parties.

"The depots are not the division," Lammers said. "The division is what stands in Schleswig. Between the two is one bridge, two ferry sets, a rail line with a known capacity, and four hundred kilometers of road that every mobilized unit, every ammunition convoy, every returning holiday-maker, and every frightened family in Jutland is planning to use in the same seventy hours. In the exercise we moved the division in three administrative packets on empty roads. I signed the annex, Vossen. I signed it every year. It was a fine annex for the war where nothing else moves."

They stood together looking at the board the way you look at a chessboard where you have just seen your opponent's next three moves, and behind them the intake curve went on climbing greenly toward hundred-and-ten.

That was also the night the roads acquired a second customer. The corps' movement plan had a layer nobody in nine days of BOLD LANCER had exercised, because it could not be exercised, because rehearsing it would have meant announcing to two hundred thousand civilians that their government had a folder with their names in it. Ausweichbewegungen — the movement of the civil population. The plan's polite fiction, agreed between the Bonn ministries and the Land and NATO in language burnished for a decade, was *dosed, directed, and deferred*: the population of the border Kreise would remain in place unless and until directed, movement authorized only westward and only on designated secondary routes, the autobahn and the federal roads reserved for the military. Vossen knew the annex intimately. He had personally negotiated the twice-yearly update of its route tables with the Territorial Command and the Landespolizei, in meetings with coffee and no urgency, and its central assumption now stood at his shoulder in the dark operations room like a guest who has arrived before the invitation was sent: the assumption that frightened people ask permission.

By the morning of the eighth there were cars on the B76 with mattresses on the roofs. Not many. Enough. The police reports called them *individual early movements*. Aakjær, who read everything, read those too, and said to Vossen without any expression at all: "In Copenhagen they will call it prudence. In the plan it is called leakage. On the road it is called a family. I mention the vocabulary now because we will be needing all three words."

Vossen's own family was in the annex. That was the fact he carried around that morning like a stone in the boot, unshareable, administratively minor. The house in Schleswig stood eleven kilometers south of the Eider crossings in Kreis Schleswig-Flensburg — inside the forward zone, category B, *movement deferred pending direction*. Marei had asked him on the telephone on Sunday night — the last unguarded call, though neither of them knew it — the one question the annex answered and he could not: *Should I take the children to my mother's in Rendsburg?* And he had heard himself give her the annex's answer, *stay, keep the tank full, keep the radio on*, because the corps' G-3 could not be seen to evacuate his own family on a Sunday while the plan said defer, and she had said, in the voice she used when she had already understood more than he had said: *Keep the radio on. Yes. Georg — who directs the directed movement?* And that was the second time in three weeks that someone had asked him who owned a number, and the honest answer both times was: it is composite.

At 080351Z, hour sixty-six of the ninety, the question of warning time was taken out of everyone's hands.

It came the way Aakjær had said it would come: not as information but as contact. The first reports had the unreal precision of all first reports — bridge demolition ambushed at Wassersleben, firing at the crossing points on the B200 and the A7 at Ellund, company-strength columns with armor moving on Flensburg and Süderlübeck from march order, artillery falling on the covering force's named positions with a promptness that told Aakjær's board more about the last six weeks than six weeks of ferry schedules had. Along the border the German covering battalions — in position since the sixth, at least; the short race had been run in time — began the fight they had rehearsed on that exact ground for thirty years, trading the first villages by the book, blowing the first of the prepared bridges, reporting in the clipped grammar of men doing at last the thing they had described so often.

In the operations room at Rendsburg the chinagraph went up red for the first time, and it was Vossen who noticed — at perhaps 0500, in the taste of the fourth coffee — the detail that made the morning historic in the small way that concerned his own education. The enemy was attacking at hour sixty-six. The Danish division stood at forty percent closed. The plan had scheduled its race against a competitor who was supposed to need weeks, and the competitor had arrived before the finish line, and yet — he stood in front of the boards and made himself see it — the reporting grid registered no alarm anywhere, because every input was green: intake on schedule, issue on schedule, march serials departing on schedule into roads that were no longer the roads in the table.

The corps was not late by any measure the corps kept. It was only late in Schleswig.

"Message to all staff sections," Hvidt said at the 0600 conference, hour sixty-nine, the first morning of the war, and his English was as flat as the first Thursday. "From this hour I want every report in this headquarters to carry two times. The time something was scheduled, and the time it happened. Where the second is missing, the report will say so in words. I have no further use" — he looked, without heat, at the green boards — "for the plan's opinion of itself."
