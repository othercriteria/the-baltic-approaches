# Hostile review: the Rendsburg G-3 decision

**Charter: planning/status.md, unsettle #1. Persona: hostile structural editor. This is the counter-brief; the reconciliation is section 4 and is deliberately brief.**

## 1. The strongest case for division altitude

Start with the misreading at the root of the corps choice. Alex Rogo is not a staff officer. He is **line management with full authority inside his boundary** — he reroutes parts, moves the bottleneck, changes the release schedule, and nobody has to be persuaded before the experiment runs. Peach exists to *threaten*, not to *approve*: the persuasion engine of The Goal is Rogo buying time to keep experimenting, never Rogo begging permission to experiment. The echelon doc's rank note ("command options trade the persuasion engine for direct authority; The Goal's structure suggests keeping the boss") gets this exactly backwards — The Goal keeps the boss AND the authority. The faithful transposition is a **commander or de-facto-commanding chief of staff of a single formation, measured in wrong units from above**: a division. Rendsburg's G-3 is Lou the accountant promoted to protagonist.

What the division book sees that Rendsburg cannot:

- **The plant, walkable.** The Jutland Division at M-hour is The Goal's factory floor with the roof off: three mechanized brigades deploying *south into a foreign country* on march tables, the division's entire combat power existing for 48 hours as **throughput across the Little Belt bridges** against a refugee flow coming the other way. Herbie is a bridge. The constraint is visible from a road shoulder, in the rain, at column speed. Davies (reference/landjut-front.md) hands you this for free and the planning docs spend it as a status report to Rendsburg.
- **The bottleneck as a machine with a location.** 6th PzGren's artillery regiment at Kellinghusen — its own garrison town — is the NCX-10: finite tubes, a Class V flow, a queue of fire missions. The logistics layer your own wargame v5 built ("supply throughput as the constraint") is *experienced* at the echelon that owns the guns and *briefed* at the echelon that reads the ammo report.
- **Metrics that mislead on-page, with ground truth in frame.** The reader-contract demands the reader "feel why a competent, earnest officer believes them." That requires seeing the ground truth the number misrepresents — same page, same day. A division G-3 both *generates* the false readiness percentages and *walks past* the battalions they describe. At corps, ground truth arrives only by liaison-trip contrivance; the metrics can be doubted at Rendsburg but never *caught lying*.
- **Doctrine-returns-home lands harder at 6th PzGren.** The German division is the actual descendant of the tradition Balck fed to TRADOC; its hold line runs through its soldiers' home Kreise (Vorneverteidigung as lived politics, not a `hold_release_day` parameter); HDv 100/100 is its native tongue. The corps book gets this theme as an English-language staff argument; the division book gets it in the blood.
- **Attrition with faces.** At division, the battalion commanders are known men and loss reports have names. At corps they are CV decrements. The premise's own quality bar — payloads "experienced, not briefed" — is a division-altitude sentence.

And the dismissed-too-quickly flag: **the division option was never tested.** Option A got one paragraph in the original screen; the post-campaign-1 assessment then "collapsed the option space" using an instrument in which divisions *are not decision-makers at all* — they are CV pools on axes. See attack 3.

## 2. The strongest case for brigade altitude (a Danish brigade, roads home)

The genre's surviving books are one-hull books: Team Yankee is a company, The Cruel Sea is a ship, Das Boot is a boat, Graves is a subaltern. Nobody rereads the corps-HQ chapters of anything.

- **The geometry is a gift the corps cannot see.** A Jutland Mechanised Brigade fights *in Holstein* and withdraws *toward Denmark*: every kilometer ceded is a kilometer up the E45 toward Padborg, retreat and homecoming fused into one movement, defeat measured on childhood roads. No other altitude in this theater has that emotional geometry. Rendsburg experiences it as a FEBA trace.
- **Your own instrument already wrote the brigade book's opening.** Wargame v1: "the log's S column marks the days the hold line forbade the withdrawal the model wanted. The book's opening act lives in that S column." The S column *at corps* is a column. At brigade it is the dawn you are not permitted to withdraw, with projected losses past 20 percent and the order signed by a headquarters 60 km behind you. The instrument's most novelistic finding to date is a brigade scene, and the planning docs filed it as corps material.
- **The feedback engine (unsettle #2) is healthiest here.** A brigade fights several engagements a day; you change the fire plan at dusk and read the answer at dawn. Iteration — The Goal's actual pedagogy — comes free at brigade tempo. (See attack 2 for how starved it is at corps.)
- **Contract clauses 1–3 at full strength.** Adrift: a covering-force brigade is priced by the theater in "days of delay purchased" — UniCo's view of Rogo's plant, exactly. Confused: the second echelon that never arrives (because someone's deep battle worked) is confusion the reader *shares*, which the contract explicitly wants. Earnest: showing up is literal.
- The honest concession — that FOFA and echelonment are illegible from a brigade TOC — deserves one pushback before section 4: The Goal teaches an entire systems theory *without ever leaving the plant*. Herbie is a boy on a hike. Goldratt never shows the corporation. The claim that operational art requires an operational-altitude viewpoint character is an assumption the source text conspicuously does not share.

## 3. Three sharpest attacks on the corps choice, regardless of alternative

**Attack 1 — The staff-officer transposition error.** Rogo has hands; the G-3 has a grease pencil. Every experiment the Rendsburg protagonist runs must first be *sold* — to the commander, and for the flagship lever, further up still. That makes persuasion scenes the load-bearing scenes, which is briefing-room drama **by construction, not by lapse** — the exact "PowerPoint-in-prose" failure the project keeps an instrument persona to police. You have chosen the altitude that maximizes the work your most-feared auditor must do, and the mitigation on file (the theater is shallow, the HQ gets shelled) buys *peril*, not *work*: an HQ under fire is a victim with maps, and getting the protagonist shelled does not change what his job produces, which is paper. Worse: a two-division corps with documented thin corps troops, in a 95–110 km theater, is functionally a division-plus wearing a corps flag — the choice buys the *register* of operational art with roughly a division's actual span.

**Attack 2 — The feedback engine is weakest at exactly this altitude, and the flagship lever may not even be his.** Unsettle #2 is not merely unexamined at corps; it is aggravated. Corps decision cycles run 24–72 hours against a three-week war: perhaps a dozen air-allocation calls, three or four reserve decisions, one counterstroke. And the chosen payload — deep battle — is the payload whose *outcomes are least observable*: to know interdiction worked, the staff must know the second echelon's schedule, which is precisely what it doesn't know (your own v3 finding: the window's existence depends on the enemy's deep fight, invisible from blue's side of the log). Rogo reads his experiment's result the same day; the G-3 attributes nothing inside the book's clock. Add the command-architecture problem the docs leave open: air apportionment in this theater plausibly belongs to **AIRBALTAP at Karup, not LANDJUT** (TO-VERIFY, but the docs haven't verified it either) — in which case the instrument's signature lever, `deep_fraction`, is one the protagonist *nominates targets for and begs*, mediated twice before anything happens. A protagonist whose experiments can neither be freely run nor clearly read has no Goal-shaped arc; he has a memo trail.

**Attack 3 — The circularity is worse than the flag admits, and it cost you the senses.** Status.md concedes the wargame was framed at corps. Go further: the model **contains no other grade of decision**. Divisions in it are CV pools on axes; there is no brigade-reserve commitment, no obstacle sequencing, no demolition timing, no divisional fires allocation, no covering-force handover — so "not one [lever] is a division decision in this theater" (protagonist-echelon.md) is true *of the model, by construction*, and was reported as if it were true of the theater. A division-framed instrument would emit a division job description with identical fluency. The corroborating evidence marshaled for corps is therefore one argument (reader contract + staff shape), not two. And what the frame silently discarded is the sensory stratum: mud, engine noise, the weight of ammunition, November at eye level — everything the project's certified strengths (procedural concreteness, material culture) are strengths *at*. A corps book must import all of it through liaison-visit windows, and every such trip will read as what it is: the author commuting to the war.

## 4. What the existing docs already answer (honestly, briefly)

- **The front is not in dispute.** The peripheral-theater logic, the Bagnall spare, the GDR archive base, doctrine-returns-home: LANDJUT survives this review untouched. My attacks are echelon-shaped, not theater-shaped.
- **Brigade illegibility is a real objection.** Option C's problem stands: my brigade case is honestly a *different, smaller book* — a fine one, but its didactic payload is tempo-and-logistics at tactical scale, not AirLand Battle's operational argument, and the premise contracted for the latter.
- **The binational ensemble is a genuine corps asset.** The 50/50 English-language Rendsburg staff (landjut-front.md) gives alliance friction natively; 6th PzGren's staff is monolingual German, the Jutland Division's monolingual Danish. My division case pays a real price here, and I have no cheap fix for it.
- **The change-of-command device partially rebuts Attack 1.** Option B's inversion — a three-weeks-in-post commander who *must* rely on the G-3 — manufactures authority-by-vacuum and is the freshest thing in the file. Partially: it launders the persuasion tax for act one; the tax returns the moment the commander finds his feet, and the flagship lever still lives at Karup if it lives at Karup.
- **The staff-section arc is real.** "The G-3's questions need the G-2's and G-4's answers" is a genuine ensemble engine that the wargame earned honestly (the enemy-model finding survived calibration), and it is native to a headquarters in a way it is not to a battalion.
- **They flagged the circularity themselves.** Unsettle #1 is this review's own charter — credit for asking. But the flag said "suspect"; the finding here is "unfalsifiable as run." Those are different severities.

## 5. Verdict

The corps choice survives on its front, its ensemble, and its command device, and dies on its source text: The Goal is a book about a man with his hands on the machines, and Rendsburg's hands hold a grease pencil — the docs converted Rogo's *authority under a hostile boss* into *advice under a persuadable one* and called it fidelity. # BRIEF: Air apportionment authority in BALTAP, mid-1980s

## Core answer (as precisely as open sources allow)

**COMLANDJUT did not own any fixed-wing air effort and did not decide the deep-vs-close split. He was a requester.** All air in the Baltic Approaches — Danish, German, and inbound reinforcements — belonged to COMAIRBALTAP at Karup, under the joint commander COMBALTAP (also Karup, from 1985 in the Finderup bunker). Apportionment (the CA/AI/BAI/CAS split) was made at the Karup joint level: COMBALTAP's decision on his air commander's advice, executed by COMAIRBALTAP's staff. This is **(a) established** as to force ownership and command relationships, and **(b) strong inference from the NATO-wide model** as to the apportionment act itself — I found no open document that names the BALTAP apportionment procedure explicitly. Detail by evidence class below.

## (a) Established facts

**1. BALTAP structure.** BALTAP (est. 8 Jan 1962, HQ Karup) was a Principal Subordinate Command under AFNORTH (Kolsås) until 1993. COMBALTAP (Danish LTG/VADM, German deputy) had four subordinate commanders: COMLANDJUT (Rendsburg), COMLANDZEALAND (Ringsted), COMAIRBALTAP (Karup), COMNAVBALTAP (Kiel-Holtenau, from 1976 Karup). — [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_Forces_Baltic_Approaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_Forces_Baltic_Approaches) (fetched 2026-07-21); mirrored in [dewiki.de/Lexikon/Allied_Command_Baltic_Approaches](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Allied_Command_Baltic_Approaches); Danish confirmation at [koldkrig-online.dk/forsvaret/nato/](https://koldkrig-online.dk/forsvaret/nato/).

**2. LANDJUT was an air-effort customer, not owner.** Air support to LANDJUT came through AIRBALTAP; the corps had no fixed-wing element. "AIRBALTAP commanded all flying units based within its sector and all reinforcements flying into its sector, as well as ground-based radar systems and stations, air defence units and the airfields in its sector" (Wikipedia BALTAP article, fetched 2026-07-21). Grokipedia's BALTAP page (search snippet, 2026-07-21) words it as AIRBALTAP "providing close air support to land forces under COMLANDJUT."

**3. The Danish dual-hat.** COMAIRBALTAP was the same officer as the chief of Flyvertaktisk Kommando (Danish Tactical Air Command), whose HQ was Bunker 1137 at Karup — i.e., the national and NATO air command were fused in one man in one bunker, and "all of the Air Force's procedures were developed jointly by the Air Tactical Command and COMAIRBALTAP." In war the entire RDAF passed to AIRBALTAP. — [forsvaret.dk Flyvevåbnets historie](https://www.forsvaret.dk/da/organisation/flyvevaabnet/flyvevabnets-historie/), [da.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyvertaktisk_Kommando](https://da.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyvertaktisk_Kommando), [koldkrig-online.dk/totalforsvar/flyvevaabnet/](https://koldkrig-online.dk/totalforsvar/flyvevaabnet/) (all fetched 2026-07-21). BALTAP HQ sat in Bunker 1137 at Karup until moving to the purpose-built Finderup bunker in 1985 (that bunker later became CAOC 1/CAOC Finderup) — [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_Air_Operations_Centre_Finderup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_Air_Operations_Centre_Finderup); Danish signals-veteran account (Maj. A.B. Christensen, "Signaltjenesten i Bunker 1137," [flvsig.abchris.dk PDF](http://www.flvsig.abchris.dk/images/Signaltjenesten%20i%20BK1137.pdf), retrieved 2026-07-21) confirms BALTAP/AIRBALTAP established in the Karup bunker complex 1961–62 and BALTAP's own telephone exchange at Finderup 1985–87.

**4. The air-defence anomaly.** Air defence over Schleswig-Holstein and Hamburg was **not** COMAIRBALTAP's — it was 2 ATAF's (RAF Germany, Mönchengladbach), under Allied reserved rights, until 1990. So over LANDJUT's German sector the counter-air/air-defence fight belonged to a different theater's air commander entirely. — Wikipedia BALTAP article and dewiki (both fetched 2026-07-21). This alone shows the corps commander at Rendsburg had no purchase on the counter-air end of the apportionment ledger.

**5. Reinforcement air went to COMAIRBALTAP, not the corps.** Planned air reinforcements for Denmark: 1 RAF Harrier squadron + 2 RAF Jaguar squadrons; USAF 1× F-15, 3× F-16, 1× A-10 squadron (Nørby, ["From Defence of the Baltic to the Airspace above Kosovo," Balloons to Drones, 29 Sep 2020](https://balloonstodrones.com/2020/09/29/from-defense-of-the-baltic-to-the-airspace-above-kosovo-the-transformation-of-the-royal-danish-air-force-1989-1999/)). AIRBALTAP "commanded ... all reinforcements flying into its sector" (Wikipedia, above); ~300 combat aircraft at COMBALTAP's disposal in the 1989 wartime picture (Wikipedia/de.wiki BALTAP).

**6. The deep/maritime-strike end was a Karup joint-level plan.** "Operation Hurricane," the coordinated anti-invasion-fleet strike (German MFG F-104s/Tornados with Kormoran, Danish/German ship-launched missiles, layered waves), was developed by the BALTAP unified command — i.e., commitment of strike air against the amphibious fleet was pre-planned at joint theater level, not negotiable at corps level. — [koldkrig-online.dk/historie/operation-hurricane/](https://koldkrig-online.dk/historie/operation-hurricane/) (fetched 2026-07-21); corroborated by the Danish officers'-association review of Bogason, [hod.dk/nyheder/forsvaret-af-adgangsvejene/](https://hod.dk/nyheder/forsvaret-af-adgangsvejene/).

**7. The Central Region comparator (documented).** "COMAAFCE does the apportionment of air assets and allows the ATAFs to allocate their apportioned forces as they see fit"; ATOCs then did detailed tasking of offensive air. — Air War College study *Command and Control of the NATO Central Region Air Forces*, March 1989, via the archive.org DTIC mirror ([archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA217946](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA217946), full text fetched 2026-07-21). And NATO-wide, pre-1990s, "Air Operations Coordination Centres (AOCCs) were still attached to a land corps, however, they were known as Air Support Operations Centres (ASOCs)" — [JAPCC, "Air-Land Integration – NATO's Strategic Joint Challenge"](https://www.japcc.org/articles/air-land-integration-natos-strategic-joint-challenge/) (fetched 2026-07-21). So the standard NATO grammar: corps ASOC requests; air-command-level ATOC tasks; apportionment decided above both.

## (b) Strong inference from the NATO-wide model

- **Where apportionment sat in BALTAP:** BALTAP replicated the NATO model one echelon down: what CINCENT/COMAAFCE were to NORTHAG/2 ATAF, COMBALTAP/COMAIRBALTAP were to LANDJUT/AIRBALTAP — with the simplification that BALTAP was already a *joint* HQ, its air commander co-located (same bunker complex) with the joint commander. The apportionment conversation was therefore a Karup conversation between COMBALTAP and COMAIRBALTAP (advised by NAVBALTAP's competing maritime-air claims — the MFGs' Kormoran Tornados were simultaneously the anti-invasion-fleet asset and the nearest thing LANDJUT had to BAI-capable air). Inference from items 1–7; no single document found stating "COMBALTAP apportions."
- **LANDJUT's machinery:** per the JAPCC statement, LANDJUT as a corps-level land command would have had an ASOC/offensive-air-support request cell fed by FACs/ALOs down the divisions, passing requests to AIRBALTAP's offensive operations staff at Karup. Consistent with Danish doctrinal writing on tactical air support in Militært Tidsskrift (e.g., [krigsvidenskab.dk "Taktiske Luftoperationer"](https://krigsvidenskab.dk/emne/taktiske-luftoperationer), 1971 — centralized air-commander control, corps requests, priority order counter-air &gt; interdiction &gt; CAS). **Not established:** a named "ATOC Karup" in the Cold War. The ATOC designation is documented for the Central Region; at Karup the offensive-tasking function lived inside AIRBALTAP/FTK's combined staff (the SOC air-defence side is well documented in Bunker 1137; the offensive side's label is not, in open sources I could reach).
- **One complication above BALTAP:** English Wikipedia's AFNORTH article states an Air Force Commander Northern Europe (always a USAF general, directly under CINCNORTH) "would assume command of all NATO air forces in AFNORTH's area in the case of war" ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_Forces_Northern_Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_Forces_Northern_Europe), fetched 2026-07-21). If accurate for the mid-80s, theater apportionment *guidance* could descend from Kolsås; given geography (Norway vs. Jutland fronts barely competed for the same airframes) the practical decision still sat at Karup. # Warsaw Pact amphibious landing on the Danish isles — source extract

**Sources actually used:** Pałka, "Planning for a Landing Operation of the Polish People's Army on the Danish Isles during the Cold War," *Kwartalnik Historyczny* CXXIX (2022), Eng. ed. no. 6, pp. 95–126 (`/home/dlk/workspace/the-mission-1986/reference/pdf/palka-polish-landing-operation-planning.pdf`); FM 100-2-2 *The Soviet Army: Specialized Warfare and Rear Area Support* (1984), Chs. 2 &amp; 4 (extracted at scratchpad `fm100-2-2.txt`). **Skipped as instructed/unusable:** the two PHP maritime-front directives (1961, 1967) are image-only facsimiles — pdftotext yields 6–7 bytes; the NIEs are covered by another agent. **Note:** FM 100-2-1 (1984) contains **no amphibious operations chapter** — its "amphibious" hits are all incidental (vehicle characteristics, river crossings); Soviet amphib doctrine lives in FM 100-2-2 Ch. 4.

## (a) Red force package for the isles operation (Polish Coastal Front plan, 1965–1985)

The landing component of the Polish Coastal Front (Front itself = First, Second, Fourth Armies + Third Air Army; Pałka p. 105–06):

- **6th Airborne Division ("red berets")** — formed 1957 from 6th Infantry Division (p. 99–100). Early-1970s strength ~3,500 men; 25 ASU-85 SP guns, 12 WP-8 rocket launchers, 24 portable ATGM launchers, 48 mortars, 12 × 82 mm recoilless rifles, 18 ZU-23-2 AA guns (p. 100 n. 13). Dropped its full complement exactly **once** in its history (October Storm, GDR, 21 Oct 1965, mostly on Soviet An-12s; some equipment had to go by land) (p. 101).
- **7th Landing Division ("blue berets")** — 23rd Infantry Division reorganized 1963 (p. 100). 1980s strength ~3,200 (down from &gt;4,000 in the 60s–70s): 40 T-55, 92 PT-76 amphibious tanks, 137 TOPAS APCs, 49 BRDM-2, 23 PTS amphibious transporters, 2 tactical missile launchers (its 41st Tactical Missile Battalion appears in the 1977 nuclear fire plan, p. 115), 18 ATGM launchers, 34 Strela-2 SAMs, 6 ZU-23-2, 8 BM-21, 18 × 120 mm and 45 × 82 mm mortars (p. 100 n. 14).
- Pałka stresses both "landing divisions" were **"each in fact of brigade strength"** — comparable to Soviet brigades or US Marine/airborne regiments (pp. 100, 104).
- **15th Mechanized Division** — added because the two landing divisions were judged insufficient; it had **no landing vessels of its own** and would move mainly on mobilized civilian ships, "coast-to-coast" (pp. 104, 113). Its 19th Tactical Missile Battalion also appears in the nuclear plan (p. 115). Full landing force in the 1977 plan: ~**20,747 personnel, 17 mechanized battalions, 307 tanks (205 medium), 175 indirect-fire pieces, 174 AT weapons** vs. an estimated ~28,000 defenders (1:1.3 in personnel; Table 4, p. 117). First landing group (6AD + 7LD) alone: ~10,163 men, 123 tanks (only 31 medium) vs. ~11,700 defenders with 47 tanks (Table 5, p. 117).
- Expected enemy (1965 plan): three Danish mechanized "Zealand brigades," an Honest John battalion, a 203 mm howitzer battalion, 30% of Danish tank inventory (Table 2 notes, p. 107); by 1977 also a **British Royal Marine brigade** on the island (pp. 113–15).
- The Soviet Army separately anticipated **six of its own divisions plus independent airborne brigades/regiments** for landings throughout the Baltic (Bornholm, Swedish islands, the Swedish coast) — the Polish lift request was "definitely not a priority" against that (p. 116).

## (b) Lift capacity and its arithmetic — the persistent shortfall

**Sealift.** Polish 2nd Landing Ship Brigade (formed 1964), at its early-1970s apogee: 22 landing ships (projects 770/771), 1 project 776 command ship, 15 project 709 landing boats, 3 project 719 cutters (p. 100). Project 770/771 ships carried ~5 T-54/55 each; the 1980s project 767 carried 9 T-72 or 17 vehicles, but of 12 planned only **5** were built (p. 103). The 1964 staff calculation: Polish ships plus the Soviets' declared six additional landing ships could lift **no more than one regiment of the 7th Landing Division at a time**; using the commercial fleet meant ~20 days of preparation and 2 more days to unload a second regiment — "this precluded the element of surprise or a blitzkrieg." Warsaw asked Moscow for 30–40 project 770s (p. 103). The 1977 plan's paper solution: **92 landing ships and cutters** — 37 Polish (22 × pr. 770/771 + 15 cutters), 37 Soviet (1 × pr. 1171, 15 × pr. 770/771, 6 × pr. 106, 15 cutters), 18 East German (6 × pr. 47, 12 × pr. 46) — plus 26 militarized merchant vessels (18 Polish, 8 Soviet). Pałka: assumptions "so inflated that one has to ask whether they would be realistic to achieve in a war that would have already been a few days old" (p. 112).

**Airlift.** 1964: Poland's ~50 mostly obsolete transports could deliver 6AD only in 8–10 runs over 12–16 hours — an air assault on Zealand "using currently possessed means of aerial transport is practically unfeasible" (p. 102). Landing the division within 5 hours required ≥80 An-12s making two runs each (An-12: ~60 paratroopers or 105 soldiers or 20 t). Poland ever owned 2 An-12s (1966) and 12 An-26s (40 paratroopers/5.5 t) (pp. 102–03). The 1969 review put the true requirement at **250 transport aircraft vs. the 50 Soviet An-12s promised (in two runs)** — partial drop of the division judged "unacceptable" (p. 109).

**1969 landing-rate arithmetic (the damning numbers):** first sea group (~3,000 men) ashore in ~2 hours *if all went well*; its transports-borne reinforcement (~2,000 men) taking up to **30 hours**; second group fully landed only within **100 hours** of H-hour (p. 109).

## (c) Timeline, phases, prerequisites (the mature 1977 plan)

- War day ~3: Polish Front offensive begins ("D" = start of Polish offensive, p. 106 n. 29 — so landing day ≈ war day 8–9). D6: First Army completes Jutland; Funen taken by mech regiments plus a "tactical sea landing" (p. 106).
- **D6–D7, morning, G-hour scheme (1977):** G−5.5 h — first (decisive) nuclear strike (p. 114). G−1 h — main body of 6AD (three reinforced parachute battalions) drops north of Haslev–Næstved–Hellestedt to block reserves and link to the beach (p. 112). G to G+3 — first sea echelon, three reinforced battalions of 7LD (embarked "along a broad front at six locations" on the Polish and GDR coasts), lands in **Køge and Fakse Bays**, establishing four landing points; G to G+6 — one more reinforced battalion + artillery battalion of 6AD air-delivered (pp. 112–13). Day 1 objective: the line Køge–Dalby–Fakse–Ladeplands, a strip ~30–40 km deep, defeating two Danish mech brigades plus territorial forces (p. 113).
- Day 2: 15th MD lands in Fakse Bay in successive waves from GDR ports (rear echelons of all three divisions embark Świnoujście/Rostock/Warnemünde), yielding "decisive force advantage" (p. 113). Exploitation: 7LD + one mech regiment → west of Køge/Copenhagen → north coast Helsingør–Tisvildeleje; 15th MD → Ringsted–Svinninge and Flakkebjerg–Korsør; 6AD → south to Vordingborg and the Møn/Falster bridges (p. 113).
- Day 3 of the landing (D8–D9): all Zealand occupied; link-up with First Army arriving via Fyn–Lolland–Falster; Copenhagen only **"blocked," not stormed** — capitulation expected (pp. 113–14).
- **Prerequisites/support:** air cover from nine Polish and Soviet assault-fighter/fighter-bomber regiments plus the Soviet 16th Fighter Division; 50 Soviet An-12s (two sorties each); Soviet and East German naval landing groups with artillery, guard, logistics ships; a Soviet minesweeper team; naval fire-support teams (pp. 111–13). Soviet Baltic Fleet to seal the Øresund, GDR Volksmarine the western Baltic; Polish fighters could cover only ~50 km out to sea (p. 103). Escort requirement Warsaw computed in 1964: ~40 ASW/torpedo boats, 4–6 destroyers, 8 small missile ships, 20 MTBs, 50 minesweepers — "the needs were immense" (pp. 103–04).
- **Nuclear underpinning:** 1965 plan — 15 missiles (~340 kt aggregate) + 5 air-delivered weapons on Zealand's anti-landing defenses at Roskilde, Slagelse, Næstved, Vordingborg (30–50 km from the beaches) and on northern-island reserves (p. 108). 1970 plan — 18 weapons (12 tactical, 6 operational-tactical), 5 strikes flown by the Soviet 132nd Bomber Regiment (p. 111). 1977 plan — 15 weapons, **total 2,095 kt**; targets itemized in Table 3, p. 115 (Fort Dragør 1×300 kt; Amager forts/HAWK/Nike 1×300; Tune 1×300; Store Heddinge 1×300; beach-defense companies in Køge/Fakse Bays 2×300 for "60% losses"; Fort Stevns 1×200; Bas-Mosede 1×15; Honest John bn 2×15; Danish tank bn 2×10; Royal Marine bde 3×10 for "30–40% losses"). The 1969 staff verdict is the hinge: an air landing "can only be contemplated under conditions of nuclear war" (p. 109).

## (d) Soviet doctrine norms (FM 100-2-2, 1984)

**Amphibious (Ch. 4):** Landings categorized strategic / operational / tactical / recon-sabotage; **operational landings** ("maritime front") — naval infantry regiment as first echelon to seize "major islands or a group of islands," the primary Naval Infantry role; a strategic multidivision landing has **never been conducted** (p. 4-1). Naval Infantry ~12,000 men total, re-activated 1964; **one regiment in the Baltic Fleet** (p. 4-1). Regiment ≈2,000 men: 3 naval infantry battalions (~400 men, 34 BTR-60 each), tank battalion mixing 44 T-54/55 + 44 PT-76, no organic artillery battalion — just 6 BM-21s (pp. 4-1 to 4-4). Echeloning: naval infantry landing teams lead, breach obstacles, seize the beachhead; the **main body of army motorized rifle troops follows**; naval infantry withdraws once ground forces pass through, ending the assault phase (pp. 4-5, 4-7). Basic assault unit is the reinforced **battalion assault force (BAF)**; immediate mission = destroy the first defense line and take shore terrain for follow-on deployment (pp. 4-1, 4-6). Five phases: preparation, loading, sea movement/debarkation, battle for the beachhead, landing of ground forces + naval infantry withdrawal (p. 4-6). Wave mechanics: PT-76s swim in first firing from the water, BTR-60 wave next, medium tanks wade ashore in a follow-on wave; engineers clear **3–6 lanes per battalion**; one tank platoon normally supports each naval infantry company; air-cushion vehicles used in Baltic exercises (pp. 4-4, 4-6, 4-7). Prerequisites and acknowledged weaknesses: tactical air superiority ("the assault force would be vulnerable... at sea and on the beaches" without it), surprise lost if the convoy is detected, heavy surf capsizing vehicles ("fire from amphibious tanks in the water would be useless in any but the calmest conditions"), obstacle belts, enemy armor vs. light armor, buoy/panel lane-marking fragility, dependence on the paired airborne/heliborne blocking force landing correctly (p. 4-8).

**Airborne (Ch. 2), relevant to the paired drop:** operations up to 500 km beyond the FEBA; up to a division for a front operation but a reinforced regiment is the common package; local air superiority (even temporary) is essential and the drop should be completed **in one flight** (pp. 2-5, 2-6). Lift norms: An-12 = 60 paratroopers/20 t/2 BMDs, **90–115 An-12s per BMD regiment**; Il-76 = 120 paratroopers/40 t/3 BMDs, 50–65 per regiment; VTA fleet ~370 An-12, 170+ Il-76, 50+ An-22, plus Aeroflot augmentation (pp. 2-3, 2-4). DZs 3×4 km, 1–2 per regiment, 4–6 per division, preferably within 30 km of the objective; drop altitude 150–300 m; night drops preferred; planned linkup for supporting heliborne forces 2–3 hours (pp. 2-6, 2-7, 3-x/4-1 area). These norms make the Polish 50-aircraft allocation legible as roughly *half of one regiment-equivalent per sortie wave*.

## (e) Evolution and 1980s status

1950 war-game version: island-hopping from Jutland (Bornholm by reinforced division; Falster→Lolland→Zealand; Copenhagen by ~day 21) (pp. 96–97). June 1961 Soviet directive creates the Coastal Front mission; 1964 Warsaw feasibility studies expose the lift gap; first formal plan approved 18 Dec 1964 / in force 28 Feb 1965; 1970 plan a "carbon copy"; 1977 plan (Gierek) stretches the whole campaign from 6 to **11–13 days**, adds a conventional-only opening variant, keeps the D6–D7 landing (pp. 97–112). Spring 1982 review: Front now straddling three directions with wings 600–900 km apart; three relief variants proposed (reinforcement / narrow to Jutland+isles / drop Denmark entirely) — Moscow deaf (p. 118). 1983 "Marzec '83" game: Gen. Barański concedes "achieving the element of surprise in the operational sense is impossible," proposes reversion to "short hops" (Fehmarn, Langeland, Møn, then Lolland/Falster, Zealand from the Great Belt side) with embarkation almost wholly on the GDR coast (p. 119). **The decisive 1980s change:** after the 27–28 May 1985 Moscow consultations, the plan approved 29 Nov 1986 **released the Polish Front from the Zealand assault** — Zealand passed to the Soviet Sixteenth Army (Northern Group of Forces) and/or United Baltic Fleet; 7th Landing Division became the 7th Coastal Defence Brigade, which in the offensive variant would embark at Gdynia/Gdańsk, join a UBF battle group in the Bay of Pomerania in three landing teams, and land in Køge Bay while Soviets took the port of Køge (pp. 120–21). Poland's own residual mission: **Bornholm** — defended (Polish estimate) by ~2,840 Danish troops, 10 tanks, 26 guns/mortars, ~30 AT weapons; two battalions of 6th Airborne Brigade on the port of Rønne + one mechanized regiment (28th MR/8th MD) by sea; island taken D2–D3; excluded from the defensive variant (p. 121). The 1988–89 revision made defense the priority but kept Bornholm (D3–D4, one airborne battalion + one mech regiment + a marine battalion); it was **never signed** by the Polish leadership (pp. 121–22).

## (f) Feasibility assessments in the paper

- Pałka's own verdict: tasks "excessive and even unrealistic. Carrying out a large-scale landing operation was simply outside its [the PPA's] capabilities. ... only the attack on Bornholm would have been feasible — provided that the Soviet Army would have allocated aerial and naval support" (pp. 122–23) — yet he rejects calling the planning pure fiction: the PPA genuinely trained and structured itself for this from the early 1960s to mid-1980s (p. 123).
- Contemporary staff verdicts embedded in the record: 1964 "practically unfeasible" (air component, p. 102); 1969 "problematic," nuclear-war-only (p. 109); the conventional variant left only "a slight advantage," making feasibility "doubtful" without Northern Group of Forces / Baltic Fleet reinforcement the plan never provided for (p. 114).
- Front commander-designate Gen. Tuczapski: the Danish/Belgian opposition was "easiest in terms of the quality of the opponent... the difficulty consisted in the fact that all this was to be tied up with a very specifically defined sea landing operation" (p. 116). Mazurek (quoted p. 123 n. 63): "paper being patient."

# Denmark/NATO's picture of the WP amphibious threat to Zealand — blue's beliefs, and where they diverged from red's reality

**Sources**: DIIS, *Danmark under den kolde krig* (2005). Core: vol. 3 kap. 77 (FE's assessments 1979–91, pp. 425–467) and kap. 80 (historians' retrospective from Eastern archives, pp. 537–554); vol. 2 kap. 50–51, 53 for the 1963–78 baseline (pp. 517–576, 597–642); vol. 4 kap. 88 synthesis (pp. 47–58); 7-pp. English summary. Page cites below are the printed page numbers. Extracted texts persist at `/tmp/nix-shell.GmMMdG/claude-1000/-home-dlk-workspace-the-mission-1986/71ede904-8327-4534-a71e-c4922ebbd729/scratchpad/bind{2,3,4}-paged.txt` (line-prefixed with printed page).

The vehicle for the official picture was FE's periodical *Truslen mod Danmark* ("The Threat to Denmark"), issued ~80 external recipients, editions 1965, 1969/70 papers, 1974, 1976, 1978, 1980/81, 1984, 1985, 1986, 1988, 1991 (renamed *Det efterretningsmæssige planlægningsgrundlag*), plus monthly *Efterretningsoversigter* (~200 recipients) (KK3 p. 425).

## (a) FE baseline on the landing threat, and its evolution

**1965 (Truslen mod Danmark 1965)** — Zealand op **not** expected at war's start: East could not count on air/sea superiority in the first days; amphibious "immediate effectiveness" judged *"forholdsvis ringe"* (fairly poor), improvable only after 2–3 months of work-up. Danish study war-gamed four timings; simultaneous D-day landing and late (D+10+) landings judged least likely; landing between D+2 and D+10 the planning case, conditional on Central Front progress. Main assault expected at **Stevns**, with subsidiary landings Møn, Lolland-Falster, later Bornholm. WP sealift (96 landing craft, March 1965) could move ~5,000 men + 112 tanks/194 APCs in one lift — a reduced motor-rifle division, reinforced by 2 airborne regiments and up to 2 naval infantry battalions; against a mobilized Zealand (3 brigades) the minimum invasion force was reckoned a full division + airborne division, i.e. beyond one lift (KK2 pp. 525–528).

**1968–70 revision (darker)** — Triggered by Soviet blue-water naval expansion, the 1960s naval-infantry/amphibious build-up, and the new continuous exercise presence off Danish waters. FE's Feb 1970 paper: threat "forøget" (increased); attack now possible "uden nævneværdigt varsel" (without appreciable warning), including by proxy (Polish/East German) forces. But still non-alarmist on capacity: "only the combined WP force would suffice for a seaborne assault, and then only against one area, if success was to be assured" (KK2 pp. 529–531).

**1970s (Truslen 1974/76/78)** — The landing against Zealand now treated as a **high-priority operation of the Soviet leadership, "that could not be launched early enough"** — the 1960s premise that it depended on success elsewhere was discarded; seizure of the straits exits sought "at the earliest possible moment of a conflict" (KK2 pp. 567–568). Expected shape: one regular landing with beachhead at **Fakse Bugt**, executed nation-by-nation in seven battalion battle groups (3 Soviet, 2 Polish, 2 East German) plus parachute forces — at most 6–8,000 men in one lift with ~65 landing ships; follow-on 1–2 Polish infantry divisions on merchant ships, needing 1–4 days (KK2 pp. 568–569). Zealand attack timed a few hours *after* the Schleswig-Holstein attack (an amphibious opening would forfeit surprise by giving tactical warning) (KK2 pp. 567–568). Yet simultaneously, from 1974 Zealand was **deleted from the "limited war / coup" scenario** — judged beyond a fait-accompli operation, implicitly a positive verdict on Zealand's defenses; 1976/78 editions call Zealand "velforsvaret" (well defended) (KK2 pp. 570–571, 569; KK4 p. 51).

**1980s (Truslen 1980–1988)** — Marked softening on the amphibious axis, hardening elsewhere:
- Timing no longer assessable; the landing made contingent on the tactical situation, sea/air superiority, and the state of Danish defenses; explicitly **not expected simultaneous** with the Schleswig-Holstein attack (Truslen 1984, s. 140; KK3 pp. 464–465; KK4 p. 57).
- Earmarked forces (1984/1988 picture): Soviets on three sectors with a battalion each; Poles on two sectors with a reinforced landing regiment each; **no East German assault troops** (their amphibious training visibly ending); preceded by 1–2 Soviet airborne regiments + 1–2 air-assault battalions (100–200 transport aircraft per regimental group). Existing sealift could move the Soviet naval infantry regiment + half the Polish landing force; the rest only if East German landing ships were borrowed. Assault echelon "well under 10,000 men"; 1988 estimated the follow-on at 1–2 Soviet divisions (KK3 p. 465). The Polish 6th Airborne and 7th Landing "divisions" of 1970s assessments were now carried as brigades (KK3 p. 431).
- The wider Coastal Front picture: 19–25 WP divisions after mobilization (5th GDR Army, 1st &amp; 3rd Polish Armies, 11th Soviet Guards Army), ~1,100 aircraft, ~500 helicopters; two operational groups, one toward Denmark. FE admitted NATO had only "sporadic knowledge" of actual Eastern war plans and ran multiple force-mix variants without ranking them (KK3 p. 462).
- **Preparation/warning assumptions**: variants of quick attack after ~1 week's preparation vs. 11–14 days; **from 1985 FE judged 11–12 days' preparation most likely** (optimal balance of superiority vs. surprise) — basis for the change not stated (KK3 pp. 462–463). Strategic warning doctrine (from Truslen 1978): political warning expected, **48 hours to 8 days**; tactical warning in the worst case: none (KK2 p. 566, carried forward).
- From 1984 an isolated attack on Denmark alone was written off entirely ("sandsynligheden … meget ringe") (KK3 p. 466). War probability was rated low throughout, as in the 1970s (KK3 pp. 455–456, 466).
- **Countervailing 1987 hardening**: FE paper to the Defence Ministry, 26 May 1987 (Nr. 526/87), argued WP had shifted weight to the North German sector — a corridor to the North Sea coast breaking NATO's north-south cohesion — i.e. an *increased* threat to Jutland/Denmark, echoing US analyst Philip Petersen and probably NATO MC-161-line thinking; this worst case steered Danish planning into the early 1990s, even as FE's own amphibious picture was softening (KK3 pp. 463–464). Even the 1990 planning basis retained an 8-division attack up Jutland and "amphibious operations against the isles" as capability-based worst cases (KK3 p. 426).

## (b) Indicators &amp; warning — what fed the picture

- **Standing WP naval patrols** off Danish waters: the Kriegers Flak (off Møn) and Trelleborg pickets; March 1984 the Soviets took over Kriegers Flak from Poland (better SIGINT fit against the main Western traffic route) — FE tracked such swaps as signal; patrol moved south of Møn Dec 1987, system wound down 1990 (KK3 pp. 443–444).
- **AGI surge as a NATO-exercise barometer**: up to five WP intelligence ships "normally" in Danish waters during NATO naval exercises; FE explicitly normalized this ("normal reaction to Western naval activity", EO Sept 1984) (KK3 p. 443). WP ELINT coverage of Able Archer 83 was reported as *ordinary*, "corresponding to WINTEX-series levels" — FE's only mention of Soviet behavior during that episode (KK3 pp. 443–444).
- **Circumnavigations of Zealand** by WP warships logged as a normal element; anomalies flagged (three Soviet missile corvettes rounding Zealand, March 1984 — strongest force ever to do it; single landing-ship circumnavigations 1981, 1982; none 1983). DIIS retrospectively reads 1984 as a power demonstration amid 1983–84 tension; FE at the time offered no assessment (KK3 p. 444; KK4 p. 54).
- **Air activity counts**: recon sorties against Danish/FRG radar and communications tallied monthly (112 in June 1984 vs. 80 in 1983, 83 in 1985); ECM training flights ~10 days/month; chaff corridors up to 700–800 km logged (KK3 p. 453). Soviet "penetration flights" toward Rügen since 1969 were treated in Truslen's warning analyses as compressing air warning "to a limited number of flight-minutes" (KK2 pp. 549–550).
- **Mobilization indicators**: during the Dec 1981 Polish crackdown FE tracked raised readiness in the East German, Hungarian, Czechoslovak armies, GSFG, Baltic and Carpathian MDs and Soviet airborne — concluding no invasion imminent "but one could be launched at relatively short notice" (KK3 p. 428).
- **Erosion of collection**: the GDR's 1 Jan 1985 extension of territorial waters 3→12 nm was assessed to reduce Danish naval/air collection "in essential degree" and to degrade warning (KK3 p. 448). The Klaipeda–Mukran rail ferry (opened Oct 1986, six large ferries planned by 1990) drew press speculation; FE judged it of limited military value since it couldn't survive wartime (KK3 p. 448).
- **Order-of-battle/OOB indicators**: basing changes read directly into the threat — e.g. Laage airfield converting to naval Fitter-K jagerbombers (Nov 1985, confirmed within the year; ~65 aircraft by Nov 1986) meant "increased possibility of air support for amphibious operations in BALTAP's area" (KK3 p. 454); SA-5 then SA-10b coverage reaching south Copenhagen (KK3 pp. 453, 455); Foxbat-F standoff missiles able to hit Danish radars from WP territory (KK3 p. 454).
- Counter-flow (red's I&amp;W against blue): Polish "yacht" reconnaissance of Danish harbors 1950s–1980s, ~30–40 Soviet/Polish/GDR diversionary (spetsnaz) patrols earmarked against Danish nodes per FE 1980 (confirmed post-war), Eastern truck/student reconnaissance of coasts and the new Farø bridges in the mid-80s (KK4 pp. 58–59; KK3 pp. 557–558).

## (c) What observed WP exercises did to the assessment

Exercises were the primary calibration instrument, and in the 1980s they mostly **calibrated downward**:
- Joint Sojus-81/-83 amphibious phases judged to "continue to represent a modest training level." Soviet national exercises impressed more: Zapad-81, and a 1983 exercise whose lift FE sized at the Soviet naval infantry brigade (~3,000 men), notable for a first night landing — but Western analysts noted the persistent absence of realistic minesweeping and integrated ASW (KK3 p. 446).
- Saltnicken landing exercise, 14–15 Aug 1986: FE reconstructed the wave structure (seven Aist hovercraft first wave; Ivan Rogov, 13,000 t, fourth wave; two merchant ships participating for the first time since 1981). Key inference: participation of 6th Guards MRD (Soviet forces in Poland) meant that division "had landing operations as an option" — i.e. could be committed against Denmark; and integration of non-naval air meant non-Baltic-Fleet aviation could appear over Denmark (KK3 p. 446). Note the mid-80s texture: the most granular exercise readout of the decade, and it *added* a division to the earmarked list even as overall amphibious weight declined.
- Nov 1986: Ivan Rogov left the Baltic for the Pacific (too big/deep-draft for the Baltic per FE) — assessed as a **15% cut in Soviet Baltic amphibious lift** (KK3 p. 446).
- Poland's June 1985 reinforced-regiment landing exercise drew the comment "it is many years since a national Polish landing exercise of this order has been observed"; later Polish exercises routine and small (KK3 pp. 446–447).
- GDR: the amphibious-trained 29th MR Regt moved from Rügen to Hagenow in 1981 (read as a downgrading of the landing threat); amphibious training tapered off, none from 1987. **FE refused to be reassured**, holding open that GDR lift would be lent to the USSR/Poland (Truslen 1984, s. 47, 65) (KK3 p. 447).
- Bottom line (DIIS's characterization of FE): given lift of only "two-three regiments" and unrealistic exercises, it was debatable whether WP had a genuinely offensive landing capability at all — FE even quoted Baltic Fleet chief Adm. Ivanov (1989) saying his amphibious forces were insufficient for offensive landings — **yet FE maintained the worst-case interpretation** that merchant hulls could substitute for missing lift, so an offensive landing "under certain circumstances" still had to be expected (KK3 pp. 447–448). Similarly, 1988's striking (and correct) new judgment that the USSR was moving away from offensive naval operations in the Baltic was noted but not allowed to restructure the threat picture (KK3 p. 452; Truslen 1988, s. 76).

## (d) Could a landing succeed? Sea denial and correlation of forces

Blue's own view:
- **Mines were the acknowledged center of gravity.** From the 1986 edition FE stressed the decisive importance of the sea and anti-invasion minefields; WP would have to clear them to land with fighting power, and clearing would take **several days *after*** air and sea supremacy had been won (Truslen 1986, s. 157). DIIS's gloss: "if the operational preconditions were not created, a landing would be suicide — this, however, was not written down" (KK3 pp. 465–466).
- FE rated WP Baltic navies quantitatively superior but qualitatively wanting: obsolete submarines, inadequate ASW and **mine countermeasures**; able to meet peacetime tasks, "hardly" wartime ones; from Truslen 1985 doubted WP could even monitor the compact Baltic battle picture against Western EW-heavy offensive thrusts (KK3 p. 452). The West retained tactical/technological naval superiority, foreseen used for deep strikes into the Baltic (KK3 p. 467).
- 1965-era: even local WP air superiority (≈200 aircraft) could not stop a few NATO nuclear-capable jets getting through to strike the landing itself — i.e. Denmark signposted that the invasion fleet could not be made safe from tactical nuclear attack (KK2 p. 528).

Red's own view (retrospective, confirming blue's arithmetic and worse):
- Zapad-81 (Adm. Kapitanets' memoirs): 75 vessels landing 2,500 men in an exercise openly supposing the Danish isles; it exposed **clearly insufficient minesweeping capacity**, need to conscript civilian vessels; his verdict — seizing the straits zone "was no simple task. Its solution was obstructed by an entire state, which had to be blockaded, destroyed and forced out of the war" (KK3 p. 545). Okean-83 allowed until D+8/9 just to gain sea control on the Nakskov–Sønderborg line (KK3 p. 546).
- Adm. Theodor Hoffmann (Volksmarine chief): Danish/German mines "a main danger"; if the barriers were laid as planned, a landing on the Danish isles would be "extremely complicated, if not impossible"; the only effective countermeasure was destroying the minelayers and depots at war's outbreak (KK3 p. 548). GDR internal 1989 math: Volksmarine + Soviet support could kill 15 of Bundesmarine's 40+ missile boats, 3–5 of 15 destroyers/frigates, 3–4 of 24 submarines — a losing exchange (KK3 p. 547 n. 27).
- Polish naval staff view: from 1971 growing mine danger pushed the joint landing operation "into the background" (KK2 p. 631). The landing had been slipped from D+2 (1961) to D+4 (1968) to **D+5 (1970s–80s)** precisely to first win sea/air control — at the cost of facing a fully mobilized Danish anti-invasion defense (KK2 p. 628). Landing sites: only Køge Bugt and Fakse Bugt ever appear in WP exercise material (surveyed by Polish intelligence 1964–65); Kapitanets personally inspected both in Sept 1978 and found neither well suited — stones, easy defender access, forts and coastal batteries, mine threat (KK2 p. 629). 1970s Polish allocation for the landing: **17 nuclear weapons (12 missiles, 5 bombs, 3–50 kt)** against the Stevns coastal area, on the premise NATO used them first (KK2 p. 630).
- DIIS overall verdict: barring a war going very badly for NATO, **the WP in the 1980s lacked the capacity** to take Jutland or to force the straits and land on Zealand; at most a defensive line at the southern entrance to the straits, with Lolland, Falster and Møn contested (hard to defend against helicopters and minor landings) — and even that only until ~1984, after which the Eastern fleets planned only defensively (KK3 p. 550). The GDR refused throughout the 1980s to raise naval infantry despite Soviet pressure (protocolled after Sojus-83), "not wishing to take part in an operation which under the prevailing correlation of forces at sea and in the air had to be regarded as suicide" (KK3 pp. 548–549).

## (e) Threat-in-being and Danish posture

- **The covering-force problem defined Zealand's garrison**: 1965 math — standing Zealand covering force of one brigade vulnerable to a surprise D-day landing; mobilization from D+2 yields three brigades, forcing the enemy to a division-plus assault; total planning balance 22,500 field-army troops + 18,500 Home Guard vs. an invasion force of ~25,000 incl. an airborne division — numerically even but the invader better equipped (KK2 pp. 527–528). This is the arithmetic that kept roughly half the Danish army pinned east of the Great Belt.
- **Anti-invasion minefields + coastal forts** were the backbone: controlled minefields in the Sound and against a landing in Køge/Fakse Bugt, covered by Stevnsfort's 150 mm twin turrets (23 km range) and Langelandsfort; mine stocks at depots (e.g. Masnedø, to 1973); FE's Truslen from 1986 made the minefields' establishment the pivotal Danish act (KK3 p. 465; koldkrig-online.dk/anlaeg/stevnsfort/). By the 1980s Stevnsfortet's own threat picture had migrated from invasion fleet to "surprise attack with paratroops, helicopters and fast nuclear-armed bombers," answered with a HAWK battery (1984) — a miniature of the wider shift.
- The landing threat justified nuclear-capable anti-invasion defense in NATO planning language (the 1965 study's point that NATO tactical nuclear weapons could reach any landing zone) (KK2 p. 528).
- Meanwhile Jutland's field army pointed the other way: Jyske Division's war task was rapid deployment to prepared positions at Lübeck for static forward defense; Denmark did *not* expect to face the enemy's main effort or gross numerical superiority in its own sector (KK3 p. 464).
- Politically, the sustained worst case underwrote continuity: FE's signal as late as 1990 was that there was "no intelligence basis for decisively changing the Danish defense" (KK3 p. 426).

## Perception vs. reality — the divergences DIIS flags

1. **Who would take Jutland**: FE assumed Soviet-led operations with mixed forces, and in the 1980s gave 5th GDR Army a leading role in quick-war variants. Reality: the assault was a *Polish* front task (Poland requested it in 1961), retained on paper to 1987; East German generals (Streletz, Goldbach) deny ever holding primary war tasks in Jutland (KK2 pp. 567, 632; KK3 pp. 538–539; KK4 pp. 55, 57).
2. **Number of landings**: FE assumed one landing (Fakse Bugt) absorbing all WP amphibious capacity. Reality: at least two — the GDR regiment was earmarked for Falster (later Lolland/Langeland per Transit-82: Lolland D+6, Langeland D+7), separate from the Zealand assault (KK2 p. 568; KK3 p. 548).
3. **Timing philosophy**: FE's 1970s belief that the Zealand landing was top-priority, "could not come early enough." Reality: red had already slipped it to D+5 out of respect for exactly the defenses (mines, nukes, mobilized defenders) FE knew about; FE's own 1980s position (landing conditional, not simultaneous) converged on the truth (KK2 pp. 567–568, 628; KK3 pp. 464–465).
4. **The mid-80s inversion**: from 1984 the WP exercise picture turned defensive (Herbstwind-84/85 repelling *Western* thrusts from the Øresund; a Femern-directed landing at most; last joint landing exercise June 1986 a "final play-through of old tasks that could no longer be solved"); the GDR had abandoned its Denmark landing as unfeasible by 1980. FE saw the pieces (GDR training stopped; Ivanov's admission; the 1988 "defensive turn" note) but retained the worst-case merchant-shipping construct, and in May 1987 actually *raised* the assessed threat to the Jutland axis under American influence — "ud fra diskutable præmisser," as DIIS puts it — an assessment contradicted by the Eastern exercise record and kept in the Danish threat picture into the 1990s (KK3 pp. 543–544, 546–547; KK4 p. 57).
5. **Warning apparatus pointing the wrong way**: the biggest 1980s Baltic anomalies FE wrestled with — the Swedish submarine incursions — were partly Western operations (Weinberger/Speed admissions), mis-scored as Soviet, inflating the perceived threat to the Danish/Swedish near area; and FE, tasked only with watching red, never registered that the era's destabilizing surge of activity in the Baltic was substantially blue (Baltops, psychological-warfare flights, ~1,100 ship/3,500 aircraft NATO sorties in Eastern operating areas in 1988) — while red's Operation RJaN watched blue's exercises for the start of a war (KK3 pp. 448–451, 459–461, 467, 551–552; KK4 p. 53).
6. **Aggregate**: DIIS concludes the threat to Denmark declined from ~1980 (decisively from 1985/87) and that Denmark was far more defensible in the 1980s than in the 1960s–70s; FE's worst case nevertheless "rådede til det sidste," with a measurable *sharpening* in the late 1980s (KK3 p. 554; KK4 pp. 56–58). Conversely, DIIS also endorses why the stakes justified caution: US Global War Games showed loss of Denmark would unhinge NATO's whole northwest-European position (KK4 p. 57, quoting Gile).
