# 13. The Visitor

The thirteenth and fourteenth days are quiet, and the quiet is worse than the battle, because everyone above the rank of major can read it.

The enemy pauses. Not everywhere and not restfully: he gives up the salient at the narrows in good order, passes the first echelon's spent divisions rearward through the Second Army's positions, keeps up a professional unpleasantness with his artillery against the Danewerk line and, for the first time in the war, against Rendsburg itself — harassing fire at the town's approaches, methodical, at hours chosen to cost sleep. We move the rest areas underground and our senior officers stop remarking on it, which is its own barometer. The deep raids come at the crossings again. The radio war thickens. But the front's great engine has visibly dropped to an idle, and Aakjær reads the idle off his board with the concentration of a doctor at a chest.

"He is reorganizing, resupplying, and deciding," the G-2 briefs on the fourteenth. "The first two are visible — his bridging is moving forward again, his artillery is thickening on the neck, his engineers work the roads behind him every dark hour. The third is not visible, and it is the only one that matters. A man six days behind his own plan, whose margin is spent, does not pause to rest. He pauses because his next act requires permission — his own, or somebody's — and because the act after a pause like this is not another push. It is an answer to the question his calendar has been asking him for a week." He lays down the pointer. "I can tell the General what he can still do. I can no longer tell the General what he will do, because from here forward his decision is not tactical. And I remind the staff of the season: from this week, every night is longer than the day, the ground bears tracked vehicles by morning frost, and the Baltic has perhaps three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who intends to use the sea. Whatever he intends, the calendar in his headquarters is now shouting."

Into this quiet, on the afternoon of the fourteenth day, at the commander's formal invitation, transported through the eastbound evacuation flow in a staff car whose driver has orders to treat him like glass, comes General a.D. Anselm Rahn — in the wet-slate civilian suit, with a briefcase that proves to contain nothing but a folded 1:250,000 of the Baltic Approaches, 1961 printing, annotated in a hand nobody is shown.

It is Hvidt who summoned him. I disclosed the Eckernförde calls the way I disclose everything now, expecting mild censure for the open line; instead the commander listened, asked two questions — *what does he refuse to give you* and *how quickly does he see* — and sent the invitation over his own signature: the corps requests the counsel of the General, one afternoon, on matters of operational assessment. "The Americans fly him across an ocean to ask him about encirclements," Hvidt says, flatly, signing. "He lives forty minutes from my war and his own army's protocol office sends him invitations to receptions. I decline to be the third institution that wastes him."

I watch my Danish general put his name under a German general's, and I keep my face still. Rahn was a subaltern in the East in the years my country had German soldiers on its own streets. And yet it was Rahn who taught me the operational art, in a lecture hall in Hamburg, an allied student among the German ones, sixteen years ago — taught it cleanly, without a gram of the myth, because he despised the myth first and loudest of anyone in the room. The sum does not resolve. Hvidt is not honoring a war. He is refusing to waste a mind. Those are different acts, and a Dane of my generation had better be able to tell them apart.

We give him the confession wall. My instinct was to prepare a briefing; Hvidt overruled it — "no theater; give him the instruments and the room" — and so we stand in a loose half-circle in the operations cell, the commander among us and pointedly not foremost, while an eighty-two-year-old man in civilian clothes reads our war off the plywood for forty minutes in a silence he makes no effort to soften.

He reads the two-times sheets. He reads the movement log's vehicle-hours, and the rationing order with the protests pinned under it, and the air program's projected-against-observed, and he spends the longest time, hands clasped behind him, before the two calendars — OURS and HIS — hung side by side in Roloff's draftsman hand.

"Who audits the enemy's column?" he asks at last.

"I do, Hr. General," says Aakjær.

"Against what do you audit it? Your evidence is his behavior. Behavior confirms a schedule only when it changes. His has now changed — the pause. So." He turns from the wall, and I watch him do the thing I remember from the lecture hall: refuse the lectern, walk into the middle of us, and begin taking our answers apart with our own tools. "You have written, here: six days behind, margin committed, initiative on the table. Fine sentences. I ask the staff of this corps the question its wall almost asks. You have kept his calendar. Where is his *wall*? What are the two times on the far side — the scheduled and the actual — of the thing he cares for most? Not ground. Ground is his reporting, and you have all learned this month what reporting is. What does his whole operation exist to produce, in one word, no *until*?"

Silence. It is, I realize, the ENDEX question, asked of the other army — and it is Roloff, of all of us, Roloff of the graph paper, who answers it, hesitantly, in the shared plain language:

"A port, Herr General. Working. Before the winter closes the sea."

"Whose winter?" says Rahn, instantly, softly. "Whose sea?" — and lets it hang, and the room does the work, and I feel the answer go through us like current through a wire, because the words are on the wall already, in Aakjær's own season brief from this morning: *three weeks of weather left in it for anyone who intends to use the sea.* The enemy's careful fires, the unbroken locks, the ports shelled with tongs: the intact things, promised to somebody — promised to *ships*. And ships obey a calendar no headquarters can argue with. The enemy's true wall — the scheduled-against-actual that governs every other number in his ledger — is not drawn on land at all. It is a sea-state table. His operation does not have six days of lag against a phase line; it has a fixed number of days of *Baltic* left. And the sea he is racing is mine. It closes over the water my children were born beside. Every day we have bought him at a company an hour we have bought, all along, though we price it only this afternoon, against that.

"Understand what you have and have not learned," Rahn says, into the room's changed silence, and now he is not soft at all. "You have not learned his intention; do not flatter yourselves; intentions are for novelists. You have learned his *constraint* — the one line on his wall that, when it is crossed, makes every other line worthless. His mass, his norms, his patience: they are all creditors, and the sea forecloses first. From this follows the only planning question worth this staff's remaining strength, and I will not answer it for you, because it is your war and my train of thought is eighty years old and stops at every station." He begins folding his ancient map, a man closing his own briefcase on the proceedings. "The question. A man who must have a working port before the sea closes, and who is late: what does his last week look like — and on which of its days is he *weakest*, not in strength, in *choices*? Find that day on your wall. Plan the corps backward from it. And permit an old soldier one remark he is too old to be disciplined for" — the pale eyes go around the half-circle, and stop, without theater, on Hvidt — "I have stood in a headquarters that was six days late against a closing season, on the other end of this sea, a lifetime ago. I can report to the corps commander what is being said this week in the enemy's operations room, verbatim, in any language he likes, because I have said it myself. It is: *there is still time, if.* Gentlemen, there is a species of officer who can make *if* last until the ice. Your work is to take the *if* away from him — visibly, provably, by his own arithmetic — before his superiors take the pen away from him. Because what his superiors write next" — he shuts the briefcase; the small click is the only sound in the cell — "is written with a different pen, and this old man has seen that also, and will not stay to dinner."

The other end of this sea. I hold the phrase and do not move. His closing season was fought at the far shore of the water that touches Fredericia, that touches the beach below my father-in-law's garden. He is not sentimental about it. Neither, I decide, standing here, can I afford to be.

He stays, in the event, twenty more minutes — ten of them alone with Hvidt behind a closed door, a conversation neither man ever summarizes — and is driven back west into the evacuation flow at dusk, one small gray car against the mattress-roofed current. We go back to the wall and stand before HIS CALENDAR, on which Roloff, without being ordered, has already pinned a fresh sheet with a heading that did not exist at noon.

It says: THE SEA'S SCHEDULE. And under it, in the G-2's hand, the first entry, transcribed from the meteorological annex with a date against it like a knife against a whetstone: BALTIC AMPHIBIOUS WINDOW, ASSESSED CLOSURE: 8–12 DECEMBER. EN REMAINING MARGIN AGAINST OWN MINIMUM PLAN: 17–21 DAYS. LESS TIME REQUIRED TO RESTORE PORT CAPACITY AFTER CAPTURE: NOT LESS THAN 10. EFFECTIVE DECISION SPACE: 7–11 DAYS.

Seven to eleven days. We have found the number under the theology at last — and it is not our number, and it never was. It is his. And for the first time since the chinagraph went up in October, the staff of Headquarters Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland stands in front of its wall and reads, in its own handwriting, not how the war might be survived, but how it might be *won*.

*Renderer's note. The two swaps compound hardest in exactly this set-piece. In the original, Rahn's "the other end of this sea" is a German recalling a German front; the mentor bond is unbroken lineage — teacher, student, one army's memory. Put a Danish narrator inside it, in present tense, and the sentence acquires a second floor: the closing season Rahn fought was contemporaneous with Denmark's occupation, and Holt cannot not do that arithmetic in the moment Hvidt signs the invitation. First person forces the reckoning into the scene rather than leaving it to narration; present tense denies Holt the retrospective settlement — he decides, standing there, what to feel, and the sum "does not resolve." The cell uniquely gains a live moral tension in the honoring, and the closing Baltic becomes possessive, Denmark's own water. It loses the original's clean pedagogical transmission: some of Rahn's authority now has to survive the narrator's wariness, which slightly dilutes the seminar's forward drive.*
