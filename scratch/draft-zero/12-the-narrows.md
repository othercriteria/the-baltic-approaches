# 12. The Narrows

The second echelon came in on the twelfth day, at dawn, under a ceiling of two hundred meters, and the day that followed was entered in the corps war diary under a heading of perfect administrative calm — DEFENSIVE BATTLE, SCHLEI–DANEWERK POSITION — and lived in the memory of every officer who served it as the day the corps nearly died.

The weather won the first hour. The maximum deep program stood loaded on its airfields — the approach routes east of Lübeck targeted folder by folder through the night — and the November sky shut like a shop. Of forty planned deep sorties, six flew before noon. The rest sat fueled under the overcast while the enemy's new army passed beneath the cloud on roads the corps had paid to know by heart, and Kjeldsen, who had built the request against his own advice about the season, stood at the air desk with the silent decency not to say so.

The blow fell where Aakjær's board had promised: the northern sector, the Danish division's ground — the division that had closed its last battalion into sector forty hours earlier, one hundred and eighty hours after the clock started on a plan that had promised ninety. Fresh regiments came through the first echelon's positions in the gray light, new equipment, new radio discipline, artillery preparation of a weight the war had not yet shown, and by 0800 the forward brigade's line on the Schlei narrows was broken in one place and bending in two, and the reports coming up the net had shed their formats and become voices.

What Vossen remembered afterward, in the way memory files a battle a man fights from a map table, was the queue. Every decision of the morning arrived at the same three-meter stretch of plexiglass and stood in line: the reserve — eleven battalions on paper, seven now, the rest already bled into the line by the week's attrition — release it whole, in halves, not at all; the banked shells — eleven days of solvency, the third of every daily scale withheld at the price of the protests still pinned to the wall — spend them now, here, or hold them for a *now* that might never improve on this one; the guidance line — for the breakthrough at the narrows pointed straight down the Schleswig road, at the line three capitals had drawn, and by 0915 the corps was again conducting two wars on one telephone exchange: the one against the Second Army, and the one for permission to fight it sensibly.

Hvidt fought both without raising his voice, and spent everything.

The banked scale went at 1040. Vossen wrote the release order himself — MASSED FIRES, ALL AVAILABLE, IN DEFENSE OF THE NARROWS — and the corps artillery, silent and hoarded for six days, opened with everything the rationing had saved, and for forty minutes did to the penetration's follow-on battalions what it had done in the exercise to a paper enemy at Hohn, except that this time Vossen stood at the counter-battery board watching the expenditure tick against Lammers' curves in real time, hour of shells against meter of ground, the two currencies at last laid naked against each other with no committee in between. The reserve went at 1225, not whole — two battalion groups with the last fresh armor, committed into the shoulder of the break under the division commander whose obstacle belt had schooled the corps a week before; Loheide's counterattack was not elegant, it was a door slammed by a tired man, and it held.

And the guidance line moved at 1400 — the morning's third battle, won in an office. Hvidt had put the question to Karup and Copenhagen at 0920 in the form the staff had built him through the long week of pricing things, and Vossen, listening on the second handset, understood that the confession wall had just fought its first political engagement. The commander did not plead necessity. He read out costs. So many companies per hour forward of the line, so many hours of civil clearance already achieved on the routes behind it — the Territorial Command's own figures — so much of the corps' remaining strength, priced against so many kilometers of political ground, with the request stated once, in the language of a man returning an audited account: *I can hold forward of Schleswig, or I can have a corps on Thursday. The two are now priced. I request the authority to choose between them, and I will own the choice.* The authority came back in ninety minutes, hedged in language for the archives, and the corps' line came back that night to the Danewerk position — the old wall, the thousand-year earthwork across the neck of the peninsula, a fact which every officer in the headquarters noted and nobody said aloud, because the day had used up everyone's capacity for history.

By dark the front had a new shape and the corps was still a corps. The narrows had held; the penetration was pinched to a salient being ground flat by fires; the Second Army's opening blow — its best, its freshest, delivered against a defense at its thinnest hour — had bought eleven kilometers and a wrecked lead division, and the line now ran where water, marsh, and the old wall made the peninsula's neck a funnel that could be held by tired men.

The price was posted on the wall at 2200, in Roloff's even hand, without commentary, which was the wall's whole doctrine. Shell stocks: the banked reserve spent to zero; Lammers' seven-day date now a five-day date, and the five days assumed convoys the marshalling yards were not currently promising. The reserve: three battalion groups remaining, none fresh. The covering divisions: the 6th Panzergrenadier at fifty-five percent of W-day combat power, the Jutland Division at seventy and falling, the numbers now audited by the wall's own discipline and therefore, for the first time in the war, believed.

And beside the corps' ledger, on the sheet headed HIS CALENDAR, Aakjær posted the day's other account, assembled from the wreckage: prisoners from four new regiments, all confirming Second Army; the passage of lines executed a full day later than the movement rates had projected — *his* schedule slipping too, even in his best hour; and at the bottom, boxed, the G-2's summary estimate, phrased with a care that Vossen read twice:

EN SECOND OPNL ECHELON COMMITTED AND BLUNTED. BY OUR RECONSTRUCTION OF HIS CALENDAR, EN NOW STANDS NOT LESS THAN 6 DAYS BEHIND HIS OWN PLAN, WITH HIS PRINCIPAL FRESH FORCE ENGAGED FWD AND HIS MARGIN COMMITTED. ASSESSMENT: THE OPERATIONAL INITIATIVE IS NO LONGER BEING EXERCISED BY EITHER SIDE. IT IS LYING ON THE TABLE.

Vossen stood before the two ledgers a long time that midnight, in the operations room gone quiet at last, the boards behind him red and exact. Twelve days. The corps had learned to see its own arithmetic, and had nearly been killed while learning; the enemy had spent his second echelon repairing a timetable and had not repaired it. Two clocks, both losing time, and between them — lying on the table, in Aakjær's phrase — the thing neither staff had yet reached to pick up.

He went out through the blackout curtain for one minute of air. The rain had stopped. The sky over the canal had cleared to a hard scatter of stars, and the wind had gone around to the east, and on the sandbags of the entrance, in the small hours of the thirteenth day of the war, the first frost of the winter was forming — the ground, which had been the defense's ally in the mud for two weeks, beginning, indifferent as all allies, to change sides.
