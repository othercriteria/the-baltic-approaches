# 8. The Crossing

Hvidt went forward on the seventh day, and took his G-3, and the trip was itself a small scandal — corps commanders at war belong to their maps, said thirty years of procedure, and the roads north were a contested resource the commander's own escort would consume — and Vossen understood within an hour that the scandal was the point. Hvidt had read the boards for a week. The boards were green. The war was not. He went to look at the place where the difference was being manufactured.

They went to the Eider first, to the crossing control point at Friedrichstadt where the movement log with the exchange rates was kept, and stood in a drizzle fine as breath on the embankment above the bridge, and watched the corps' circulation the way a doctor watches a vein.

It was mid-morning, military precedence, Yellow-3 closed. The southbound flow was the Danish division's tail: the second battalion of a brigade that the ninety-hour clock had promised six days ago, self-propelled guns under netting on transporters, then trucks, trucks, trucks, bridging sections on their monstrous trailers taking the bend above the bridge at walking pace, each one — Vossen counted — each one paying eleven, twelve, thirteen seconds of bridge to get its length across. The northbound verge held the other war: a recovered tank on a transporter going back for rebuild, two ambulances, and a police-escorted counterflow of empty civil buses returning for tomorrow's columns. In the gaps — there were gaps; that was the revelation; he had not expected the gaps — the bridge stood empty, thirty seconds, fifty, while the next serial closed up from the staging area at Schwabstedt where a Danish movement lieutenant with a signal lamp was holding vehicles at intervals that had been calculated in some staff college against air attack and were now, in the event, being enforced against physics: bunch them, and a stray shell or a shed load closes the bridge with a hundred vehicles packed on the wrong bank; space them, and the bridge runs empty seconds all day, and the empty seconds were the war.

"Tell me what I am looking at," said Hvidt. He said it the way he had said everything since October: buying, not testing.

Vossen looked for a long time before he answered, because the annex answer — *a route element operating at capacity* — had died somewhere on the drive up, between the third stationary column and the staging area's patient, lethal arithmetic, and what stood in its place had been assembling itself since the night Lammers said *the depots are not the division*.

"A valve," he said finally. "Herr General, the entire question of when the Danish division stands complete in its sector — the ninety hours, the clock this headquarters has been beating itself against for a week — reduces this morning to seconds of plank. Everything north of here can only arrive as fast as this crossing and the two like it pass vehicles. Everything south of here can only fight with what has passed. The depots could run at two hundred percent and it would change nothing that matters; the columns would merely queue deeper at Schwabstedt. The plan wrote the division's arrival as a sum of stages — mobilize, issue, march, occupy — and scheduled each stage to its own calendar, and I signed that annex too. But the stages are not brothers, Herr General; they are a chain, and this is its narrowest link, and the corps' true clock is not ninety hours and never was. It is this bridge's passage rate, multiplied by the hours we can keep it working, minus every empty second we are watching right now."

The drizzle ticked on the map cases. Below, a bridging trailer crawled its thirteen seconds.

"Then why," said Hvidt, "is there a lieutenant at Schwabstedt spacing the serials to a staff-college interval, and a colonel in Rendsburg — I signed his priority list — feeding this valve on a schedule built from when units *become ready* rather than from when the bridge *becomes empty*? If the crossing is the corps' clock, who serves the crossing?" He turned from the embankment. "The answer is nobody. Everybody serves his own stage of the chain, to his own green board — the depots their intake, the march groups their tables, the bridge its safety interval — and each of them is performing well, Vossen, that is the obscenity of it, every one of those men will end this war with an excellent report. Come. We are going to go and be unreasonable."

---

What followed went into the corps war diary as three unremarkable orders issued over the commander's signature between 1400 and 2200 on the fourteenth of November, and into Vossen's education as the day the abstraction grew hands.

The first order subordinated the chain to the link. The crossings — Friedrichstadt, the rail bridge at Sorgbrück, the heavy ferry at Nordfeld — became the corps' clock: a single movement authority at the Eider, answering to the G-3, empowered to call serials forward from staging at the rate the crossings actually passed them, minute by minute, weather by weather, and to hold everything else regardless of whose table said otherwise. Release to the roads would follow the valve's rhythm, not the depots'. "If the bridge is drinking," was how the Danish movement colonel put it, translating the order into something his lieutenants could love, "the bottle pours. If the bridge is full, the bottle waits. No pouring because the bottle is proud of being full."

The second order attacked the empty seconds. Staging moved closer — under camouflage the staff college would have wept over — so the call-forward interval dropped from minutes to tens of seconds. The safety spacing was cut by half, by name, on the commander's authority, the risk written down and signed for: one shell on a bunched column against three hundred bridge-seconds an hour, priced against each other in plain language in an order every officer on the route would read. The rail bridge, held till now for the ammunition trains, got a second life between trains passing tracked vehicles on flatcars — Lammers produced the loading tables out of some drawer of his memory where the whole Reichsbahn apparently lived.

The third order was the one Vossen drafted with the strange feeling of writing a sentence he had been walking toward since a party in October. It stopped things. It took the corps' engineer effort off two lower-priority route improvements — projects with schedules, with green boards of their own, with officers whose whole war had been those culverts — and put every company of it onto the crossing complex: approach repair, redundant ferry sites, the anti-air umbrella thickened, dummy bridging built at Süderstapel with the carpentry platoon and the honesty of a poker hand. Nothing in the third order created anything. It only subordinated — that was the word he used in the order, *nachgeordnet*, subordinated — everything the corps did within fifteen kilometers of the Eider to the single number nobody had owned a week ago.

The number responded. Not dramatically; rivers do not applaud; but the crossing log for the fifteenth showed passage up forty percent on the fourteenth, and the staging queues at Schwabstedt down by half, and the Jutland Division's closing — the corps' oldest sorrow — moved for the first time since W-day faster than its revised projection. Roloff plotted the two curves on the wall beside his graph paper, scheduled against actual, and for one day the actual crossed to the good side of the scheduled, and half the night shift found an excuse to walk past it.

Hvidt allowed the wall its day. Then, at the evening conference on the fifteenth, he stood in front of the curve and delivered the shortest lecture of the war, eleven words, into a room that had begun, just perceptibly, to feel that it had solved something:

"Now. What does the corps run out of *next*, and when?"

Lammers had the answer already typed — Vossen saw the sheet come out of the G-4's folder and understood that the two of them, the commander and the quartermaster, had arrived at the same place down different roads, the way the war kept teaching the staff its sections' walls were the confusion. The corps' artillery had fired, in seven days, one hundred and eighty percent of the planning rate that the ammunition stocks had been sited against. The forward sites were drawing down the way a tide goes out — undramatic, universal, visible only if someone laid the curves together and read the date where they met. Lammers had laid them together and the date was in the folder, and it was nine days away, and the convoys from the depots in the rear could change the date only if they held the roads that the Danish division's tail and the civil columns and the engineer effort were also holding, and every one of those had a green board of its own.

"The bridge is drinking," said Aakjær into the silence, with his terrible mildness, "and now we learn the well is not deep. I offer the observation, Hr. General, before someone celebrates: constraints are not defeated. They move. Somewhere across the water, I promise you, a thin man with my job is watching his own curves cross, and his date is also nine days away, or seven, or twelve — and the campaign, though neither headquarters has said it aloud yet, has become a race between two arithmetic problems." He looked at the wall, at the one good curve. "We have just spent a week learning to see ours. The war will be decided by which staff first learns to see the *other man's*."
