# 1. ENDEX

The war ended at eleven minutes past two on a Friday afternoon, and Oberstleutnant Georg Vossen heard it end the way he had heard it conducted for nine days: through a loudspeaker with a cracked cone, in English, in a Danish accent.

"All stations this net. ENDEX, ENDEX, ENDEX. Time one-four-one-one zulu. Acknowledge by station order."

The acknowledgments came in one by one, each in its own accent — the flat German of the division at Neumünster, the sing-song of the brigade at Fredericia, the BBC vowels of the British liaison officer who had spent the whole exercise being polite about the weather — and Vossen stood at the operations map of Headquarters Allied Land Forces Schleswig-Holstein and Jutland and watched a captain begin to peel the war off the plexiglass with a razor blade.

It came off in strips. Nine days of chinagraph: the red pencil-work of the enemy's three echelons, the blue goose-eggs of the covering force, the phase lines named for birds — HERON, CURLEW, BRANT — the boundary between the German division and the Danish division running down the Eider like a property dispute, the neat cross-hatched boxes of minefields that existed only as referee's notations, the bridge symbols over the Kiel Canal annotated with demolition states that had changed four times in nine days. The captain worked from north to south. The enemy came off first, because the enemy was on top.

Under it all, when the plastic was clean, there was just the map again: Schleswig-Holstein in 1:250,000, the shape of the campaign that had not happened, would not happen, printed in the patient greens and browns of the Landesvermessungsamt. It was raining lightly on the real Schleswig-Holstein outside. It had been raining on the exercise too. The umpires had made them fight in it, which everyone agreed was realistic of the umpires.

"Herr Oberstleutnant." Major Roloff, at his elbow, with the folder. Roloff was the kind of officer who had the folder before you knew you wanted the folder. "The wash-up figures. Endstand as of ENDEX."

Vossen took it and did not open it, because he already knew what it said, because he had written half of it. BOLD LANCER '83 had gone well. Everyone had said so for three days, in the careful way professionals say it, touching wood in five languages. The corps had fought the standard scenario — ORANGE forces crossing the demarcation line on a Saturday morning with the usual tactful fiction about their nationality — and the corps had done what the General Defense Plan said it would do. The covering force had delayed. The demolition belts had gone in on schedule. The Danish division had married up with its wartime positions on time — on exercise time, anyway, arriving by an administrative march that had been walked through in three moves over two days, because you cannot close the E45 to Danish civilians in October for a rehearsal. The line had held on CURLEW on D+6 with, said the folder, seventeen percent overall attrition to friendly forces and a computed forty-one percent to the enemy first echelon.

Numbers like that had a shine on them. You could feel the shine through the cardboard.

"The Endstand looks very fine," said Vossen.

"Yes, sir," said Roloff, in a voice with nothing in it either way, which was one of the reasons Vossen liked him.

Across the operations room, under the good lights where the map tables were pushed together, the rest of the wash-up was assembling itself. Oberst Lammers, the G-4, had claimed a full table for his ledgers and was stacking them like a man building a defensive position of his own. Oberstløjtnant Aakjær, the G-2, stood with his hands in his pockets in front of the intelligence board, which still had its acetate on, because the enemy on Aakjær's board was not part of the exercise and did not stop existing at 1411 zulu. Aakjær was Danish, narrow, prematurely gray at forty-one, and he read the Baltic the way other men read a newspaper over breakfast, with an air of expecting bad news and finding it.

The working language of the headquarters was English. This was the standing joke of Rendsburg, too old now to be told as a joke: a hundred and forty Germans and a hundred and forty Danes, and not a native English speaker among them above the rank of corporal, all conducting the defense of the Baltic Approaches in the language of manuals none of their armies had written. Vossen had stopped noticing it his second month. He noticed it again only at moments like this, hearing Lammers say *shortfall* with the vowels of Osnabrück, and it struck him sometimes that the language was the most honest thing in the building. Nobody could be eloquent in it. You could only be correct.

At 1500 the commander came down for the internal wash-up — the family one, before next week's formal review with the umpire staff and the visitors from Karup and Kolsås. Generalleutnant Brauer had commanded LANDJUT for thirty-four months and was leaving it in eleven days, and he walked through the operations room the way a man walks through his own house when the moving crates are already in the hall: touching nothing, seeing everything.

"So," Brauer said, in English, because the rule was the rule even for him on his eleventh-to-last day. "Show me how we won."

They showed him. It took fifty minutes. Roloff ran the map with the long pointer; the G-3 section had built the brief the way the corps always built briefs, D-day by D-day, phase line by phase line. The covering force purchases forty hours forward of the canal. The demolitions go in — the briefing acetate had a tidy stepped diagram of them, belts one through four, like a staircase the enemy had to fall down. The enemy first echelon is attrited to combat-ineffectiveness against the main position. The Danish division completes its concentration by ninety hours and takes over the northern sector complete. The line holds on CURLEW. On D+8, conditions are assessed as favorable for local counterattacks to restore the forward edge.

Vossen delivered the assessment paragraphs himself. He heard his own voice doing it — level, fluent, a year of practice in the room and fifteen years of practice at the trade — and some part of him stood a little behind his own shoulder and listened. *Delay imposed on the enemy: sixty percent beyond planning norm. Friendly attrition within acceptable parameters. Ammunition expenditure consistent with planning rates.* The sentences had the weight of coins. You could stack them. They stacked well, and the stack was the corps' answer to the only question anyone ever asked it, which was: can you hold?

Brauer listened with his arms folded. He asked four questions in fifty minutes.

He asked why the fuel state of the Danish division's second brigade had gone amber on D+3. Lammers answered: an assumption in the movement table about civilian tanker requisition; in war it would be requisitioned; in exercise it was administratively simulated, and the simulation cell had processed it late. Laughter, brief and warm. The exercise's fault. Not ours.

He asked whether the counter-battery fight in the Hohn gap had used the corps' own guns or the divisional artillery. Both, said Vossen, by exception; the fire had been massed for forty minutes on D+5, and it had broken the enemy's leading regimental artillery group, and the umpires had assessed it generously.

He asked — mildly, the way he asked everything in his last month — whether anyone had computed what the forty minutes had cost in tube ammunition against what the ships had landed at Esbjerg by D+5.

There was a pause of a particular quality, the pause of a room checking which folder that answer was in.

"The expenditure was within the planning rate for the phase, Herr General," said Lammers at last, which was true, and was not what had been asked.

Brauer nodded slowly, and let it go, and asked his fourth question, which was about the weather annex, and was a joke, and got its laugh. At the end he stood up and said the things a departing commander says — that the staff had shown itself the equal of any in the Alliance, that the exercise proved the concept, that the incoming commander would take over an instrument in tune — and then he said one thing more, with the moving-crates look on him:

"Gentlemen. Nine days, and the largest formation any of us commanded against us was made of paper and had exactly the strength the scenario gave it, and arrived exactly when the timetable said, and wanted exactly what the narrative said it wanted." He buttoned his jacket. "Real enemies are less punctual. Note it in the record: the corps performed to standard. I have signed worse truths." A smile took most of the sting out. Most of it. "Thank you. ENDEX."

The reception afterward was in the mess, which had been a Wehrmacht riding hall once and had the ceiling to prove it, and there Vossen, holding a glass of Danish beer he did not much want, saw General a.D. Anselm Rahn standing under the far windows in a civilian suit the color of wet slate, entirely alone and entirely at ease, reading the framed photographs on the wall as if they were documents.

Rahn had been on the visitors' list as one line among forty: *Gen a.D. Rahn, A.* — invited to ENDEX receptions by standing courtesy, the way retired generals are, because he lived forty minutes away in Eckernförde and because someone in the protocol office kept an old list. He was eighty-one or eighty-two now. He had taught Vossen at the Führungsakademie sixteen years ago — one term, the elective nobody's career needed, *Operative Probleme in historischer Beleuchtung* — and had retired the year after, and Vossen had seen him perhaps three times since, at things like this, under windows like these.

"Herr General," Vossen said.

"Vossen." Rahn did not offer his hand; he offered the wall. "They have hung a photograph of the exercise of 1978. Here. You can see it was October then also. You can see the same three umpires' caravans in the same field near Owschlag. I believe it is the same mud." He turned. His face had gone to bone with age but the eyes had not changed since the lecture hall: pale, patient, unfair. "I am told it went well."

"It went well, Herr General."

"Yes. It always goes well." He said it without any edge at all, which was worse than edge. "I sat yesterday in the visitors' gallery for the counterattack discussion. Very disciplined. Your Danish colleagues especially — they argue better in English than my generation argued in German, there is less room in it for opera." He took a sip of what looked like mineral water. "Tell me one thing, as my former student, and then I will let you go back to being congratulated. Nine days. What was the corps *for*?"

Vossen looked at him.

"I ask it simply," Rahn said. "You wrote the assessment; I heard your sentences read back in the gallery — delay imposed, attrition inflicted, expenditure within norms. Fine sentences. Each one measures the corps against a number. I ask what the numbers measure the corps against. What was it *for*, the whole apparatus, the two divisions and the bridges and the forty minutes of massed fire that nobody costed against the port of Esbjerg —" so he had heard that too — "what was the single thing it existed those nine days to produce? Not to *do*, Vossen. To produce."

"To hold the line CURLEW," Vossen said, "forward of the canal, until —"

"Until," said Rahn, and stopped, exactly there, like a man setting down a glass.

The word hung. Vossen found, with an irritation he was too well trained to show and too honest not to register, that the sentence he had been about to finish had three different endings, and that he had briefed all three of them in the past year in different rooms, and that they were not the same ending. *Until the enemy culminates. Until reinforcement arrives. Until release of — until the situation develops.*

"You see," Rahn said, not unkindly. "It is an old man's question. It was asked of me once, in a different country, in a worse year, and I also knew every answer except the one that was wanted." He set the glass on the windowsill, precisely, next to the photograph of the mud of 1978. "Your exercise proved the corps can do what it rehearsed. Nobody rehearses the question. When you have an answer — one answer, one sentence, no *until* — come to Eckernförde and tell me, and I will tell you whether it was worth nine days." He was already turning away, courteous, finished, an old man leaving a party at six o'clock. "My compliments on the assessment, Herr Oberstleutnant. It was correct throughout."

Vossen watched him go — watched two colonels step out of his path without knowing they had done it — and stood with his warm beer among his congratulated colleagues, in the successful headquarters of an unfought war, with the word *until* lodged under his sternum like a fishbone.

Behind him, at the buffet, Aakjær the G-2 was saying to Lammers, in English, in his flat Baltic murmur: "...only interesting thing all nine days is what the scenario did not exercise. There was no shipping in it. I asked the umpires. In the narrative, the Baltic was quiet. Imagine — that sea, quiet." A short laugh nobody joined. "Whose narrative is that, I wonder."

Monday, the moving crates. Eleven days, the new commander. It had gone well; everyone said so. Vossen put down the beer unfinished and went back through the rain to his office, and did not look at the clean map, and could not have said why not.
