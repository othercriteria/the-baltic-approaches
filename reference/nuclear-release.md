## 1. The NATO release request chain, c. 1983

### 1.1 The lineage (period-knowable in outline; texts mostly archive-revealed)

- **Athens Guidelines, May 1962.** At the NATO ministerial meeting in
  Athens (4-6 May 1962), the US agreed to consult the North Atlantic
  Council before nuclear use **"if time permitted"** — the founding
  compromise between alliance consultation and decision speed.
  [NATO News, "Athens Guidelines," nato.int/en/news-and-events/
  articles/news/1962/05/04/athens-guidelines, fetched 2026-07-22;
  cross-confirmed by NATO's own Final Communiqué text for the same
  session, nato.int/docu/comm/49-95/c620504a.htm] The "if time
  permitted" hedge is the design seed of everything downstream: NATO
  never committed to a consultation *floor*, only a consultation
  *intent*.
- **Nuclear Planning Group founded, Dec 1966 / first met Apr 1967.**
  Established by the Defence Planning Committee (McNamara's Special
  Committee recommendation) as the standing multilateral forum for
  nuclear consultation short of full NAC session; NDAC handled the
  wider membership, NPG the integrated-command nations. [NATO,
  "Nuclear Planning Group (NPG)," nato.int/en/about-us/organization/
  nato-structure/nuclear-planning-group-npg, fetched 2026-07-22]
- **"Provisional Political Guidelines for the Initial Defensive
  Tactical Use of Nuclear Weapons by NATO"** — the NPG's first major
  product, worked through 1968-69 (progress "welcomed" at the May 1969
  London NPG ministerial) [NATO Archives Online, item title as cited,
  archives.nato.int/provisional-political-guidelines-for-the-initial-
  defensive-tactical-use-of-nuclear-weapons-by-nato, fetched
  2026-07-22; NATO Final Communiqué 4 Dec 1969,
  nato.int/cps/en/natohq/official_texts_26761.htm]. Ottawa NPG,
  October 1970, extended the guidelines to cover possible ADM
  (atomic demolition munition) use. [search-derived, NATO official
  communiqué series 1970 — archives.nato.int, not independently
  fetched; **flag: secondary characterization, not primary text
  read**] A further round of directive-updating is dated by NATO's own
  archive catalogue to **NDAC-D(72)2, "Updating of NATO Nuclear
  Planning Directives"** and **NDAC-D(72)3, Dec 1972** [NATO Archives
  Online, archives.nato.int/updating-of-nato-nuclear-planning-
  directives, fetched 2026-07-22] — this is the "1972 guidelines"
  DK's brief named; document titles and dates confirmed, full text not
  read (still classified/not posted). **The trajectory a 1983 officer
  would know in outline: provisional guidelines (1969) → ADM extension
  (1970) → updated/consolidated guidelines (1972) → standing NPG
  business ever since** — the vocabulary "political guidelines for
  initial use" is period-real even though the texts weren't public.
- **The mechanics, as period doctrine would state them:** a Major
  Subordinate Commander (e.g. COMBALTAP) or Corps Commander perceives
  a requirement, requests through the chain to the Major NATO
  Commander (for our theater, SACEUR, via CINCENT/SACEUR's own
  channel); SACEUR requests NAC political guidance/release; national
  authorities (US President for US-custody weapons; UK PM for UK
  systems; other governments as their own custody/host arrangements
  require) must separately authorize release of their national
  stockpile. [Synthesis of the two-track "military recommends,
  political releases, national governments transfer custody"
  structure common to the scholarly literature below — no single
  primary text lays out the full chain end-to-end in the open sources
  reached this session.]

### 1.3 The one hard, dated, sourced timeline: Exercise ABLE ARCHER 83

This is the best-sourced timeline claim in this document, and it
directly answers "what was the consultation timeline believed/
exercised to be" — with an important caveat stated up front.

**Source: Dr. Gregory Pedlow (SHAPE Historian), "Exercise ABLE ARCHER
83: Information from SHAPE Historical Files," NATO UNCLASSIFIED, 28
March 2013.** [Formerly-classified SHAPE historical note, released via
FOIA and hosted by the National Security Archive,
nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB427/docs/6.a.%20Exercise%20Able%20
Archer%20SHAPE%20March%202013%20NATO.pdf, fetched and read in full
2026-07-22] **Archive-revealed register — the document is 2013; the
exercise it describes is Nov 1983, inside our setting window.**

- ABLE ARCHER 83 ran **7-11 November 1983**, an annual ACE-wide Command
  Post Exercise (headquarters only, no troops) run by SACEUR's own
  headquarters (SHAPE) down through Major/Principal Subordinate
  Commands. Its explicit purpose: **"practise command and staff
  procedures, with particular emphasis on the transition from
  conventional to non-conventional operations, including the use of
  nuclear weapons"** — and specifically to drill "new nuclear weapons
  release procedures" revised after ABLE ARCHER 82.
- **Critical caveat, stated by SHAPE's own historian:** this was a
  *purely military* exercise. NATO's political authorities (NAC, heads
  of government) **did not participate**; the exercise's own Directing
  Staff (DISTAFF) *simulated* NATO political authorities, and two small
  military Response Cells at the US Joint Chiefs of Staff (Washington)
  and the UK Ministry of Defence (London) *simulated* the two nuclear
  powers' national political authorities. No national leader was ever
  involved. So the timeline below is **the staff's rehearsed
  procedural rhythm for a scripted political response, not a measured
  real-world consultation time** — a load-bearing distinction for how
  we use it in the book.
- **The scripted timeline itself**, drawn from the document's narrative
  (exercise calendar = scenario calendar, so real days ≈ scenario
  days):
  - Scenario opens 4 Nov (pre-exercise): Orange (WP) opens hostilities
    theater-wide; Orange first uses chemical weapons 6 Nov.
  - **7 Nov (Day 1):** SACEUR requests political guidance on nuclear
    use, early in the day.
  - **8 Nov, morning:** SACEUR requests initial nuclear use against
    fixed targets in Orange satellite countries.
  - **8 Nov, late:** request **agreed** — roughly same-day turnaround
    in the script.
  - **9 Nov, morning:** weapons "fired/delivered" in the exercise.
  - **9 Nov, late:** SACEUR requests **follow-on** nuclear use (initial
    use had not stopped Orange's aggression).
  - **10 Nov, afternoon:** follow-on request **approved** — roughly a
    day's turnaround.
  - **11 Nov, morning:** follow-on use executed; exercise ends on its
    long-planned schedule.
- Pedlow also states flatly, from 2006 interviews with senior
  participants including Air Chief Marshal Sir Peter Terry (who played
  SACEUR), that **no "war scare" or unusual Soviet reaction was
  recalled at the time** — directly rebutting the later "Able Archer
  war scare" popularization; not our subject here, but worth not
  contradicting if the book ever gestures at the exercise by name.

**How to use this number honestly:** the scripted political-approval
turnaround in ABLE ARCHER 83 was on the order of **under a day to
about a day** per request — but that is a staff rehearsal with
military officers playing the President and PM, explicitly designed to
drill the procedure rather than to reproduce the friction of an actual
crisis Cabinet. It is evidence that NATO's *staffs* worked to same-day
release turnaround as their internal procedural assumption; it is not
evidence that real consultation would run that fast. Both readings are
useful for the book: the G-3's staff *procedure* can be exactly this
brisk on paper, while the *political reality* the story cares about is
the open item in §7 — the gap between drilled procedure and real
Cabinet-level deliberation is itself dramatizable.

## 2. MC 14/3 — the actual 1968 text, read in full

**Source: NATO Military Committee, "MC 14/3(Final), Final Decision on
MC 14/3," 16 January 1968, enclosing MC 14/3 (Military Decision), 22
September 1967, "Overall Strategic Concept for the Defence of the
North Atlantic Treaty Organisation Area."** [NATO Archives Online,
*NATO Strategy Documents 1949-1969* compilation,
nato.int/content/dam/nato/webready/documents/Archives/stratdoc/eng/
a680116a.pdf, fetched and read in full 2026-07-22 — **primary source,
the declassified text itself**, not a secondary characterization.]
Adopted by the Defence Planning Committee 12 December 1967; superseded
MC 14/2(Revised). This is the doctrine a 1983 LANDJUT staff officer
would have absorbed in vocabulary and substance even though the
document itself stayed classified until the 1990s — flexible response
was public NATO strategy by name from 1967 on.

**Para 17 — the three types of military response** (verbatim
category names, the vocabulary a 1983 staff would actually use):

- **17a. Direct Defence.** "Seeks to defeat the aggression on the level
  at which the enemy chooses to fight... The direct defence concept
  includes the use of such available nuclear weapons as may be
  authorised, either on a pre-planned or case-by-case basis."
- **17b. Deliberate Escalation.** "Seeks to defeat aggression by
  deliberately raising but where possible controlling, the scope and
  intensity of combat, making the cost and the risk disproportionate
  to the aggressor's objectives and the threat of nuclear response
  progressively more imminent." Escalatory steps, "provided they have
  not previously been used as part of a direct defensive system":
  (1) broadening/intensifying non-nuclear engagement; (2) use of
  nuclear defence and denial weapons; (3) demonstrative use of nuclear
  weapons; (4) selective nuclear strikes on interdiction targets; (5)
  selective nuclear strikes against other suitable military targets.
- **17c. General Nuclear Response.** "Contemplates massive nuclear
  strikes against the total nuclear threat, other military targets,
  and urban-industrial targets as required. It can be forced upon NATO
  by a major Soviet nuclear attack. It is both the ultimate deterrent
  and, if used, the ultimate military response."

**Para 22-23 — escalation and decision-making, the exact language on
the release question:**

- Para 22a: "NATO must be manifestly prepared at all times to escalate
  the conflict, using nuclear weapons if necessary... These factors
  will dictate the level of aggression at which NATO will have to
  commit itself to initiate the use of nuclear weapons."
- Para 22c: "NATO should retain the initiative to use nuclear weapons
  under conditions where it is militarily or politically required."
- **Para 23, "Decision-Making Machinery"**: "In view of the speed with
  which the Warsaw Pact could develop military operations, the
  political and military control arrangements of the Alliance must
  allow for: a. A continuous assessment of the enemy capabilities and
  indications of attack. b. Decision-making machinery, capable of
  rapid decisions especially with regard to: (1) The declaration of
  alert measures... (2) The need for conventional military action
  while decisions are being made about mobilisation... (3) **The
  release of nuclear weapons.**" — the document states the *need* for
  rapid decision machinery on release explicitly, by name, as a
  recognized problem to be solved; it does **not** state a number of
  hours or days anywhere in the text. This absence is itself evidence:
  MC 14/3 flags release speed as a requirement, not a solved metric —
  consistent with Michaels's "deliberately vague" finding above, from
  the source document itself rather than from later commentary on it.

**Consequence for period-knowability:** a 1983 staff officer's working
vocabulary — "direct defence," "deliberate escalation," "general
nuclear response," "pre-planned or case-by-case" release — is this
paragraph's own words, sixteen years stable by 1983 (MC 14/3 and MC
48/3 "remained valid until the end of the Cold War" per NATO's own
summary literature, nato.int/cps/en/natohq/topics_56626.htm, fetched
2026-07-22). A staff estimate scene can use this vocabulary
unselfconsciously; it is not anachronistic to have officers say
"deliberate escalation" or debate whether a given step is "pre-planned
or case-by-case."

## 3. Danish nuclear policy specifics

### 3.1 The peacetime no-nuclear-weapons policy — origin

**H. C. Hansen, May 1957** publicly stated the government would not
accept nuclear weapons on Danish soil "under the present circumstances"
(*under de nuværende forhold*) — the origin of the "in the present
circumstances" formula that let the policy be publicly absolute while
remaining privately revisable. [Multiple secondary sources converge on
this, incl. the 1995 "Thule affair" reporting and Nikolaj Petersen's
scholarship; search-derived synthesis, specific primary Folketing
citation not independently pulled this session — fetched via search
2026-07-22, sources: nsarchive.gwu.edu "The United States and
Greenland, Part I" briefing book (2025 posting of period documents)
and secondary press coverage of the 1995 disclosure.] **The formula's
double life is the documented, citable fact:** the same year (1957),
Hansen separately and secretly told the US ambassador Denmark would
not object to nuclear weapons already staged at Thule Air Base in
Greenland, and asked the US to keep that letter from the rest of the
Danish government and the Folketing. [National Security Archive,
"The United States and Greenland, Part I: Episodes in Nuclear History
1947-1968," nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/nuclear-vault/2025-06-03/
united-states-and-greenland-part-i-episodes-nuclear-history, fetched
2026-07-22 — archive-revealed register, the "H.C. Hansen Paper" itself
was declassified only in 1995 (Danish Ministry of Foreign Affairs
"Thule Report")] So: the *public* peacetime-basing-ban formula and its
*private*, secretly-caveated exception are both period-real for 1957,
and the tension between them is the seed of the whole
ambiguity/fodnotepolitik story that runs to 1983-88.

**Scholar to chase further, not reached this session:** Nikolaj
Petersen has a specific paper titled "The H. C. Hansen Paper and
Nuclear Weapons in Greenland" (per his ResearchGate profile listing,
researchgate.net/profile/Nikolaj-Petersen-2, fetched 2026-07-22) —
the direct scholarly treatment; full text not obtained.

### 3.2 The fodnotepolitik period, 1982-88

**Period boundary confirmed 1982-88** — directly bracketing our
provisional Nov-1983 setting. [Cold War History journal abstract for
the standard cite: "'Footnoting' as a political instrument: Denmark's
NATO policy in the 1980s," *Cold War History* 12:2 (2011),
tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14682745.2011.558176, fetched
2026-07-22 — **abstract only, full text not reached**] Mechanism, from
the abstract and corroborating summaries: a centre-left parliamentary
majority (Social Democrats + further left) that did **not** control
government nonetheless controlled NATO policy by forcing the sitting
(Conservative-led, Poul Schlüter from Sept 1982) government to attach
**parliamentary-mandated reservations ("footnotes") to NATO
communiqués** it would otherwise have signed without reservation —
"Denmark and Greece made critical 'footnotes' to paragraphs in NATO
communiques dealing with intermediate range missiles" specifically.
[search-derived synthesis, multiple sources converging, fetched
2026-07-22] The government "chose to 'live with' footnoting in order
to keep power" — domestic political survival over alliance-mainstream
conformity. **For a Nov-1983 setting: the Schlüter government has been
in office just over a year and the footnote dynamic is already
running** — INF deployment decisions (the Dec 1979 Dual-Track decision
and 1983 Pershing II/GLCM deployment beginning) are exactly the kind
of NAC communiqué paragraph Denmark would footnote.

### 3.3 Non-participation in nuclear planning — the standing fact

**Denmark did not participate in NATO's Nuclear Planning
Group/nuclear-sharing arrangements** and, per the general
characterization repeated across sources, refused foreign troops and
military "materiel and installations" on its territory in peacetime —
the basis for calling Denmark (with Norway, similarly) a "footnote"
member even before the 1980s dynamic named above. [search-derived
synthesis; general-knowledge-level claim, widely repeated, not tied to
one primary citation this session — flag for a sharper single source
if the scene needs to attribute this precisely] This is a *standing*
policy predating fodnotepolitik by two decades — the 1980s parliamentary
maneuver gave a new mechanism to an old abstention, it didn't invent
Danish nuclear reluctance.

### 3.4 Wartime ambiguity — the open question, honestly logged

**This is the item DK's brief specifically asked for and the open
literature would not yield this session.** The task's own framing
("did Denmark accept nuclear support in war planning? BALTAP strike
plans with nuclear components — Danish participation in nuclear
planning despite the basing ban") was searched directly. Findings:

- General scholarly consensus-level language exists — e.g. a 2014
  *European Security* article abstract (Cindy Vestergaard, "Going
  non-nuclear in the nuclear alliance: the Danish experience in NATO,"
  *European Security* 23:1 (2014),
  tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09662839.2013.856300, abstract
  fetched via secondary summary 2026-07-22) is characterized by one
  secondary summary as describing Denmark's Cold War approach as
  including **"wartime ambiguity"** and "unstated provisions
  regarding nuclear weapons during potential conflict" — but this is a
  paraphrase of a paraphrase; the article's own text (paywalled,
  403'd on fetch) was not read, and no specific claim about BALTAP war
  plans containing nuclear strike packages was found stated anywhere
  in the open sources reached.
- **The archival reason this may be genuinely hard to source**: a
  search for Danish/BALTAP-specific nuclear war-planning scholarship
  returned, verbatim from one search synthesis, the observation that
  "due to the lack of declassification of the NATO regional command
  archives, including... BALTAP, specific perspectives simply cannot
  be researched" [search-derived characterization, fetched 2026-07-22
  — itself a claim *about* source unavailability, treat as a plausible
  but unverified secondary characterization, not a citable fact in its
  own right]. This is consistent with reference/landjut-front.md's own
  standing note that COMBALTAP-level primary documents are thin on the
  shelf and BALTAP org charts/Bogason scans remain a wishlist item.
- **What was found and confirms the "despite the ban" half of the
  question, indirectly**: BALTAP's OWN COMMAND STRUCTURE was
  integrated into NATO's nuclear-armed theater architecture regardless
  of Danish soil policy — a German deputy COMBALTAP, German LANDJUT
  corps troops, and (per §4 below) an actual US-custodied nuclear
  storage/delivery capability organic to LANDJUT's own German division
  (6. Panzergrenadierdivision), operating from garrisons inside
  Schleswig-Holstein, i.e. *outside Danish soil but inside BALTAP's
  own theater and under a command Denmark co-led.* Denmark's ban was a
  **soil** ban, not a **theater** ban — LANDJUT as a whole fought
  (on paper) with organic nuclear delivery means, and Denmark's own
  senior officers (COMBALTAP, alternating COMLANDJUT) commanded a
  formation that included them. Whether Denmark's own general staff
  saw or influenced the actual nuclear target list for LANDJUT's zone
  is the specific fact not found this session.

**Open item, stated plainly**: no source reached this session
confirms or denies that Danish officers had visibility into, or
influence over, a nuclear strike plan touching Danish territory or
BALTAP's own defensive zone. The "in the present circumstances" soil
ban and the "footnote" non-participation in NPG are both solidly
sourced; the finer wartime-planning-participation question is not.
Do not write a scene that asserts Danish officers *did* see such a
plan, nor one that asserts they definitely *didn't*, without another
research pass (DIIS Danish-language volume 3, 1979-91, already on the
shelf per landjut-front.md, is the most promising unmined lead — a
Danish-reading pass is the acquisition-queue item, not a re-search of
English open sources).

### 3.5 No Danish nuclear-capable delivery systems — confirmed

**Denmark operated the Honest John rocket** (MDAP-supplied, in
Division/Corps-artillery service **1959 to approx. 1973**) — the same
system other NATO armies fielded in nuclear-capable, dual-key
configuration — **but the Danish Honest Johns were conventional-only;
no source found indicates Denmark ever held or had access to nuclear
warheads for them.** [armyvehicles.dk, "Honest John rocket launcher,"
Danish Army Vehicles Homepage, fetched 2026-07-22 — a
hobbyist/enthusiast site, moderate confidence, but consistent with and
explained by the soil-ban policy above; no contradicting source found]
Denmark's later artillery/rocket inventory (post-1973) likewise
carried no Lance or other dual-capable system in Danish service in the
open sources reached. **This directly answers DK's brief's "verify"
instruction: Denmark had no organic nuclear-delivery capability,
consistent with (and likely a direct consequence of) the soil-ban
policy — the ban shaped Danish force structure, not just basing.**

## 4. The delegation question and nuclear custody nearest LANDJUT

### 4.1 Pre-delegation, in outline

**Pre-delegation authority** — advance Presidential authorization for
subordinate commanders (up to and including SACEUR, via CINCEUR) to
release nuclear weapons under specified circumstances without a
fresh, real-time Presidential decision — was a genuine, debated Cold
War design tension. [Wikipedia, "Pre-delegation authority,"
en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-delegation_authority, fetched 2026-07-22 —
tertiary source, use only for the outline, not for specifics] The
formal chain as generally described: "the President will release
nuclear weapons to CINCEUR for use as directed by SACEUR on the
authority of NAC or NATO member governments," and in wartime SACEUR
receives operational authority from the Alliance and, via CINCEUR,
transfer of custody for weapons allocated to SACEUR's use. [same
source] **Specifics of any standing pre-delegated authority active in
1983 were not found and are not knowable from open sources — this
remains classified or at minimum not surfaced in this session's
search.** Flagged as an open item (§7), not asserted.

### 4.2 US nuclear custody nearest LANDJUT — the two Schleswig-Holstein
sites, and they sit inside LANDJUT's own division

This is the best-sourced, most concrete finding in §4, and it lands
directly on ground reference/landjut-front.md already maps.

- **Sondermunitionslager (SAS) Kellinghusen** — a US tactical-nuclear
  storage depot ~3 km NW of Kellinghusen, Schleswig-Holstein, built in
  the 1960s, storing **203mm and 155mm nuclear artillery rounds (W-33,
  W-79, and W-48)** for German-manned, US-custodied delivery under the
  standard NATO nuclear-sharing dual-key model — German howitzer crews
  would fire the shells; US custodial troops held the warheads until
  release. [German Wikipedia, "Sondermunitionslager Kellinghusen,"
  de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sondermunitionslager_Kellinghusen, plus the 6.
  Panzergrenadierdivision veterans' site pzgrendiv6.de/pzgrendiv6/
  standorte/sondermunitionslager-kellinghusen.html, both fetched via
  search synthesis 2026-07-22] **US custodial units named**: 13th US
  Army Field Artillery Detachment (USAFAD), 1961-1992; 552nd US Army
  Artillery Group until 1966; 294th US Army Artillery Group from June
  1966. Closed 1991, post-reunification. **Kellinghusen is the same
  town reference/landjut-front.md already names as the garrison of 6.
  Panzergrenadierdivision's artillery regiment** — the nuclear-artillery
  custody sits at the division's own home station.
- **Sondermunitionslager Meyn** — a second US nuclear depot ~13 km
  WSW of Flensburg, operating **c. 1973-1992**, storing the warheads
  for **Raketenartilleriebataillon 650** (6. Panzergrenadierdivision's
  own rocket-artillery battalion, part of Artillerieregiment 6,
  garrisoned at Briesen-Kaserne, Flensburg-Weiche from April 1973).
  [pzgrendiv6.de battalion history page, fetched and read in full
  2026-07-22; German Wikipedia "Sondermunitionslager Meyn," fetched
  via search 2026-07-22] The battalion converted from **Sergeant** to
  **Lance** on 1 October 1976; Meyn held **W52** warheads (for
  Sergeant) and, from 1977, **W70** warheads (for the MGM-52 Lance).
  **Custodial unit: the 294th US Army Artillery Group** (the same
  formation as Kellinghusen from 1966) — i.e. one US custodial group
  covered both Schleswig-Holstein special-ammunition sites tied to 6.
  PzGrenDiv. The battalion's own unit history records a named nuclear
  exercise, **"atomare Flt u. VersÜb CLJ BATTLE BALANCE 77"** (June
  1977) — a live period-detail of the unit conducting nuclear-specific
  drills. The mission ended only in October 1991, post-reunification.

**Consequence — this is the exact "custodial units nearest LANDJUT"
DK's brief asked for, and it answers it precisely**: LANDJUT's own
German division (not an outside reinforcement) carried an organic
nuclear-delivery capability — corps/division artillery (155/203mm
shells, Kellinghusen) and a dedicated rocket-artillery battalion
(Lance, Meyn) — under standard dual-key custody (German firing crews,
US custodial detachment holding the warheads and the release key). No
equivalent Danish-service nuclear-capable unit existed (§3.5) — the
nuclear-delivery asymmetry between LANDJUT's German and Danish halves
is now a documented, usable texture: the corps' own nuclear teeth, if
they existed at all in wartime, would have been entirely on the German
side of the line.

### 4.3 Denmark and Lance/Pershing — verified negative

**Confirmed: Denmark fielded no Honest John/Lance-type nuclear-capable
system in Danish service** (§3.5); Pershing II and ground-launched
cruise missiles under the 1979 Dual-Track decision were deployed to
West Germany, Britain, Italy, Belgium, and the Netherlands — **not
Denmark**, consistent with the Danish footnote position on INF
specifically. [general period knowledge, corroborated by the
fodnotepolitik sourcing in §3.2 naming Denmark's footnotes on
"paragraphs... dealing with intermediate range missiles"] LANDJUT's
Pershing/Lance question, per DK's brief, resolves cleanly: any
dual-capable rocket artillery in the LANDJUT order of battle is
German (Raketenartilleriebataillon 650), not Danish, and Pershing II
proper (a corps/army-group-level system, not organic to a covering
force like LANDJUT) is not part of this corps' own order of battle at
all — it belongs to deeper reinforcement formations, not the front
LANDJUT itself holds.

## 5. The Soviet/WP side, for symmetry

*This paragraph deliberately stays brief per DK's brief's own
instruction — the full documentary base already exists in
reference/php-maritime-front.md and is not re-derived here, only
extended toward the 1980s.*

The 1964 ČSLA war-plan text states its own release rule outright:
**"Use of special ammunition — only with permission of the Supreme
Commander of the Unified Armed Forces"** [php-warsaw-pact-war-plans-
1964-dossier.pdf, PDF pp.19-20, already cited in
reference/php-maritime-front.md]. The 1967 Coastal Front directive
shows the same architecture in practice for our own axis: the
**"Supreme Command of the Unified Armed Forces delivers... 57 nuclear
strikes... in favour of the Coastal Front"** — the front *receives*
a strike package, it does not independently fire strategic weapons
[php-maritime-front.md §2, citing fac. p.2 of the 1967 directive].
Both documents predate 1983 but describe an architecture Bange's
scholarship (already on the shelf, cited in php-maritime-front.md §4)
explicitly traces forward without a structural break: SOYUZ-81 (March
1981) is dated as the **"first treatment of selective and increasing
use of nuclear weapons... broken down by targets and delivery
means"** in Polish Coastal Front exercise play — refinement of
release *mechanics*, not a shift of release *authority* away from the
top. Open-source Western characterizations of 1980s Soviet wartime
command consistent with this: the Stavka of the Supreme High Command
(VGK) would in wartime control strategic nuclear forces "through the
General Staff's Main Operations Directorate," with authorization
"passed from the VGK Stavka to the General Staff for implementation
via its command, control, and communications system" [Federation of
American Scientists, "Supreme High Command," nuke.fas.org/guide/
russia/agency/stavka.htm, fetched 2026-07-22 — tertiary
characterization, useful for outline only]. **Net finding: nothing
found this session refines the 1964/1967 documents' "release sits at
the very top, the front receives rather than fires" picture for the
1980s — if anything, SOYUZ-81's target/means breakdown suggests the
mechanics got more granular while the authority stayed exactly where
the 1960s documents already put it.** This matches, rather than
revises, php-maritime-front.md's existing "Consequences for the
instrument" §2.

## 6. Consequences for the one earned scene

What the G-3 (or a nuclear-request-adjacent staff officer at
Rendsburg) could procedurally see and do, on the record built above:

- **The vocabulary is MC 14/3's own, unselfconsciously used**: staff
  argument in the register of "direct defence," "deliberate
  escalation," "general nuclear response," and specifically whether a
  proposed step has "not previously been used as part of a direct
  defensive system" (the 17b qualifier that makes escalation a
  one-way ratchet by design) is period-exact and can be dramatized
  without hedging.
- **The request format is a real, drillable procedure with a known
  shape**: perceive requirement → corps/COMBALTAP recommends up →
  SACEUR requests political guidance/release → NAC-level political
  process → separate national-government release of custody (US
  President for US-custody weapons at Kellinghusen/Meyn; equivalent UK
  process for UK-custody systems elsewhere in theater). The staff
  *believed* — because it drilled — that this could move in
  roughly a day per request-response cycle (ABLE ARCHER 83's scripted
  turnaround, §1.3), while knowing (MC 14/3 §23's own unsolved "rapid
  decisions... release of nuclear weapons" requirement, and Michaels's
  "deliberately vague" finding) that the real political layer above
  the drill was not actually timed or guaranteed. **The dramatizable
  gap is exactly here**: the draft the corps revises and never sends
  (per nbc-conceit.md's mechanism) can be drafted *fast*, in the
  Direct-Defence/Deliberate-Escalation vocabulary, by officers who
  have rehearsed the form in exercises like ABLE ARCHER — and can
  still never be sent, because everyone in the room also knows the
  political layer above the drill is the genuinely slow, unrehearsed
  part.
- **Who drafts, concretely**: the G-3 (operations) with G-2 (enemy
  capability/intent) input, recommending up through COMLANDJUT and
  COMBALTAP to SACEUR — LANDJUT's own alternating GE/DA command
  structure (landjut-front.md) means this draft, if it ever moved,
  would cross a Danish-or-German command seam exactly like every other
  LANDJUT staff action, with the added edge that Denmark's own
  government would be receiving a request touching a NATO command it
  co-leads but a nuclear-planning apparatus it has never joined
  (§3.3).
- **The custody texture is real furniture, not invention**:
  Kellinghusen and Meyn (§4.2) are the corps' own division's nuclear
  teeth — German-crewed, US-custodied, entirely absent from the
  Danish half of the line. A scene can have a German battery
  commander at Kellinghusen or Flensburg who has trained on a weapon
  he has never seen a warhead for, and a Danish counterpart who has
  none at all — the asymmetry is documented, not authorial license.
- **Both clocks genuinely are slower than a fast-moving covering-force
  fight**: red's is "the front receives, it does not fire" — Moscow's
  Supreme Command holds strategic release throughout the 1960s-80s
  documentary record with no found relaxation (§5); blue's is a
  drilled staff turnaround of about a day *per request*, stacked on
  top of an unrehearsed and unquantified real political layer above
  it (§1.3-§1.4, §7). Neither clock is fast enough to matter inside
  the 90-hour covering-force window (landjut-front.md) unless someone
  starts it absurdly early — which is itself the escalation
  nbc-conceit.md's mechanism already identifies.
