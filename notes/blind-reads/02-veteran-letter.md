*Panel of 2026-07-25, session 52662a0d. Persona: retired Bundeswehr
Oberst, b. 1934, late-70s LANDJUT staff tours. Fully blind. NOTE:
the review corpus had 19/19a in swapped order (session build error,

Dear Mr. Klein,

Our mutual friend asked me to read your manuscript and to answer honestly, warning me that you would prefer bruises to compliments. This suits me. I served from 1956 to 1992; I commanded a Panzergrenadier battalion, did a division G-3 tour, and spent the late seventies in two staff appointments in Schleswig-Holstein, where my daily work was conducted alongside Danish colleagues very like the men you have written. I read your book in three evenings, with a pocket diary from 1983 beside me, and I will proceed in staff fashion, by paragraphs, because it is the only prose style I ever mastered.

First, the thing that must be said plainly: the subject of your book is staff work, and you have written it as though you had done it. Books about our war-that-never-came are always about tanks. Yours is about who owns a number, and whether the reporting system can tell a promise from a receipt, and what a bridge's empty seconds cost. Brauer's four questions at the wash-up; Hvidt's "orphaned assumptions"; the two-times order; the discovery that the crossing has no colonel — I sat in perhaps two hundred exercise wash-ups and no general ever asked the Esbjerg question, which is precisely your point, and it is a true point, and it hurt me to read it at age ninety-two as it would have hurt me at forty-five.

Second, the men. I have known Witzel personally; he had other names, but every headquarters of the old Bundeswehr contained him, initialing his signature file through the storm, and your sentence about the corps that stops filing at noon having decided by one o'clock that it is dying could be carved over the door of the Führungsakademie. Roloff I recognize with something close to pain — the pen that does not pause while his own street becomes a report line; we had such men, and they were the army. Lammers is true; Kjeldsen is true (the airman who restates everything in his own units before arguing — I worked with three of him at Karup liaison); Loheide is true, and his refusal to ask for the counter-battery is exactly right: they never asked. The elderly Territorial Oberstleutnant of reserves, the district administrator who never softened a figure, is the truest minor figure in the book. Bjelke, I regret to tell you, is also true. Aakjær I met in Danish uniform more than once — that dryness, deployed as an instrument, is exactly the Danish staff register, and your ear for the two armies' small courtesies (the frokost, the German rolling his acetates and the Dane rolling his, the language declined in both directions with Rahn) is the best rendering of that binational household I have read anywhere. Only Rahn feels partly assembled — the omniscient sage who is always right is a literary convenience — though his refusal of the blessing in Chapter 17 redeems him, because that refusal is what a real teacher would have done.

Third, and here I must reach for the red pencil. Rahn is eighty-two in October 1983, which makes him born in 1901; Chapter 13 calls him "a German general born in 1921." These cannot both stand, and I urge you to keep 1901 and strike 1921: a man born 1901 was a staff officer in Kurland in 1944 — a headquarters six days late against a closing sea, on the other end of that water — and your own beautiful allusion only works for the older man. Born 1921, he ends the war a Hauptmann at best and teaches nobody anything in 1967.

Fourth, the command arrangements. You have handled Holt's rank honestly — an Oberstløjtnant does not hold a corps G-3 desk, and you know it, and Witzel's "the chair is vacant, which is a different administrative condition" is exactly how such a thing would be spoken of; I have heard that sentence, nearly verbatim, about a different chair. A Danish Generalløjtnant as COMLANDJUT, however, is your one large licence. In my years the commander was German and the senior Dane sat as deputy; the balance was tended like a garden. I do not say you must change it — Hvidt is the best general in the book, and a Dane ordering the corps' economies gives you your drama — but you should know it is a licence, and you might let one German officer feel it, once, aloud. Which brings me to the related softness: a Danish commander trades away Holstein villages while Loheide's soldiers bleed on their own home ground, and the book lets the alliance's courtesy hold throughout. Our courtesy was real, but it had exactly that fault line, and in a real November it would have spoken at least once, ugly, in some corridor. You are entitled to one such scene and the book is thinner without it.

Fifth, geography and the calendar, where you are almost embarrassingly good. The Danevirke facing south; Sehestedt on the canal; the Friedrichstadt complex with the Marschbahn's swing-bridge and its sleeping operator; the single track south of Vamdrup; the E3 by its 1983 name; the Hohn gap; and your weekdays are correct — the warning order on Sunday the sixth, the attack on Tuesday the eighth; I checked. Two faults. In Chapter 2 you put rail ferries on the Halsskov–Knudshoved run; the rail ferries ran Korsør–Nyborg, and your own Chapter 15 gets it right — reconcile them. And your exercise is called BOLD LANCER; the real series in that country was BOLD GUARD, which you may of course have bent deliberately. Also: "forty thousand reservists of the Jutland Division" is too many — that is nearer the whole Jutland field army; the division is a fraction of it.

Sixth, my one operational objection of weight: your headquarters never moves. A corps main HQ static in Rendsburg for a month of war, its compound politely spared — this is the book's greatest kindness to itself. We planned to fight from displacing, dispersed command posts precisely because the enemy's missiles and his Spetsnaz did not extend such courtesies, and your wonderful wall of paper would have had to survive being taken down, trucked, and re-pinned in the rain, three times — which, I gently submit, is a better story, not a worse one. Similarly, the forcing of the Kiel Canal — the single greatest obstacle event of your campaign — happens between chapters. I wanted that night, and you owed it to me. And I assume the ordering of "19a — The Pocket" before "19 — The Door" is a draft accident, since the pocket follows the door in time.

Seventh, the enemy. You have made the Jutland axis a Polish Front with a Soviet minder in the corner, which is what the captured planning we later saw actually described, and which no novel ever gets right — they always send Russians. Zawadzki's refusal of the folder, considered as staff work for eleven minutes, page by page, is the finest scene in the book, and Rylski's honest columns becoming Kuragin's exhibit is the truest thing in it about that system.

Last, what moved me, and why. The Reinfeld message — "which of you we are dying for, respectfully, sirs, decide" — is the Territorial Army's whole tragedy in one line; that friction between the corps and the Heimatschutz was real, we all knew it, and nobody ever put it in writing because writing it was unbearable. The countermarch exchange, with rest logged SCHEDULED and NOT YET ACHIEVED, made an old man put the manuscript down for a while. And the furniture under tarpaulins with a Feldjäger posted on it: yes. That is what fighting on the soldiers' home ground would have been, and books never know it.

Publish this, after the repairs. It is the first novel of that November that understands the war we actually prepared for was a war of clerks, and that this was not a small thing but the whole thing. My notes are yours to use; my name you may leave out of it. And if you are ever near Kiel, the coffee is kept, as Rahn would say, for occasions.

With respect and, if I may, with comradeship,

Joachim Tarnow
Oberst a.D.
